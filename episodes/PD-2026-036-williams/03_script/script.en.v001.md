## ACT I — ARRESTED IN YOUR OWN DRIVEWAY (≈0:26–3:10)

This is not a nightmare, and it is not a hypothetical. It happened to a father in a quiet Detroit suburb in the winter of twenty twenty. He had done nothing wrong. ## ACT II — THE COMPUTER PICKED HIM (≈3:10–6:25)
[VO:] Go back to where it started. In twenty eighteen, someone walked into a downtown watch shop and stole a few thousand dollars of merchandise. The only witness was a camera bolted to the ceiling. It caught one grainy, low-light frame of a man in a dark cap — the kind of smeared, pixelated image you could squint at all day and fail to recognize your own brother. There was no name attached to that face. So the police did what more and more departments now do by reflex. They handed the blur to software, and they asked it to go find a person. [CLM-0003][CLM-0004]
(VIS: a stylized low-res surveillance reconstruction pixel-zooms; a dark-cap figure, illegible by design; the frame is fed into an abstract face-scan interface. Never presented as the real evidence frame.) (SFX: a camera-hiss; a digital ingest chirp as the frame loads into the scanner.)

[VO:] Here is how that actually works. Face-matching software takes one image and compares it against a vast state database — millions of driver's-license photos and mug shots, ordinary people who have never been accused of anything in their lives. The software does not know who anyone is. It produces a ranked list of faces that look statistically similar, each one scored by a probability. Feed it a sharp photo and it does reasonably well. Feed it a blurry one, and it will still always hand back somebody. In this case it reached into old license records and surfaced a photo from years before — a man who had simply renewed his license one afternoon. That was the whole link. A guess, ranked by a number, pulled out of a database he never knew he was standing in. [CLM-0004]

[VO:] And a face on a screen is not supposed to be enough. Everyone in that room knew it. A match is a lead — a name to look into, not a name to arrest. But instead of treating it that way, the detectives took the exact photo the computer had picked and dropped it into a physical lineup. Then they showed that lineup to a store contractor. Not someone who had witnessed the theft. Someone who had only ever watched the same blurry video the computer watched. He pointed at the picture. And that circular little nod — a machine's guess, confirmed by a person staring at the machine's own source — was written down as probable cause to arrest a living human being. [CLM-0004][CLM-0007]

The uncomfortable answer is that this technology does not miss at random. It misses in a pattern. And that pattern has been measured, in a government lab, in black and white. [CLM-0002]

## ACT III — WHY IT REACHES FOR THE WRONG FACE (≈6:25–9:20)
[VO:] In twenty nineteen, the federal government's own standards laboratory ran the largest test of its kind on these algorithms. The finding was blunt. A great many of them returned false matches far more often on Black and Asian faces than on white ones. Not by a hair. In some systems, ten to a hundred times more often. Sit with what a false match even is. It is the machine looking straight at you and confidently naming a stranger — or looking at a stranger and confidently naming you. And in test after test, if your face was Black or Asian, the very tool the police were leaning on was far more likely to write your name onto a crime that was never yours. [CLM-0005]
(VIS: a neutral bias bar chart builds — code-built motion-graphics; two bars, a wide gap; the multiplier reads as clean type. No baked-in numbers in any still.) (SFX: data bars fill with soft ticks; a decisive low boom lands on the gap.)

[VO:] And the way police use these tools makes it worse, not better. Searching one blurry face against a database of millions is the exact situation where false matches pile up highest — and the study found the very worst error rates there landed on Black women. An earlier study of commercial face software had already sketched the same shape. It misread the gender of darker-skinned women up to a third of the time, while almost never getting it wrong for a lighter-skinned man. Now, this is not a claim that a machine holds a grudge. The truth is stranger and more fixable than that. These systems learn from the faces they are shown, and for years they were shown too few of some of us. Train a machine on a narrow slice of the world, and a narrow slice is all it can see. [CLM-0005][CLM-0006][CLM-0013]
(VIS: a "trained on these faces → fails on those faces" motion-graphic; a lopsided dataset visualized as anonymous silhouettes, most one tone, few others; deliberately neutral, dignified.) (SFX: a soft imbalance motif; a low sustained bed, no melodrama.)

[VO:] Now bring it all the way back to you. Your face is almost certainly already sitting in one of these databases. If you hold a driver's license, you are in the lineup — a permanent one you never volunteered for and can never step out of. You do not have to break a law, know a criminal, or go anywhere near a crime scene. You only have to look, to a piece of software, a little like somebody else. The man walked off his own lawn in handcuffs was not careless, and he was not unlucky in the way we usually mean it. He was simply matched. [CLM-0001][CLM-0013]

## ACT IV — ONE MAN GOT A RULE. YOU GOT A WARNING. (≈9:20–11:40)
[VO:] He did not let it go. In twenty twenty-one, civil-rights lawyers sued the city on his behalf, and three years later the case settled. By news accounts, he was paid somewhere around three hundred thousand dollars — but the money is not the part that matters here. As part of that deal, the city was forced to rewrite its rules. Police there can no longer arrest anyone on a face-match alone. The match is treated as a lead and nothing more. Every earlier case where the software was used to make an arrest has to be pulled and reviewed, going back years. And a court will keep watch over the department to make sure the promise holds. It is, for now, one of the strictest limits any American city has put on this technology. [CLM-0008][CLM-0010][CLM-0009]

Before his story ever broke, at least two other men — one in the same city, one two states away — had already been arrested this exact way, their cases only surfacing later. By the count of the group that represented him, there are now more than a dozen known wrongful arrests like his in this country, and nearly every person named in them has been Black. Each one an ordinary person. Each one matched by a machine. And each one believed over their own flat denial. [CLM-0012]
