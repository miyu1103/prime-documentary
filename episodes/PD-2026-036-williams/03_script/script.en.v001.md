## ACT II — THE COMPUTER PICKED HIM (≈3:10–6:25)
[VO:] Go back to where it started. In twenty eighteen, someone walked into a downtown watch shop and stole a few thousand dollars of merchandise. The only witness was a camera bolted to the ceiling. It caught one grainy, low-light frame of a man in a dark cap — the kind of smeared, pixelated image you could stare at all day and still not recognize your own brother in it. There was no name attached to that face. So the police did what more and more departments now do by reflex. They handed the blur to software, and they asked it to go find a person. [CLM-0003][CLM-0004]
(VIS: a stylized low-res surveillance reconstruction pixel-zooms; a dark-cap figure, illegible by design; the frame is fed into an abstract face-scan interface. Never presented as the real evidence frame.) (SFX: a camera-hiss; a digital ingest chirp as the frame loads into the scanner.)

[VO:] So look at what the software actually does. It takes one image and compares it against a vast state database — millions of driver's-license photos and mug shots, ordinary people who have never been accused of anything in their lives. The software does not know who anyone is. It produces a ranked list of faces that look statistically similar, each one scored by a probability. Feed it a sharp photo and it does reasonably well. Feed it a blurry one, and it will still hand back somebody. In this case it reached into old license records and surfaced a photo from years before — a man who had once renewed his license one afternoon. That was the whole link. A guess, ranked by a number, pulled out of a database he never knew he was in. [CLM-0004]

## ACT III — WHY IT REACHES FOR THE WRONG FACE (≈6:25–9:20)
[VO:] In twenty nineteen, the federal government's own standards laboratory ran the largest test of its kind on these algorithms. The finding was blunt. A great many of them returned false matches far more often on Black and Asian faces than on white ones. Not by a hair. In head-to-head tests, ten to a hundred times more often. Consider what a false match actually is. It is the machine looking straight at you and confidently naming a stranger — or looking at a stranger and confidently naming you. And in test after test, if your face was Black or Asian, the very tool the police were leaning on was far more likely to write your name onto a crime that was never yours. [CLM-0005]
(VIS: a neutral bias bar chart builds — code-built motion-graphics; two bars, a wide gap; the multiplier reads as clean type. No baked-in numbers in any still.) (SFX: data bars fill with soft ticks; a decisive low boom lands on the gap.)

[VO:] And the way police use these tools makes it worse, not better. Searching one blurry face against a database of millions is exactly where false matches pile up highest, and the worst error rates of all landed on the faces of Black women. An earlier study had looked at a related but different job — software guessing whether a face is a man or a woman — and found the same shape: it misread darker-skinned women up to a third of the time, while almost never erring on a lighter-skinned man. That is not the same task as the matching that sent police to his door, but the gap runs the same way. And this is not a machine holding a grudge. When that same federal lab looked closer, the gap all but vanished on systems built in Asia — strong evidence the fault lies in the faces a system is trained on, not in the technology itself. These tools learn from what they are shown, and for years, faces like his barely made the set. [CLM-0005][CLM-0006][CLM-0013]
(VIS: a "trained on these faces → fails on those faces" motion-graphic; a lopsided dataset visualized as anonymous silhouettes, most one tone, few others; deliberately neutral, dignified.) (SFX: a soft imbalance motif; a low sustained bed, no melodrama.)

[VO:] Now bring it all the way back to you. Your face is almost certainly already sitting in one of these databases. You do not have to break a law, know a criminal, or go anywhere near a crime scene. To a piece of software, you only have to look a little like somebody else. The man who was marched off his own lawn in handcuffs was not careless, and he was not unlucky in the way we usually mean it. He was simply matched. [CLM-0001][CLM-0013]
