On 5 July 2013 a train stopped at Nantes, Quebec. It was carrying seventy-two loaded tank cars of petroleum crude oil. It was parked on a descending grade, on the main track, just over seven miles above the town of Lac-Mégantic. At fifty-eight minutes past midnight and twenty-one seconds, the train began to move. Forty-seven people were killed, and much of the centre of the town was destroyed.

The cause is on the record. The Transportation Safety Board investigated for just over a year, and in August of twenty fourteen it published what it had found. It identified eighteen distinct causes and contributing factors. Hand brakes were applied to seven. Seven, out of a train of seventy-eight vehicles. And all seven were on the locomotives and the buffer car. Not one hand brake was applied to any of the seventy-two tank cars. A fire on the lead locomotive was put out by shutting the engine down. With every locomotive shut down, the air compressor stopped supplying the air brake system. The train entered a curve built for eighteen to twenty-two miles an hour at sixty-five.

This film is about the test. The railway's own instructions set a minimum by a formula: ten per cent of the cars, plus two. For this train that is nine. Seven were applied. The investigation concluded that the real number was a minimum of seventeen, and possibly as many as twenty-six. But none of that was left to judgement, because there was a test, and the standard it set is one sentence long: it must be apparent, when the slack runs in or out, that the hand brakes are sufficient to prevent that cut of cars from moving. The test was performed with the independent brakes still active. So the thing holding the train during the test was not the thing being tested. It was the check itself that could not fail.

What the record also holds: eight months earlier, in October of twenty twelve, that locomotive's engine was repaired with an epoxy-like material which, in the investigation's own words, lacked the strength and the durability required for this use. The railway wrote a safety management system in two thousand and two, did not begin implementing it until two thousand and ten, and by twenty thirteen it was still not functioning effectively. Transport Canada does not validate a railway company's special instructions, and it gives no specific guidance on how many hand brakes must be applied to a parked freight train. All sixty-three derailed tank cars were in compliance with the specification requirement that was in effect at the time of their approval and construction. Fifty-nine of the sixty-three were breached. And no decision was documented, after the fire was extinguished, to move the train or to apply additional hand brakes.

It has been argued ever since that a second crew member would have changed what happened. The investigation was not able to conclude that, and this film does not conclude it either.

ON THE LEGAL RECORD, AS OF AUGUST 2026
A criminal trial opened in Sherbrooke in October of twenty seventeen, before a bilingual jury. Three men were charged with criminal negligence causing forty-seven deaths. Before the trial began, the judge ruled the Board's report inadmissible, and the jury did not consider any of its conclusions. In January of twenty eighteen, after nine days of deliberation, the jury acquitted all three. The month after the acquittal, the railway itself pleaded guilty to a federal offence and was fined a million dollars. Six former employees pleaded guilty to breaking the Railway Safety Act, specifically to failing to test the hand brakes after they had been applied; five of them were fined fifty thousand dollars each, and the engineer received a conditional sentence of six months, served in the community. A settlement fund for the victims was approved by a United States bankruptcy judge in October of twenty fifteen. A settlement fund is not a verdict. No individual was ever established as the cause of it.
