## ACT_1 — NINETY SECONDS OF ORDINARY

Everything that follows begins with a plumbing problem.

Not a nuclear problem. A plumbing problem — the kind of thing that happens in a building, on a Wednesday.

At thirty-six seconds past four in the morning on the twenty-eighth of March, 1979, a feedwater pump tripped at the Three Mile Island Nuclear Station, Unit 2, near Middletown, Pennsylvania. The plant was running at ninety-seven percent power. <!-- TM-401, TM-402 -->

Two seconds later, the turbine and the generator shut themselves down. That is what they are supposed to do.

Pressure in the reactor climbed a hundred pounds above normal, and a relief valve at the top of the pressuriser opened to let it out. That is also what it is supposed to do.

Eight seconds in, the reactor scrammed. Control rods dropped into the core and the chain reaction stopped. **As it was designed to do** — those are the Commission's words. <!-- TM-403 -->

So far, this is a machine looking after itself.

Now the thirteenth second.

The relief valve should have closed at thirteen seconds, when the pressure came back down to two thousand two hundred and five pounds. <!-- TM-404 -->

It did not. <!-- TM-404 -->

And here is the sentence that decides everything else. The light on the control panel that told the operators about that valve was not wired to the valve. It reported the signal that had been sent to it.

The signal said: close.

The valve stayed open for two hours and twenty-two minutes. In the first hundred minutes, some thirty-two thousand gallons — over a third of the entire capacity of the reactor coolant system — escaped. <!-- TM-405 -->

A reactor that has stopped fissioning is still hot. It is hot the way a stove ring is hot after you switch it off, except that it will stay hot for days, and the only thing standing between that heat and the fuel is water.

That is worth holding onto, because it is the part that surprises people. The dangerous phase of a nuclear accident is not the chain reaction. The chain reaction stopped in the eighth second and never came back. The danger is the leftover heat, and the danger is arithmetic: keep water on it, or do not.

The Commission put the whole morning into one conditional sentence, and it is worth hearing before the morning happens.

Had the valve closed as it was designed to, or had the operators realised it was stuck open and shut the backup valve, **or if they had simply left on the plant's high pressure injection pumps, the accident at Three Mile Island would have remained little more than a minor inconvenience.** <!-- TM-406 -->

Three separate ways out. Any one of them. In the first minutes.

The water was leaving. The panel said it was not.

At fourteen seconds, an operator checked that the emergency feedwater pumps had started. They had. He did not see two small lights showing that a valve on each emergency line was closed. <!-- TM-407 -->

The Commission wrote down why, and it is one of the most ordinary sentences in the whole report. **One light was covered by a yellow maintenance tag. No one knows why the second light was missed.** <!-- TM-407 -->

A minute and forty-five seconds in, with their water blocked, the steam generators boiled dry. <!-- TM-408 -->

At two minutes, the high-pressure injection pumps started by themselves, pushing about a thousand gallons a minute into the reactor. This is the system whose entire job is this exact moment. <!-- TM-409 -->

And then an operator shut one of them down, and turned the other down to less than a hundred gallons a minute. <!-- TM-409 -->

Not from carelessness. Because the pressuriser level was rising — and everything he had ever been taught said that a rising pressuriser means too much water. He was reading a real instrument, correctly, and drawing the trained conclusion.

The instrument was telling the truth about the pressuriser and lying about the reactor, because water was pouring out of the open valve and taking the reading with it.

At eight minutes, someone — **just who is a matter of dispute** — discovered that no emergency feedwater was reaching the steam generators, and opened the closed valves. <!-- TM-410 -->

Then the morning got long.

At a quarter past six, the water in the reactor was below the top of the core. <!-- TM-411 -->

At twenty-two minutes past six, an operator shut a backup valve and finally stopped the leak — two hours and twenty-two minutes after it started. <!-- TM-411 -->

By quarter to seven, as much as two-thirds of the twelve-foot core stood uncovered, and analyses done later put the core temperature as high as three and a half to four thousand degrees. <!-- TM-412 -->

Shortly before seven, the shift declared a site emergency. By twenty past seven, a monitor in the containment dome was reading eight rems an hour. <!-- TM-413 -->

The federal regulator's regional office found out at a quarter to eight — when it opened for the day. <!-- TM-413 -->

Not because anyone called it in the night. Because the working day began, and somebody walked into an office, and there was a message waiting.

The reactor had been uncovering for an hour and a half.

And there is a detail underneath the pump that almost nobody tells you, because it is too small to be dramatic.

The thing that tripped the feedwater pumps in the first place was a maintenance job on the condensate polishers — the units that clean the water. A faulty valve let water leak into the compressed air that operates the polishers' valves.

The Commission followed that back, and wrote this. **The same problem of water leaking into the polishers' valve control system had occurred at least twice before at TMI-2.** And then: **Had Met Ed corrected the earlier polisher problem, the March 28 sequence of events may never have begun.** <!-- TM-414 -->

At least twice before. In the same plant. On the same system.

This is the first appearance of a shape that is going to repeat for the rest of the hour: something known, written down, not carried through.

So how does a plant get to a morning like that, from a pump?

## ACT_2 — THE ROOM WHERE IT WAS SUPPOSED TO BE SEEN

Before the failures, the design. Somebody built that room on purpose, and the purpose was good.

A nuclear control room is an argument made in furniture. Every gauge is there because someone decided an operator would need it. The wall of small backlit tiles — the annunciator panel — exists so that a fault announces itself instead of waiting to be found.

That is a sound idea. Here is what it did that morning.

**During the first few minutes of the accident, more than one hundred alarms went off, and there was no system for suppressing the unimportant signals so that operators could concentrate on the significant alarms.** <!-- TM-501 -->

One hundred. In minutes. With nothing to sort them.

And the Commission went further, into something worse than noise. **Information was not presented in a clear and sufficiently understandable form. Although the pressure and temperature within the reactor coolant system were shown, there was no direct indication that the combination of pressure and temperature meant that the cooling water was turning into steam.** <!-- TM-502 -->

Read that twice. Both numbers were on the wall. The meaning of the two of them together was not.

The formal finding is blunt: **the control room was not adequately designed with the management of an accident in mind.** The architect-engineer **had never systematically evaluated control room design in the context of a serious accident** to see how it would serve. <!-- TM-503 -->

And the alarm problem was not a surprise. **The danger of having too many alarms was recognised by the designers during the design stage, but the problem was never resolved.** <!-- TM-503 -->

There is more, and each item is small, and together they are the morning.

**Some key indicators relevant to the accident were on the back of the control panel.** <!-- TM-504 -->

Several instruments **went off-scale during the course of the accident, depriving the operators of highly significant diagnostic information.** They were not designed to follow the course of an accident. <!-- TM-504 -->

The computer printer registering the alarms fell hours behind the events, and at one point jammed, losing information. <!-- TM-505 -->

And a year before all of this, after a smaller incident, an operator complained to his superiors about the control room. **No corrective action was taken by the utility.** <!-- TM-506 -->

One of the men who was in that room said it to the Commission as plainly as it can be said. **I would have liked to have thrown away the alarm panel. It wasn't giving us any useful information.** <!-- TM-507 -->

Now, the temptation.

The temptation is to say: they should have known. Any decent operator would have. And that version has been printed for forty years.

The Commission looked at the four men on that shift and wrote something else. **Each was a product of his training — training that did not adequately prepare them to cope with the accident at TMI-2.** <!-- TM-415 -->

And when the Commission summed up the whole event, it made a judgement that gets quoted rarely, because it is not satisfying. **The equipment was sufficiently good that, except for human failures, the major accident at Three Mile Island would have been a minor incident.** <!-- TM-603 -->

Which sounds like blaming the men — until you read the next part, and find that by human failures it did not mean the four of them.

And this had been said, in writing, before it happened.

More than a year before the accident, an engineer at the company that built this kind of reactor wrote an internal note about a smaller event at a different plant, in September of 1977. If that event had happened at full power, he wrote, **it is quite possible, perhaps probable, that core uncovery and possible fuel damage would have occurred.** <!-- TM-608 -->

Core uncovery. The exact words. Seventeen months early.

The Commission found that warnings of that kind **had been disregarded** — by the manufacturer, and by the regulator. <!-- TM-608 -->

So if the accident was not the operators, and not the equipment, where was it?

## ACT_3 — THE TEST THAT ANSWERED THE WAY IT WAS ASKED

Go back six months, to the autumn before.

A reactor leaks. All of them leak a little; that is not a scandal, it is plumbing. What matters is how much, and whether you know where it is going.

So the licence carries a number. The technical specifications for Three Mile Island Unit 2 limited leakage **to one gallon per minute from unidentified sources**. <!-- TM-103 -->

Unidentified is the operative word. Water you can point at is a maintenance job. Water you cannot account for is a question.

And if you exceed one gallon a minute, an Action Statement requires you to bring it back inside the limit **within four hours** — or shut the reactor down. <!-- TM-103 -->

That last clause is the one that costs money. A shutdown is not a rebuke. It is a bill, arriving daily, for as long as it lasts.

And it is worth being precise about what a technical specification is, because the phrase sounds like paperwork and is not. To get a licence at all, the company had to write down the rules by which it agreed to run the plant — and **violation of those standards could result in the shutdown of the facility.** <!-- TM-101 -->

They are not guidelines from a regulator. They are the promises the company made about itself, in exchange for being allowed to operate at all.

The reactor coolant those specifications protect does two things, and the Commission wrote them in the order that matters: **keep the facility at a safe temperature, and transfer heat generated by the nuclear reaction.** <!-- TM-102 -->

Everything in this film happens inside that first phrase.

Now: how do you know whether you are inside the limit?

You run a water inventory balance. You measure what is in the system, you account for what you added and what you took out, and the difference is your leak. A leak rate test.

The licence and the regulations both required something specific of that test. They required the company to **establish, implement and maintain an accurate and meaningful reactor coolant system water inventory balance procedure to demonstrate that unidentified leakage was within allowable limits.** <!-- TM-201, TM-202 -->

Accurate and meaningful. Those two words are the whole of this act.

On the seventh of November, 1983 — four and a half years after the accident — a federal grand jury in the Middle District of Pennsylvania returned an indictment against the company that ran the plant. <!-- TM-206 -->

There were eleven counts. Here is the one that matters, and it is the one the company would eventually plead guilty to. <!-- TM-301 -->

The charge, in the grand jury's own words.

Beginning sometime before the eighteenth of October, 1978, and continuing up to and including the twenty-eighth of March, 1979, the company **willfully** possessed, used and operated the plant in violation of the regulations. That is: the company **was on notice that its procedure for the reactor coolant system water inventory balance did not accurately and meaningfully measure the amount of unidentified reactor coolant leakage.** <!-- TM-203 -->

And then the sentence this programme exists to put in front of you.

**Despite such notice, the defendant company continued to use such inaccurate and meaningless procedure in an effort to generate results which appeared to establish that reactor coolant leakage was within allowable limits.** <!-- TM-203 -->

In an effort to generate results which appeared to establish.

That is not a clerical failure. That is a test being run to produce an answer.

The regulation behind that count is Part 50, Appendix B of the federal nuclear rules — the **Quality Assurance Criteria for Nuclear Power Plants**. Not a filing requirement. The rules about whether the things you check are actually checked. <!-- TM-204 -->

The prosecutor told the court why he insisted on that specific count and no other. **There had to be a guilty plea to a significant count. Count 2 is such a count. If one looks at the language contained in it, it goes to the very heart of the leak rate test, the primary method of measuring leakage from unidentified sources.** <!-- TM-314 -->

And now the thing this programme will not do.

Look at the dates in that charge again. It runs up to and including the twenty-eighth of March, 1979. It ends on the morning of the accident. It does not reach into it. <!-- TM-203 -->

No document in this story says the faked tests caused the meltdown, and this film is not going to be the first to say it.

What is true is smaller and stranger and harder to put down.

For months before the morning when a valve stuck open and the water started to leave, the instrument whose job was to notice water leaving had already been, in the government's own words, inaccurate and meaningless — and the company had been told so, and had kept using it. <!-- TM-203 -->

And there is one more thing about that indictment, which is a matter of timing rather than law.

It was signed on the seventh of November, 1983. <!-- TM-206 -->

Four years and seven months after the accident. A member of Congress said out loud, nine days later, what everybody in Harrisburg already knew — that the Department had returned these indictments **on the eve of the expiration of the statute of limitations.** <!-- TM-206 -->

Not early. Not in the middle. At the edge of the last day it was legally possible.

So what did the law finally do about it?

## ACT_4 — WHAT IT COST

On the twenty-ninth of February, 1984, in Courtroom Number Two of the federal building in Harrisburg, the company changed its plea. <!-- TM-313 -->

It pleaded **guilty** to one count — Count 2, the leak rate count, under the Atomic Energy Act and the quality assurance rules. <!-- TM-301 -->

It pleaded **no contest** to six others. <!-- TM-302 -->

And four counts were dismissed. <!-- TM-303 -->

Among the dismissed was Count 11, which had charged that the company **knowingly and willfully falsified, concealed, and covered up by trick, scheme, and device, from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, a material fact** — that the procedure did not accurately measure the leakage. <!-- TM-205 -->

That was the only count in the indictment brought under the ordinary federal criminal code, the false-statements law that applies to lying to any agency of the United States. Defence counsel said so on the record: the government was not pursuing **the only one that charges a violation of the Criminal Code of Title 18.** <!-- TM-305 -->

So the company was never convicted of lying to the regulator. It was convicted of running a meaningless test.

Then the judge read the sentence.

**And now, this twenty-ninth day of February, 1984, it is adjudged on Count 2 that the Defendant shall pay a fine to the United States in the sum of five thousand dollars.** Five thousand on each of four more counts. Ten thousand on each of two. <!-- TM-304 -->

**The total fine to be paid on all counts is forty-five thousand dollars, and shall be paid within thirty days.** <!-- TM-304 -->

Forty-five thousand dollars.

For scale, and this is the Commission's own figure, the direct financial cost of the accident was estimated at **one to two billion dollars.** <!-- TM-714 -->

The fine was three-thousandths of one percent of the low end of that.

But there is a second number, and it is the interesting one, and it is almost always reported wrongly.

The plea agreement carried a condition. **As a material condition of this agreement**, the company had to place **one million dollars** in an interest-bearing escrow account, and that money would pass **absolutely and without condition** to the **Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency**, to assist in emergency preparedness and planning **within twenty miles of the station.** <!-- TM-311 -->

That million was not a fine. It was not cleanup money. It was a payment for the ability to warn people.

And the judge explained why she had taken it. The maximum penalty available after a trial would have been eighty-five thousand dollars — **none of which would go to the direct benefit of the citizens negatively impacted by the Defendant's conduct.** One million of these dollars, she said, **will benefit the public.** <!-- TM-312 -->

Which brings this story to the part that happened five years earlier, and that almost nobody connects to it.

On the Friday of the accident, at about half past twelve, the Governor of Pennsylvania advised **pregnant women and preschool aged children** to leave the area within **five miles** of the plant. That advisory was lifted on the ninth of April. <!-- TM-709 -->

Not a general evacuation. An advisory, to two groups of people, in a five-mile ring.

And it was issued into a disagreement. That same morning, the state's own radiation bureau told the Governor that **no protective action of any kind was required**, while the chairman of the federal commission was recommending that exactly those people be told to go. <!-- TM-710 -->

Some of the alarm was later found to be based on error. **A mistaken interpretation of a burst of radiation** led some federal officials to recommend immediate evacuation on the Friday; over the weekend, others believed there was an imminent danger of a hydrogen explosion inside the reactor vessel. <!-- TM-711 -->

And there is a detail from those days that says more about American preparedness in 1979 than any of the arguing did. The standard protective measure for a population under a plume of radioactive iodine is a tablet of potassium iodide, which saturates the thyroid so it cannot take up the radioactive kind. It is cheap. It is not a new idea.

At the time of the accident, the Commission recorded, potassium iodide for this use **was not commercially available in the United States.** <!-- TM-713 -->

Not rationed. Not delayed. Not available.

Three workers inside the plant received doses of three to four rems in the months that followed, above the regulator's quarterly limit of three. <!-- TM-706 -->

And the measuring itself had holes in it. **There were deficiencies in instrumentation for measuring the radioactivity released, particularly during the early stages of the accident** — which is to say that on the morning when the public most needed a number, the instruments that make the number were among the things not working properly. <!-- TM-705 -->

And underneath all of it, the Commission recorded a small administrative fact that reads, in hindsight, like the point of the entire film.

**Starting Saturday, the state emergency management director was no longer included in these meetings — with the result that the state agency, and the county and local civil defence organisations, had to rely primarily on the news media for information about conditions at the site.** <!-- TM-712 -->

The people whose job was to tell the public what to do were watching television.

Five years later, a court ordered a million dollars paid to that same agency, for emergency planning, in the twenty-mile ring. <!-- TM-311, TM-712 -->

So what was actually broken here?

The Commission answered that, and its answer is not the one the public took away.

**As the evidence accumulated, it became clear that the fundamental problems are people-related problems and not equipment problems.** <!-- TM-603 -->

And by people, it did not mean the men on shift.

**In the testimony we received, one word occurred over and over again. That word is 'mindset.'** One federal official used it five times in ten minutes. **I think the mindset was that the operator was a force for good — that if you discounted him, it was a measure of conservatism.** <!-- TM-604 -->

Discounted him. Written him out of the calculation, and called that caution.

**The most serious mindset is the preoccupation of everyone with the safety of equipment, resulting in the down-playing of the importance of the human element in nuclear power generation.** What the regulator and the industry failed to recognise is that **the human beings who manage and operate the plants constitute an important safety system.** <!-- TM-605 -->

The people were the safety system. Nobody had put them on the diagram.

And about the regulator, the Commission found a specific disease and gave it a name. **There is a lack of closure in the system — important safety issues are frequently raised and may be studied to some degree of depth, but are not carried through to resolution; and the lessons learned do not reach those individuals and agencies that most need to know about them.** <!-- TM-606 -->

Raised. Studied. Not carried through.

More than a year before the accident, an engineer at the company that built the reactor wrote, internally, that if an earlier incident at another plant had happened at full power, **it is quite possible, perhaps probable, that core uncovery and possible fuel damage would have occurred.** The Commission found that warnings of this kind **had been disregarded.** <!-- TM-608 -->

Nobody died at Three Mile Island.

The Commission estimated the collective dose to the two million people within fifty miles at about **two thousand person-rems**, against an annual background dose, for the same population, of about **two hundred and forty thousand** — somewhat less than one percent of what they receive from the world anyway in a year. <!-- TM-701 -->

The maximum dose to any one member of the public was **seventy millirems**. On the basis of present scientific knowledge, the Commission wrote, the doses were **so small that there will be no detectable additional cases of cancer, developmental abnormalities, or genetic ill-health.** <!-- TM-703 -->

And it attached a caveat that has to travel with that sentence wherever it goes. **The health effects of radiation dose levels of a few rems or less are not known.** The estimates are extrapolations from higher doses. <!-- TM-704 -->

What the Commission did find was this. **The major health effect of the accident appears to have been on the mental health of the people living in the region** — highest among adults within five miles, and among those with preschool children. <!-- TM-707 -->

The harm that landed was the harm of not knowing.

And the industry did change. The Commission had recommended it in a sentence that sounds mild and is not: merely meeting the requirements of a government regulation does not guarantee safety, and therefore **the industry must also set and police its own standards of excellence.** <!-- TM-801 -->

That year, the utilities created their own body to do it. The federal auditors later described it exactly: in response to the accident, the industry **established INPO to assist utilities in improving the safety of plant operations** — created **not to supplant the regulatory role** of the government, but so the industry could make its own operations safer. <!-- TM-802 -->

It works. By most accounts it works well.

And there is one line in the auditors' report about it that this programme is going to leave with you, without comment. The government's inspection notices are available to the public. **INPO's reports are not.** <!-- TM-803 -->

The regulator changed too. In May of 1980 it published an action plan built from the accident, and among the changes was a simple one: from then on, **at least two inspectors would live near each plant and work exclusively at it.** <!-- TM-804, TM-805 -->

People, standing in the building, all the time. That was the fix.

And the thing that did not recover was public confidence. **The accident raised concerns all over the world and led to a lowering of public confidence in the nuclear industry and in the regulator.** <!-- TM-806 -->

But the Commission had already written the sentence that outlasts all of it, and it did not put it at the end of a chapter about valves. It put it in the overview, as a conclusion about the whole enterprise.

**Whether or not operator error explains this particular case — given all the above deficiencies, we are convinced that an accident like Three Mile Island was eventually inevitable.** <!-- TM-607 -->

Eventually. Inevitable. Not this plant, not that shift — the arrangement.

So what is the rule that was running that morning?

## ENDING

Go back to the light.

It is a small rectangle on a wall of small rectangles, and behind it there is a bulb, and the bulb is wired to something.

That morning, one of them was wired to the order sent to a valve, and not to the valve. One of them was behind a yellow tag. A hundred of them came on at once, with nothing to say which mattered. Some of the ones that mattered were on the other side of the panel, where a person standing in the room could not see them. <!-- TM-407, TM-501, TM-504 -->

And in a filing cabinet somewhere in that same building, there were months of leak rate results, all of them inside the limit, produced by a procedure the company had been told did not measure anything. <!-- TM-203 -->
