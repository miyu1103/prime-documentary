## HOOK — 0:00–0:20
[VO:] It is a winter night in 1992, a little after three in the morning. A federal agent is parked on a public street, looking at an ordinary house across the road. He never knocks on the door. He never steps onto the lawn. He just raises a device to his eyes, points it at the home, and reads the heat coming off the walls. From that heat, he decides he knows what is happening inside [CLM-0005] [CLM-0006]. No warrant. No foot on the property. Just a machine, a wall, and a guess about a stranger's private life. Nine years later, the Supreme Court had to answer one question about that quiet moment: was it a search?

## OPENING — 0:20–1:15
[VO:] We treat the home as the one place the government cannot reach without a warrant. Your walls are supposed to be the edge of your private world. But technology has a way of slipping around old rules. A thermal camera does not break down your door or pick your lock. It just measures the warmth your house gives off, and turns that warmth into a picture of what you might be doing behind the walls [CLM-0008]. Over the next twelve minutes: the freezing night that started it, the hidden operation the heat gave away, and the line the Court finally drew at the front door of every home in America [CLM-0003].

## ACT I — THE SCAN — 1:15–3:30
[VO:] The house sat on Rhododendron Drive in Florence, Oregon — part of a small triplex, three homes joined under one roof [CLM-0005]. A man named Danny Kyllo lived in one of them. Federal agents had come to suspect that he was growing marijuana inside, under lights [CLM-0006].

[VO:] Growing marijuana indoors takes light — a great deal of it. Indoor growers run high-intensity lamps for hours at a time, and those lamps throw off serious heat [CLM-0006]. And heat is hard to hide. It seeps through walls and roofs and leaks out into the cold night air, whether you want it to or not [CLM-0008].

[VO:] So on January 16, 1992, a little after three in the morning, an agent named William Elliott sat in a car across the street with a thermal imaging device — an Agema Thermovision 210 [CLM-0005]. A thermal imager does not see through walls. It sees heat. Almost every object gives off infrared radiation that human eyes cannot detect, and the device turns that invisible warmth into a picture — black for cool, white for hot, gray for everything in between [CLM-0008].

[VO:] The scan took only a few minutes, and the picture told a story. The roof over the garage and one side wall of Kyllo's home glowed far hotter than the rest of the house, and hotter than the neighboring units under the same roof [CLM-0006]. To Elliott, that pattern meant one thing: powerful grow lamps, running inside [CLM-0006].

[VO:] He never went in. He never crossed the yard. He read the outside of the house, in the dark, and drew a firm conclusion about the inside.

## ACT II — THE PROBLEM — 3:30–6:00
[VO:] That conclusion did real work. Along with a tip from an informant and unusually high electric bills, the thermal scan helped Elliott convince a judge to sign a search warrant [CLM-0007]. When agents finally went inside, they found what they expected: an indoor growing operation with more than a hundred marijuana plants [CLM-0007].

[VO:] Kyllo was charged with manufacturing marijuana. He asked the court to throw the evidence out, arguing that the thermal scan itself was an illegal search of his home. When that failed, he pleaded guilty — but kept the right to appeal the one question that mattered [CLM-0007].

[VO:] And it was a genuinely hard question. Think about what the agent actually did. He stood on a public street, where anyone is allowed to be. He aimed a device at the outside of a house. He measured heat that was already leaking out into the open air. He never touched the home, never entered it, never even set foot on the property [CLM-0009].

[VO:] By the old rules, that looked like no search at all. For most of its history, the Fourth Amendment was about physical trespass — an officer breaking in, going through your drawers, carrying your things away. Elliott did none of that. He simply gathered heat that the house was giving off on its own.

[VO:] But push a little harder, and it starts to feel very different. The entire point of the scan was to learn what was happening inside a private home — something the agent could not see with his own eyes from the street. He used a machine to pull private facts out of the one place the Constitution guards most closely. If the government can do that freely, then the wall around your home is only as private as the next new sensor allows [CLM-0001].

[VO:] So which is it? A harmless reading of heat in the open air — or a search of your home by another name?

## ACT III — THE RULING — 6:00–9:00
[VO:] On June 11, 2001, the Supreme Court gave its answer, and it was close — five justices to four [CLM-0002]. The majority opinion came from Justice Antonin Scalia, and the lineup behind it did not follow the usual conservative-liberal script; justices who rarely agreed stood on the same side [CLM-0002]. This was not ordinary politics. It was a fight about the meaning of the home.

[VO:] Scalia began with first principles. "At the very core" of the Fourth Amendment, he wrote, "stands the right of a man to retreat into his own home and there be free from unreasonable governmental intrusion." [CLM-0004] The home is not just another place the police might search. It is the place. And the Fourth Amendment, the Court said, draws a firm line at its entrance — a line that must be "not only firm but also bright." [CLM-0003]

[VO:] Then came the rule that made the case famous. Where the government uses a device that is not in general public use to explore details of a home that it could not have learned without physically going inside, that is a search [CLM-0001]. And a search of a home, without a warrant, is presumptively unreasonable [CLM-0001].

[VO:] Notice how carefully that is built. It does not turn on whether the officer trespassed. It turns on the technology reaching past the wall to reveal the inside. Scalia's worry looked forward, not back. If the rule depended only on how good today's device happened to be, then privacy in the home would shrink a little every time a better machine came along. So the Court fixed the line at the wall itself: what the inside of your home holds is presumptively yours, and pulling it out with a special device is a search — whether that device reads heat, or something no one has invented yet [CLM-0001].

[VO:] Four justices disagreed, and their argument was not foolish. Justice John Paul Stevens wrote the dissent [CLM-0002]. The agent, he said, never went through the wall. He only measured heat that had already radiated off the outside surface of the house and drifted into the public air. That, Stevens argued, was "off-the-wall" observation, not "through-the-wall" surveillance — closer to smelling an odor that floats out of a kitchen than to peering inside a room [CLM-0009]. Heat that leaves your house, in his view, is already out in the world, and collecting it should not count as searching your home [CLM-0009].

[VO:] It is a real distinction. But the majority would not rest the privacy of the home on it. Whatever you call the heat, the purpose and the result were the same: the government learned what was going on inside a house without a warrant. And that, five justices held, is exactly what the Fourth Amendment is there to stop [CLM-0001].

## ACT IV — WHAT IT MEANS — 9:00–11:00
[VO:] It is worth being precise about what the Court did, and what it did not do. It did not ban thermal imaging. It did not erase the evidence against Kyllo for good. It sent the case back down with a narrower instruction: this kind of scan is a search, so it needs a warrant — and the lower court now had to decide whether the warrant still stood up once you set the thermal evidence aside [CLM-0010]. The lesson was not "you can never do this." It was "get a warrant first."

[VO:] That difference is the whole point. The Constitution rarely tells the police they can never learn something. Usually it just says: go to a judge, show your reasons, and get permission before you reach into a private space. Kyllo pulled the home firmly back inside that rule [CLM-0010].

[VO:] And the reason the case still matters lies in the phrase Scalia chose: technology "not in general public use." [CLM-0003] In 1992, the fight was over a heat camera the size of a lunchbox. But the logic reaches much further. Every year, the tools for sensing what happens inside a home get cheaper, smaller, and sharper — drones overhead, better cameras, devices that read signals through walls [CLM-0011]. Kyllo is the case courts keep returning to when they ask how far the government can push those tools before it needs a warrant [CLM-0011].

[VO:] The strength of the ruling, and its weakness, is that same phrase. "Not in general public use" is a moving line. A device that is exotic today can sit in everyone's pocket tomorrow — and the more ordinary a technology becomes, the weaker your claim that using it invades your privacy [CLM-0011]. The Court protected the home against the future, but it tied that protection to a standard the future keeps rewriting [CLM-0011].

## ENDING — 11:00–12:00
[VO:] So go back to that agent in the car, on the freezing street, a little after three in the morning. He never knocked. He never crossed the lawn. He simply read the heat coming off a stranger's walls and decided he knew what was inside [CLM-0005]. The Supreme Court looked at that quiet, no-contact search — a search with no break-in and no trespass at all — and said the home draws a bright line, and the government had stepped over it [CLM-0001] [CLM-0003].

[VO:] But notice what saved the home here: walls. Something solid for the heat to leak through, an edge the Court could point to and call the entrance. The Fourth Amendment grew up protecting exactly that — houses, walls, doors, the ground you stand on.

[VO:] So what happens when the thing the government wants to reach has no walls around it at all? When your private words travel as sound through the open air, or as a signal down a wire — into a glass phone booth on a public sidewalk, where anyone can see you, but no one is supposed to be listening? Long before the heat camera, the Court faced that exact question, about a man, a booth, and a hidden microphone. The answer moved the Fourth Amendment from protecting places to protecting people. That is next time.
