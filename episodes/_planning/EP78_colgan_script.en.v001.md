## HOOK
At seventeen minutes past ten on a February night, a turboprop on approach to Buffalo was five miles from the runway. <!-- CO-001, CO-002 -->

## ACT_1 — A RED BAR ON A GLASS SCREEN
Start with the instrument, because the instrument is honest.

On the pilot's display there is a vertical strip of numbers. That is the airspeed. Beside it, at the bottom, sits a red and black bar. <!-- CO-301 -->

The bar is not decoration. It marks the speed below which the wing stops flying. When the aeroplane slows, the bar climbs the scale toward the number. <!-- CO-301 -->

If the number reaches the top of the bar, two things happen at once. The stick shaker fires — a hard mechanical rattle in both control columns, aural and physical, impossible to ignore. And the airspeed numbers change from white to red. <!-- CO-301, CO-302 -->

The machine says it twice. Once through your hands. Once through your eyes.

It is the twelfth of February, 2009. The aeroplane is a Bombardier Q400, registration November two hundred Whiskey Quebec, flying as Continental Connection 3407 for a regional carrier called Colgan Air. <!-- CO-001 -->

There are forty-five passengers, two flight attendants and two pilots aboard. <!-- CO-003 -->

Both pilots are based at Newark. <!-- CO-004 -->

Neither of them lives there.

The first officer began her journey to work at ten to eight the previous evening, Pacific time, from her home near Seattle. She flew across the country overnight, changed at Memphis, and reached Newark at twenty-three minutes past six that morning. <!-- CO-609 -->

She had no crash pad. There was nowhere in New Jersey that was hers. <!-- CO-608 -->

The captain commuted too, from Florida. A colleague said he had wanted a crash pad near Newark and had been trying to avoid paying for one by bidding trips that started or ended somewhere he could get home from. He slept in hotels, or in the company crew room. <!-- CO-703 -->

Both of them were seen in that crew room on the twelfth, before a report time of half past one in the afternoon. <!-- CO-610 -->

The flight is short. Newark to Buffalo, in the dark, in February, in icing conditions.

Icing changes one thing that matters here. There is a switch on the overhead panel on the captain's side, marked REF SPEEDS. Set to increase, it moves the stall warning earlier — the shaker will now fire at a lower angle of attack, so an iced wing gets the same margin a clean wing has. <!-- CO-304 -->

It is a good design. It says: the wing is not what it was, so I will warn you sooner.

The crew talked, on the approach, about things that were not the approach. The Board would later record that as a failure to adhere to sterile cockpit procedures, and would list it as one of four contributing factors. <!-- CO-202 -->

They ran the ice protection check. The captain said the twenty-four hour check had been done. The first officer confirmed it, after noticing in the aeroplane's logbooks that a previous crew had not completed the daily one. <!-- CO-305 -->

Then they slowed for the approach.

And nobody watched the bar.

That is the Board's first contributing factor, in its own words: the flight crew's failure to monitor airspeed in relation to the rising position of the low-speed cue. <!-- CO-202 -->

The bar climbed. The number fell to meet it. The numbers went red.

The shaker fired.

⟨HELD⟩

At that moment the aeroplane was still flying. The wings were level. The load factor was one G. There was enough airspeed available to correct the situation. <!-- CO-204 -->

The captain pulled back on the control column. <!-- CO-203 -->

The wing stalled. The aeroplane rolled forty-five degrees left, then back through level, then further. After the first stick pusher activation the captain applied a forty-one pound pull force to the column, and the roll reached one hundred and five degrees right wing down. <!-- CO-203, CO-205 -->

At about seventeen minutes past ten it came down on a house in Clarence Center, five nautical miles north-east of the airport. <!-- CO-001, CO-002 -->

Everyone on board died. So did one person on the ground. Fifty people. <!-- CO-003 -->

## ACT_2 — THE INSTRUCTION HE DID NOT FOLLOW
Here is where the easy story arrives, and it is worth taking seriously, because it is half true.

The easy story is this. A first officer on fifteen thousand eight hundred dollars a year had flown all night across the continent to get to work. A captain with no fixed address near his base had slept in a crew room. Both were tired. The airline had made that normal. Therefore the airline killed them. <!-- CO-605, CO-703 -->

Hold that thought, because every fact in it is in the record. <!-- CO-605, CO-609, CO-703 -->

Now hold the Board's sentence beside it.

The pilots' performance was likely impaired because of fatigue. **But the extent of their impairment, and the degree to which it contributed to the performance deficiencies that occurred during the flight, cannot be conclusively determined.** <!-- CO-801 -->

Read that again. The Board is a body that exists to assign cause, and it declined. Not because it was protecting anyone. Because the evidence would not carry the weight.

A film that says fatigue did it is more satisfying than the Board and less true.

So what does the Board say instead.

Probable cause: **the captain's inappropriate response to the activation of the stick shaker, which led to an aerodynamic stall from which the airplane did not recover.** <!-- CO-201 -->

That is the finding. It is about four seconds and a pair of hands.

And to understand why it is so hard to look at, you have to understand what the machine was doing while it happened.

The stick shaker is a warning. It says: you are close.

The stick pusher is not a warning. The stick pusher is an instruction. <!-- CO-303 -->

When the aeroplane has actually stalled, the pusher drives the elevator two degrees nose down and pushes both control columns forward. It is a physical command to a human body: **push**. Lower the nose. Get the air moving over the wing again. <!-- CO-303 -->

And a crew can overpower it. The system was designed so they could. <!-- CO-303 -->

That is not a flaw. There are situations in aviation where a pilot must be able to override an automatic system, and the engineers who built it knew that.

But it means the last safeguard on a Q400 is a suggestion that a strong pull can defeat.

Forty-one pounds. <!-- CO-205 -->

The stick forces at the moment the autopilot came out were likely less than two pounds. The aeroplane was not fighting him. Nothing was dragging the column back. <!-- CO-206 -->

⟨HELD⟩

Which leaves the question this programme actually exists to ask. **Why would a man with an airline transport pilot certificate pull, when every hour of his training said push?**

## ACT_3 — THE RECORD THAT WAS THERE ALL ALONG
Before the record, the reason for the record.

Airlines cannot watch their pilots fly. A carrier employs people who spend their working lives out of sight, in weather, at night, making decisions nobody in the building will ever see. So the industry does the only thing it can. It tests them, over and over, and it writes down what happened.

Every check ride is a file. Every failure is a file. The paper is the substitute for the watching.

Now the record.

On the first of October, 1991, the captain was disapproved for his instrument rating on his initial flight check. He was flying a Piper PA-28. The tasks he did not pass included the instrument cockpit check, a partial-panel approach, an NDB approach, and holding. He passed it twenty-four days later. <!-- CO-401 -->

On the fourteenth of May, 2002, he was disapproved for his commercial single-engine certificate on his initial check, in a Cessna. The tasks were takeoffs, landings, go-arounds and performance manoeuvres. He had two hundred and ninety-six hours. He passed five weeks later. <!-- CO-402 -->

On the ninth of April, 2004, he was disapproved for his commercial multi-engine certificate. The entire flight portion. He passed twenty days later. <!-- CO-403 -->

And on the fifteenth of October, 2007 — by now a first officer at Colgan — he was disapproved for his airline transport pilot certificate. The task was approach and landing with an engine failure. He passed three days later. <!-- CO-404 -->

Twelve days before that, on the third of October, he had received an unsatisfactory grade on his upgrade check in the Saab 340. The same task. Engine failure on approach. <!-- CO-405 -->

Four disapprovals across sixteen years, and a fifth event in the same fortnight as the fourth.

Now stop, because this is where a careless film goes wrong.

Failing a check ride is not rare. In 2008, the overall failure rate for a first attempt at an airline transport pilot certificate was **thirteen point one per cent**. Roughly one applicant in eight. <!-- CO-407 -->

If failing were the signal, the industry would have no pilots.

So the record on its own proves nothing. What matters is not the failures. What matters is who could see them.

Colgan's employment application asked a question. One line. **"Have you ever failed any proficiency check, FAA check ride, IOE or line check?"** <!-- CO-501 -->

In 2005 he answered yes. And he described one. *I missed the NDB approach, received additional instruction, then repeated the approach and passed.* <!-- CO-502 -->

True. Complete? No.

At that point he was carrying two other certificate disapprovals. Neither appeared on the form. <!-- CO-503 -->

⟨HELD⟩

At the public hearing in May 2009, Colgan's vice-president of administration was asked about it, and said this: if the captain's incomplete answer had previously been known, **it would have resulted in his dismissal from the company.** <!-- CO-504 -->

Sit with the shape of that.

The company had a rule. The rule was real. The rule had a consequence, and the consequence was severe.

And the company had no way to find out.

The question was asked. The answer was accepted. The file that would have contradicted it existed, at the Federal Aviation Administration, the whole time.

Nobody was hiding it. Nobody had to. The systems simply did not talk.

And what about the other seat.

The first officer was hired in January 2008. Before that she had been a flight instructor, in Scottsdale and then in Phoenix. Her résumé listed the aeroplanes she had flown — Pipers, Cessnas, a Beech, a Diamond — and reported **no experience with turbine-powered aircraft at all**. <!-- CO-601, CO-602, CO-603 -->

That was not a secret either. It was on the application.

Her hours met the company's internal minimum: six hundred hours total, a hundred of them multi-engine. And the Board notes, carefully, that this minimum **was typical for new hires at regional airlines at that time.** <!-- CO-604 -->

She was not an outlier. She was the industry.

On the cockpit voice recorder that evening, before the sterile cockpit rule took effect, she said what she had earned the previous year. Fifteen thousand eight hundred dollars. And then: *"I'm just lucky 'cause I have a husband that's working."* <!-- CO-605 -->

The captain said his own figure. About sixty thousand. <!-- CO-606 -->

She said her husband had made more in one weekend of military drill than she made in a whole pay cycle. A recent raise would bring her an extra two hundred dollars a paycheck. <!-- CO-607 -->

Those are the numbers for the two people flying the aeroplane.

Here are the numbers for the company. In 2008 the average salary of a Colgan Q400 captain was sixty-seven thousand dollars, and of a first officer, twenty-four thousand. <!-- CO-701 -->

And one more line, which the Board records without comment.

**Colgan did not have locality pay for its pilots. Its management personnel received locality pay.** <!-- CO-702 -->

Locality pay is the adjustment that recognises it costs more to live near an expensive airport. The people who had to be near the airport did not get it. The people who worked in the building did.

Which is how you end up with a first officer flying all night to get to a job she cannot afford to live beside.

None of that is the probable cause. The Board did not make it the probable cause and neither will this film.

But it is the water everything else was swimming in.

## ACT_4 — WHAT FIFTY DEATHS BOUGHT
The Board issued its report in February 2010, a year after the crash.

The probable cause was the captain's response to the stick shaker. The four contributing factors were the crew's failure to monitor airspeed, the failure to keep a sterile cockpit, the captain's failure to manage the flight, and **Colgan Air's inadequate procedures for airspeed selection and management during approaches in icing conditions.** <!-- CO-201, CO-202 -->

Notice the fourth one. Three findings about two people in a cockpit, and one about the company that wrote the procedures they were following.

The report ran to two hundred and ninety-nine pages, and its safety issues were not mostly about that night. They were about monitoring failures, professionalism, fatigue, remedial training, **pilot training records**, airspeed procedures, stall training, and the FAA's oversight of all of it. <!-- CO-505 -->

And then something happened that almost never happens.

The families did not go home.

They came to Washington. They kept coming. They sat through hearings. And in August 2010, Congress passed the Airline Safety and Federal Aviation Administration Extension Act. <!-- CO-901 -->

It did three things that matter to this story. `[SECONDARY — confirm against the statute]`

It required every first officer at a commercial carrier to hold an airline transport pilot certificate — the rule everyone now calls the fifteen-hundred-hour rule. <!-- CO-902 -->

It required safety management systems at commercial air carriers. <!-- CO-903 -->

And it required the Federal Aviation Administration to build **a central database of pilot records, kept for the life of a pilot, which an airline must review before it hires.** <!-- CO-904 -->

That third one is the one that answers this file.

Not more training. Not a better warning horn. A database — so that the next time a company asks a man whether he has ever failed a check ride, the company does not have to take his word for it.

⟨HELD⟩

There is one thing the fifteen-hundred-hour rule does not do, and an honest film has to say it.

**It would not have changed this flight.** The captain already held an airline transport pilot certificate. The rule raises the bar for the right-hand seat. He was in the left. <!-- CO-404, CO-902 -->

So did fifty people die to produce a rule that would not have saved them?

No. They died and a different rule came out of it — the one about records — and that rule points exactly at what went wrong. The headline reform and the useful reform were not the same reform. That happens more often than anyone admits.

## ENDING
Go back to the instrument.

The red bar is at the bottom of the scale, where it belongs. The numbers are white. The aeroplane is flying. <!-- CO-301 -->

That is what it looks like when everything is correct. It looks like nothing.

The bar climbed. The numbers turned red. The columns shook. Then a machine pushed forward and a man pulled back, and the aeroplane rolled a hundred and five degrees. <!-- CO-301, CO-203, CO-205 -->

Every one of those was a signal, delivered on time, to the people who needed it.

And underneath them, quieter and older, was a different signal that had also been delivered on time. Four times, over sixteen years, to a filing system. <!-- CO-401, CO-402, CO-403, CO-404 -->

One line on a form asked for it. The answer came back partial. The company said afterwards that a complete answer would have ended his employment. <!-- CO-501, CO-502, CO-504 -->

Nobody checked, because in 2005 there was nothing to check against. <!-- CO-904 -->

Tonight, somewhere, a red bar is sitting at the bottom of a scale on an aeroplane you are not on.

And in a building you will never enter, a file about the person flying it is open on a screen, because a database exists now that did not exist then. <!-- CO-904 -->

Somebody looked.

That is the whole difference.
