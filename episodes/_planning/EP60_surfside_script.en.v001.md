## ACT I — THE BUILDING (1:10–8:00)

Champlain Towers South stood twelve floors above the sand in Surfside, on the Atlantic coast of Florida. A hundred and thirty-six apartments. A lobby, a lift, a corridor with identical doors. From the beach it looked like every other building on that stretch of coast, because it was.

Behind it, at ground level, there was a swimming pool.

That pool sat on a slab of concrete. Under the slab there was a car park.

This is worth holding on to, because almost everything that follows happens in the gap between those two facts. Above the slab: water, tiles, loungers, planters full of heavy tropical greenery. Below it: columns, painted bays, and the cars of the people who lived upstairs. The deck was a roof. Most people who walked on it had no reason to think of it that way.

A structure like that is called a flat plate. There are no beams. The floor is a flat sheet of concrete, and it is held up by columns that push straight into its underside, like fingers into the base of a cake. Where a column meets the slab there is usually a thickened square — a drop panel — because that small patch of concrete has to carry everything the column carries.

Builders like flat plates for good reasons. Without beams, a floor is thinner, so a building of a given height can hold more storeys. The formwork is simple, which makes the pour fast and cheap. The ceiling below is flat, which is easier to live under and easier to run pipes through. Across the world there are hundreds of thousands of car parks and podium decks built exactly this way, and almost all of them will stand for as long as anybody needs them to.

But a flat plate is unforgiving in a specific way, and the reason is geometry. Load does not spread evenly through a flat sheet. It runs downhill towards the supports and concentrates where they are. In a beamed floor, the beams gather the load along their whole length and hand it to the columns gradually. In a flat plate there is no intermediate step. The strength of an entire floor is decided in the few square metres of concrete immediately around each column head.

Engineers design those patches with a margin. The connection is meant to be able to carry considerably more than it will ever be asked to carry, because nobody can predict what a building will be given to hold over fifty years, and because concrete is a material with variation in it. That surplus has a name. It is called the margin against failure, and it is the difference between a structure that survives its own surprises and one that does not.

Remember that phrase. It is the one the federal government uses, five years and one collapse later, to explain this building.

So the object at the centre of this story is not really a building. It is a joint.

Champlain Towers South was built in 1981, and for most of its life nothing about it made news. People bought apartments there for the reason people buy apartments on that coast. You could hear the water from the balcony. The lift worked. In the mornings the deck caught the sun before the street did. Children learned to swim in that pool. Cars came and went from the garage underneath it, several times a day, for four decades.

Salt air is hard on that kind of construction, and everyone who builds on a coast knows it. Concrete is not solid the way a stone is solid. Look at it closely enough and it is a sponge — a hard one, but a sponge, threaded with pores too small to see. Salt water finds those pores. Sea spray lands on a deck, sits in a puddle, and works down.

Inside the concrete there is steel. That steel is what actually holds the floor together; the concrete's job is mostly to hold the steel in place and to keep the air and water away from it. In a healthy slab the chemistry of the cement keeps the steel passive, coated in a microscopic film that stops it rusting at all.

Chloride destroys that film. Once salt reaches the bar in enough concentration, the steel starts to corrode, and corroding steel does something that plain rust on a gate does not do. It swells. Rust occupies several times the volume of the metal it came from, and it does that inside a material with nowhere to give. The concrete cracks outward from the bar. The crack opens a path. More water arrives. More steel corrodes. More concrete cracks.

The process feeds itself, and it accelerates, and the visible signs of it arrive late.

There is nothing sinister in any of that. It is chemistry, it is thoroughly understood, and it is exactly why buildings on the ocean are inspected, sealed, drained and repaired on a schedule for as long as they stand. A coastal building is not a finished object. It is a maintenance commitment with apartments attached.

Drainage matters more than it sounds like it should. A flat deck over occupied space is waterproofed, and the waterproofing is meant to be laid to a fall, so that rain runs to outlets and leaves within minutes. When a deck is flat, or when the outlets sit even slightly high, water does not leave. It stands in shallow sheets after a storm, for hours, sometimes for days, and it works quietly at every seam and every upstand until it finds a way through. A waterproof layer does not have to fail across a whole deck to matter. It only has to fail where the water is.

And decks collect weight. By the second decade of a building's life there are planters on them — big ones, full of soil, because tropical planting needs depth. There is sand under the pavers, laid to level the surface. There is furniture, and paving replaced with heavier paving, and everything else that people put on a surface they think of as ground.

Every one of those things is ordinary. Every one of them is load, resting on a slab that was designed a long time before anybody put them there.

None of this was hidden. All of it was visible to anyone who went down into the garage and looked up.

For thirty-seven years, nothing happened.

## ACT II — 2018, IN WRITING (8:00–15:00)

In October 2018, an engineer named Frank Morabito walked the property.

It was routine work. Buildings of that age on that coast get recertified, and the association had commissioned a structural field survey in advance of it. Morabito's firm did the kind of inspection that firms do, which is less dramatic than the phrase suggests and more physical than most people imagine.

You walk the site. You look at every surface you can reach. You sound the concrete with a hammer, because sound concrete rings and concrete that has separated from the steel behind it gives back a dull knock, and an experienced ear can map a defect through a wall that looks perfect. You photograph what is wrong. You measure. You go into the places nobody goes — the garage, the plant rooms, the underside of everything.

Then you write it up, and you price it, because a report that describes a problem without costing the repair is of no use to the people who have to decide.

He went down into the garage. He walked the pool deck. He looked at the entrance drive and the planters.

What he found, he wrote down.

The waterproofing below the pool deck had failed. Not was failing — had failed. And because it had failed, water had been getting into the structure underneath it for a long time.

In his report he described the consequence in six words that the town of Surfside, the association and the residents of that building would all be given a copy of. The failed waterproofing was "causing major structural damage to the concrete structural slab below these areas."

Read that as an engineer writes. Major is not a colourful word in that sentence. It is a category. Structural is not decoration either; it distinguishes damage to the thing that holds the building up from damage to the things that do not.

He also wrote down where. Three places: the pool deck, the entrance drive, and the planters.

Hold on to the planters. In October 2018 an engineer walking that property listed them as one of the locations where failed waterproofing was damaging the structure underneath. Eight years later, a federal investigation would list the same objects — heavy planters, added sand, pavers — among the things that had been putting more load on that deck than it was built to hold. One man with a hammer and a national laboratory with five years of modelling ended up pointing at the same tubs of soil.

He also recorded what the concrete looked like: "abundant concrete cracking and spalling."

Spalling is what it sounds like. It is concrete coming off in pieces, usually because the steel behind it has rusted and swollen and pushed it away. Abundant is doing work in that phrase too. It is not a description of one bad patch.

When you see spalling on a soffit — the underside of a slab, the ceiling of a garage — you are not looking at cosmetic damage. You are looking at the visible end of a process that has been running invisibly for years, and at reinforcement that is no longer the size the designer drew.

The report did not use the word emergency. It did not need to. It said the damage was major, it said where it was, and then it did the thing engineering reports do, which is to say what putting it right would cost.

Nine point one million dollars.

That was the estimated probable construction cost of the repairs. It is a large number for a building of a hundred and thirty-six apartments — tens of thousands of dollars a household — and it is a very small number compared with the one at the end of this film.

The report went to the association. It went to the town.

Two days before the board met in November 2018, a board member named Mara Chouela forwarded it, together with a report on the mechanical and electrical systems, to the town of Surfside.

The man it reached was Ross Prieto, the town's chief building official. In a small town on that coast, that is the office that decides what a building is allowed to be. It issues permits. It signs off inspections. When it says a thing must be done, it is done, and when it says nothing needs doing, nothing does.

He came to the board meeting.

【OST: OCTOBER 2018 · "MAJOR STRUCTURAL DAMAGE"】

According to the minutes of that meeting, Ross Prieto told the board that the building was in very good shape.

That sentence is in the record. It was written down by the association's own secretary, in the ordinary course of a meeting about the ordinary business of a building.

Consider what it did. It did not forbid anything. It did not order anything. It did not start a clock. A board that had just been handed a report describing major structural damage to the slab under its pool deck was told, by the official whose entire job is buildings, that theirs was in very good shape.

After that, nothing was required of anyone. The repairs became a project rather than an obligation — a large expense that the residents would have to agree among themselves to pay for, on a timetable of their own choosing, with no authority waiting on the other end of it.

The work did not begin. The report went into the association's files, and into the town's files, where it stayed.

Nearly three years later, in June 2021, after the building came down, a reporter reached Prieto and asked him about the report. He said he did not remember receiving it. On the advice of a lawyer, he declined to say more.

That is the whole of what is known about that exchange, and this film is not going to guess at the rest of it. Whether he read the report, whether he understood what it described, what he meant by the words in the minutes — none of that is established, and inventing it would be the easiest and cheapest thing this film could do.

What can be said is narrower and harder to look at. The report was written. It was delivered, twice, to two different institutions. It was read into a meeting as good news. And under the swimming pool, the concrete went on doing what concrete does.

## ACT III — THE MONEY (15:00–20:00)

There is a gap in this story that is not about engineering at all, and it lasts nearly three years.

A condominium is not a landlord. There is nobody to send the bill to. There is an association, and the association is the residents themselves, and when a building needs nine point one million dollars of structural work, that money can only come from the people who live in it. Every one of a hundred and thirty-six households has to be persuaded to pay a share.

There are two ways an association finds that kind of money. It can save it in advance, in a reserve fund, which means charging every owner more every month for decades against a repair nobody can yet see. Or it can raise a special assessment when the bill arrives, which means asking several hundred people, at once, for tens of thousands of dollars each.

Reserves are the responsible answer and they are politically miserable. Every board that raises them is charging today's owners for tomorrow's concrete, and every board that does not is quietly popular. Boards are elected.

So the second route is the common one, and anyone who has sat on a residents' committee knows exactly how it goes.

There are people who want the work done immediately and people who want a second opinion, and both of them are being reasonable. There are people on fixed incomes for whom the number is not an inconvenience but an impossibility. There are people who have just retired into the apartment they saved thirty years for and who now hear that they owe a sum they do not have, for concrete they cannot see, under a pool deck that looks entirely normal. There are owners who rent their apartments out and will never walk on that deck at all.

There are elections, and boards change, and a new board inherits an estimate that is now two years old and asks, not unreasonably, whether it is still the right estimate.

None of that is unusual and none of it is wicked. It is what happens when the structure of a building and the finances of a building are owned by the same three hundred people, and when the person who has to approve the repair is also the person who has to pay for it.

Meanwhile the price did not stand still. Estimates age. Construction costs rise. And concrete that is corroding does not wait for a vote — every month of discussion is a month of the same process running underneath the discussion.

By the spring of 2021 the assessment the residents were being asked to approve was no longer nine point one million dollars.

It was more than fifteen million.

It is worth doing that division, because the arithmetic is the reason the gap exists at all.

Nine point one million dollars, spread across a hundred and thirty-six apartments, is an average of about sixty-seven thousand dollars a household. Shares are not equal — they follow the size of the apartment — so some owners would have owed less than that and some a great deal more. That was the 2018 number.

More than fifteen million dollars, across the same hundred and thirty-six apartments, is an average of more than a hundred and ten thousand.

That is the sum an owner was being asked to find. Not for a new lobby or a better pool. For concrete under a deck, most of it in a garage, none of it visible from any apartment in the building.

Delay was not irrational at those numbers. It was expensive in a way nobody could see and cheap in a way everybody could feel, which is the most reliable way for a decision to be postponed.

In April 2021, the board president, Jean Wodnicki, wrote to the residents to explain it.

That letter is the second warning, and it is the harder of the two to read, because it is not written by a consultant. It is written by a neighbour, to neighbours, about their own home, and it is trying to persuade them to spend money they would rather not spend.

【OST: APRIL 2021 · MORE THAN $15 MILLION】

She told them the damage they could see had got worse. Her words: the observable damage, including the damage in the underground garage, "has gotten significantly worse since the initial inspection."

She told them it was "accelerating."

And she told them what that meant for the years ahead — that the damage would "begin to multiply exponentially" over time.

Read the sequence again, because it is short and it is the whole film. In October 2018 an engineer wrote that the slab under the pool deck had major structural damage. In April 2021 the president of the board wrote to every resident that it had got significantly worse since then, that it was speeding up, and that it was going to multiply.

Nobody in that building was uninformed. The letter went out on the ninth of April.

## ACT IV — THE LAST SPRING (20:00–26:00)

Then people went on living there, because that is what people do.

This is the part of the story with no incident in it. There is nothing to report from April, May and the first three weeks of June 2021 except ordinary life in a building where the paperwork was, at last, moving. The assessment had been explained. The work had a price. Contracts were being arranged. On paper, this is the story of a building that was fixing itself, a few years later than it should have.

It is worth staying in those weeks rather than hurrying past them, because they are the answer to the question people ask about disasters afterwards, which is why nobody left.

Nobody left because there was nothing to leave. A building with a repair project is not an emergency. It is the most ordinary situation in coastal Florida. Scaffolding goes up somewhere on that shoreline every week of the year. A resident who had read every word of the April letter would have concluded, correctly, that their building needed expensive work and that the board had a plan to pay for it. That is not a reason to pack.

And there is something in the shape of that letter that deserves saying plainly, because it is not particular to this building. The people who knew the most about the damage were also the people asking everyone else for money. That is how a condominium works: the warning and the invoice arrive in the same envelope, signed by a neighbour.

It is very hard for a warning to be heard cleanly in that form. An owner who does not want to pay fifteen million dollars between a hundred and thirty-six households has an obvious and entirely human way to read a letter describing accelerating structural damage, which is that the board is making the case for its own assessment. Nothing about that is stupid. It is what a bill sounds like.

So the second warning, the one written by a resident to residents, had to do two jobs at once — describe a building that was deteriorating faster than expected, and persuade the people living in it to fund the repair — and those two jobs pull in opposite directions. The more urgent the description, the more it can read as advocacy.

There is no villain in that paragraph. There is only a structure of ownership in which the person who must be alarmed and the person who must be persuaded are the same person.

And the damage that was written about was in the garage and under the deck, which is to say in the two places in a residential building that nobody looks at. Nothing in an apartment on the ninth floor was different in June 2021 from how it had been in 2015.

So the last spring passed the way springs pass. Cars went in and out of the garage under the pool, several times a day, parking under a slab that two engineers had now written about. Towels were left on loungers. Laundry dried on balcony rails. After a heavy rain, water stood in a shallow sheet across the deck the way it had for years, and then went, the way it always had.

The people who lived at Champlain Towers South were not standing on the edge of anything they could see. They were living in a building with a repair project attached, which describes a great many buildings, and a great many people, on any given morning.

And underneath them, in the first week of June, in a garage nobody had any reason to be standing in, two connections between the columns and the pool deck slab failed.

There was no event to hear. Nothing shifted in a way anyone would notice from the eleventh floor. A connection that had been carrying its share of that deck stopped carrying it, and the load it had been holding went somewhere else — into the concrete and the columns beside it, which took it, because load does not stop existing when a support does. It has to go somewhere, and it goes to whatever is nearest.

That is the moment the collapse begins. Not on the twenty-fourth of June. In the first week.

## ACT V — THE THREE WEEKS (26:00–34:00)

For five years after the building fell, nobody could say with authority what had failed first. There were theories, and some of them were good theories, and none of them was a finding.

That changed on the twenty-second of June 2026, when the National Institute of Standards and Technology published the technical findings of its investigation. The team was the National Construction Safety Team; its investigative co-leads were Judith Mitrani-Reiser and Glenn Bell. They had been working on it since 2021 — collecting the physical evidence, modelling the structure, testing what the drawings said against what had actually been built.

Their finding on the sequence is specific, and it is the reason this film exists.

Two connections between garage columns and the pool deck failed in early June 2021. And that failure did not stop where it started. It spread. Over about three weeks it moved through the structure, connection by connection, until the twenty-fourth of June.

The mechanism has a name. It is called punching shear, and it is the characteristic failure of a flat plate — the exact risk that the thickened patch of concrete at each column head exists to prevent.

It works like this. The column stays where it is. The slab, loaded from above, moves down around it. Because all the load in that bay is trying to get into that one column, the concrete immediately around the column head is in the most punishing condition anywhere in the floor. If it gives, it does not crack politely across the middle of the room. A cone of concrete shears out around the column and the column head passes through the slab, the way a pencil goes through paper.

The connection stops existing. It happens fast, and from below there is almost nothing to see beforehand.

And then the load that connection was carrying has to go somewhere. It transfers to whatever is nearest — the neighbouring connections, which were designed to carry their own share and not somebody else's. If they have margin to spare, they hold, and the building stands there with an injury nobody can see. If they do not, the next one goes, and then the next.

That is what NIST found happened here, and it is why the number in their finding is three weeks and not three seconds.

Stay with that for a moment, because it is the strangest fact in this case. A building that fails all at once gives nobody anything. There is no interval. But this deck did not fail all at once. Two connections went in the first week of June, and the load they had been carrying moved outwards into the concrete beside them, and that concrete took it, and held it, and was overloaded from that moment on. Then something else gave, and the load moved again. Each transfer made the next failure more likely and the one after that more likely still, because every time the deck lost a support, the survivors were asked for more.

It ran like that for about three weeks, underneath a swimming pool, in a garage with cars parked in it.

Nothing in the record shows that anybody knew it had started. The signs of a flat plate in that condition are on the underside of the slab and around the column heads — the ceiling of a car park, at night, in a building where the structural problems had been a topic of correspondence for three years and were, everybody understood, going to be dealt with when the money was raised.

Which raises the question this film has been carrying since its first minute. Why was there not enough left to spare?

【OST: PUNCHING SHEAR】

NIST's answer comes in two parts, and the second is worse than the first.

The first part is the deck itself. The pool deck's structural design was understrength, and the steel reinforcement in it had been misplaced during construction — not where the drawings put it. On top of that, over the decades, the deck had been given more to carry than it began with: heavy planters, added sand, pavers. And the reinforcement inside it had been corroding the whole time, in the way Act I described, in a place where water stood.

Any one of those, on its own, is a maintenance problem with a known answer. Together they are an arithmetic problem, and the arithmetic had been running against that deck for a very long time.

The second part is a single sentence from the investigation's co-lead, and it is not about the pool deck at all. It is about the whole structure.

Judith Mitrani-Reiser: "When building structures are designed and built to required codes and standards, they have margins against failure, meaning they should be able to support much more load than they are expected to bear. In the case of Champlain Towers South, however, these margins against failure were too narrow from the start."

From the start.

Not from 2018, when the survey was written. Not from 2021, when the letter went out. From the beginning of the building's life, before a single person in this story had moved in, the distance between what that structure could carry and what it would be asked to carry was thinner than a building is supposed to have.

Every argument in that association about money — every meeting, every vote, every owner who could not afford the assessment and every owner who could — was an argument about restoring something to a condition that had never been good enough.

Glenn Bell, the other co-lead, set out where that shortfall came from: "The low margins against failure were primarily caused by two factors… First, severe and widespread deviations in the building's original structural design from the codes and standards of the day, but also some limitations in those codes and standards. And second, deviations in the building's construction from the design drawings."

Two kinds of gap, then. Between the code and the design. And between the design and the building that was actually poured.

Neither is a thing a resident can see. Neither is a thing that can be found by walking a garage with a hammer, because a hammer tells you the state of the concrete, not whether the drawing it was built from was ever adequate. And neither can be fixed by approving an assessment.

【OST: "TOO NARROW FROM THE START"】

There is one more thing to line up before this act closes, and it does not require any interpretation at all. It only requires reading two documents next to each other.

In October 2018, a structural engineer wrote that failed waterproofing was causing major structural damage to the concrete slab below the pool deck, the entrance drive and the planters.

In June 2026, a federal investigation found that the collapse began at two connections between garage columns and the pool deck; that the pool deck's design was understrength; that its reinforcement had been misplaced during construction; that heavy planters, added sand and pavers had increased what it was carrying; and that the steel inside it had been corroding for decades.

Those two documents are about the same slab.

That is not an argument and it is not a claim about what would have happened if the repairs had gone ahead — nobody has established that, and this film will not pretend otherwise. It is simply the observation that the piece of this building which the federal government eventually identified as the place where the collapse began is the piece of the building an engineer had put in writing, and priced, two years and eight months earlier.

It is worth being precise about what that finding does and does not establish, because this is where films about disasters usually overreach, and because the people in this story are entitled to that precision.

It establishes that the structure had less strength in reserve than the standards of its own day called for, and that the pool deck's connections were where the shortfall was worst. It establishes the order of events: those two connections first, in early June, and then three weeks of spreading failure.

It does not establish that anybody understood the shortfall at the time the building was designed or built. It does not establish that the 2018 repairs, if they had been carried out at once, would have prevented what happened — NIST does not say that, and neither will this film. And it does not establish anybody's intent, then or afterwards.

What it establishes is narrower than blame and heavier than blame. The margins were thin from the beginning. Salt and water spent forty years eating into what was left of them, in a place where the drainage let water stand. Two connections gave out in the first week of June. The rest followed over three weeks. And every warning that a human being could have been expected to act on had been put in writing, twice, and read into a meeting as good news.

## THE NIGHT (34:00–36:45)

On the twenty-fourth of June 2021, at about half past one in the morning, part of Champlain Towers South came down.

It was a Thursday. Most of the people in the building were asleep.

【OST: 24 JUNE 2021 · ~1:30 A.M.】

Ninety-eight people died.

This film is not going to describe that. It is not going to name them, or stage their last minutes, or show the search that followed. Their families have been asked to look at those images for five years, and nothing here requires it of them again.

What the record contains, and what this film can say, is this. The building had been examined. It had been written about, priced, discussed, and warned about — by an engineer, and then by one of its own residents. The money to repair it had been counted twice, and both counts were smaller than what it cost not to. And when it finally went, it went in the middle of the night, without notice, from a garage that nobody was standing in.

## ENDING (36:45–40:00)

On the twenty-third of June 2022, one day before the first anniversary, Judge Michael Hanzman of the Miami-Dade Circuit Court gave final approval to the settlement of the claims arising from the collapse. It involved more than twenty-four defendants. The total was one billion, twenty-one million, one hundred and ninety-nine thousand dollars.

Set that beside the other number in this story. Nine point one million dollars to repair the building in 2018. Fifteen million by 2021. One billion and twenty-one million afterwards.

Judge Hanzman said this about it from the bench: "It will never be enough to compensate them for the tragic loss they have suffered. This settlement is the best we can do. It's a remarkable result. It is extraordinary."

About ninety-six million of it was set aside for the people who had lost one of the hundred and thirty-six apartments. Around a hundred million went to legal fees.

Divide that ninety-six million the same way the assessment would have been divided, and it comes to something over seven hundred thousand dollars an apartment. The assessment those same owners had been arguing about, and had not paid, averaged a little over a hundred thousand.

The building was worth more destroyed than the repair was worth intact. That is not a moral observation. It is what the two documents say when you put them side by side.

In December 2021, a Miami-Dade grand jury reported on the collapse. It set out recommendations for condominium safety across Florida — inspections, reserve funds, the things this case had made obvious to everyone who read about it. It did not reach a conclusion about what had caused this particular building to fall. That answer was still four and a half years away, in a federal laboratory.

And no individual and no company has been criminally charged over the deaths of those ninety-eight people.

That is where the case stands.

It is worth understanding what that last sentence does and does not mean, because it is easy to hear it as an accusation and it is not one. A criminal charge requires a prosecutor to be able to prove, to a jury, that a specific person did a specific thing that the law forbids. A finding that a structure had thin margins from the start is not that. It does not name a person. It does not identify a decision. Most of the people who designed and built that deck made those choices more than forty years ago, under the codes and the practices of that time, and the federal finding says the codes themselves had limitations. That is a hard thing to put in an indictment, and the record shows nobody has.

So the account, five years on, closes like this. A billion dollars moved. Recommendations were written. Nobody was charged. And the answer to what actually failed arrived from a laboratory in June 2026, four and a half years after the grand jury had reported that it could not say.

There is one more thing in the record, and it is the reason this film is forty minutes long instead of five.

Every fact that mattered at Champlain Towers South was written down before the building fell. The waterproofing below the pool deck had failed: written down, October 2018. The damage to the slab was major and structural: written down, October 2018. It had got significantly worse and was accelerating and would multiply: written down, April 2021. The price of stopping it: written down twice, and both times in a figure smaller than the price of not stopping it.

The building did not fall because nobody knew.

It fell with the paperwork in order.
