The highest court in the country would say yes. It could.

This is Prime Documentary. The year is 2005, and the case is Kelo versus City of New London. It is remembered as one of the most disliked rulings the Supreme Court has handed down in modern times, and not because the Court struck something down. Because it let something stand.

The whole fight is over four words in the Fifth Amendment. Private property shall not be taken for public use, it says, without just compensation. Everyone agrees the government can take your home to build a highway, so long as it pays you. The question here was smaller, and stranger. What actually counts as public use? Could a city take a dozen lived-in homes, hand the land to private developers, and call the jobs and taxes that might follow a public use all its own?

## 3. BODY

### ACT 1 — The plan for the point
【Fastest, shortest act. Present tense. Symbols only: a shuttered factory; a "distressed" city; a corporate site plan; a model of a gleaming waterfront; the older, modest houses of Fort Trumbull; the condemnation notices.】

New London, in the late 1990s, is a city that has been losing for a long time. The industry has thinned. The jobs have gone. A federal military facility nearby had shut its doors, and the state had labeled the city distressed. The people in charge are desperate for something, anything, to turn it around.

Then comes the anchor. The pharmaceutical giant Pfizer announces it will build a research headquarters in New London, a facility worth some three hundred million dollars, right beside an old waterfront neighborhood called Fort Trumbull. And the city sees its opening. If Pfizer is the magnet, the land around it becomes worth something. So the city hands a private nonprofit, the New London Development Corporation, the job of remaking the entire point.

The plan is grand. Ninety acres. A waterfront hotel. Office towers and research space. New upscale housing. A marina. A riverwalk. On paper it promises thousands of jobs and millions in fresh tax revenue for a city that badly needs both.

There is only one problem. People live there.

Fort Trumbull is a modest, older neighborhood, about a hundred and fifteen properties. Most of the owners take the buyout and leave. But a handful will not. They are not holding out for a bigger check. They simply do not want to give up homes they love.

Susette Kelo is one of them. She is a nurse. She bought her pink house in 1997, on the parcel of the map the planners had numbered three, and she made it hers. Another holdout was an elderly woman who had been born in her house and had lived there her whole life. These are not speculators waiting to cash out. They are people at home.

When they will not sell, the city takes the land the hard way. It reaches for eminent domain, the government's power to force the sale of private property whether the owner agrees or not. The notices go out. The homes are condemned, not because anything is wrong with them, but because they stand where the plan wants something else.

So the holdouts sue. Their argument is plain, and it goes straight at those four words. You are not taking our homes for a public use. You are taking them for a private developer, on a promise. And a promise of jobs and taxes is not what public use was ever meant to mean.

They do not fight alone. A small public-interest law firm, the Institute for Justice, takes up their cause and carries it, appeal after appeal, out of a Connecticut courtroom and toward Washington. What started as one small neighborhood's refusal to move slowly becomes a national test of a single question. How far, exactly, does public use reach?

### ACT 2 — What "public use" means
【Medium pace. Doctrinal build. Symbols: the marble colonnade; the text of the Fifth Amendment; a gavel; a blighted street from an old case; a map of concentrated land. Card: "5 / 4" holds until the vote lands. Neutral attribution: Stevens for the majority, Kennedy concurring.】

The case climbs, the way these cases do, all the way to the Supreme Court of the United States. And to understand what the Court decides, you have to know that those four words had already been stretched, long before Susette Kelo.

For most of the country's history, taking land for a public use meant something you could picture. A road. A bridge. A courthouse. A rail line the public could ride. But twice before, the Court had read the words more loosely. It had let a city clear a blighted, run-down neighborhood and turn the land over to private builders. It had let a state break up farmland held by a tiny handful of owners and sell it off to ordinary buyers. In each, the taking served a public purpose, even though private hands ended up holding the land.

New London leaned on exactly that idea. Its neighborhood was not blighted. Nothing was falling down. But reviving a dying local economy, the city argued, jobs and taxes and growth, was a public purpose all its own, and that was enough.

On June the twenty-third, 2005, the Supreme Court agreed.

【CARD: "5 / 4" resolves here, on the vote.】

By a vote of five to four.

Justice John Paul Stevens wrote for the majority, and the move he made is the entire case. The Constitution says public use. The Court read it to mean public purpose. Promoting economic development, Stevens wrote, is a traditional and long accepted function of government. The city had not seized these homes on a whim. It had studied the area, drawn up a careful, comprehensive plan, and decided the whole point would benefit. When a legislature reaches a judgment like that, the majority said, courts should not sit as referees over the wisdom of it. If the plan served a public purpose, it satisfied the Constitution, even though private developers would build on the land.

Read the holding closely, because it is narrower than the anger around it suggests. The Court did not say the government can take your home for any reason it likes. It said a taking that is part of a genuine, deliberate plan to benefit the public can count as a public use, even when private parties are the ones who profit from what gets built.

And the fifth vote came with a warning attached. Justice Anthony Kennedy joined the majority, but he wrote separately to mark the limits. A taking meant only to enrich some favored private party, dressed up in public benefits that are pretextual, or trivial, or implausible, would still be forbidden. Some cases, he suggested, might be so suspicious that a court should look harder before allowing them. It was a door deliberately left open. It just was not enough, on these facts, to save the pink house.

### ACT 3 — The four who said no
【Longest emotional beat. Symbols: four empty chairs at the bench; the Motel 6 sign and a grand hotel; a farm and a factory; the words "for public use" fading from a page. Neutral attribution: this is the dissent, not the Court. O'Connor verbatim, then Thomas.】

Four justices dissented, and it is their words, not the majority's, that people carry out of this case.

Justice Sandra Day O'Connor wrote the main dissent, joined by the Chief Justice and by Justices Scalia and Thomas. Her warning was simple. If a promise of more jobs and more taxes is all it takes to justify a taking, then almost no home is ever truly safe, because almost any wealthier owner would throw off more jobs and more taxes than the family living there now.

Listen to how she put it. The specter of condemnation, she wrote, hangs over all property. Nothing is to prevent the State from replacing any Motel 6 with a Ritz-Carlton, any home with a shopping mall, or any farm with a factory.

And the harm, she argued, would not fall on everyone alike. The beneficiaries, she wrote, are likely to be those citizens with disproportionate influence and power in the political process, including large corporations and development firms. The government, she warned, now has license to transfer property from those with fewer resources to those with more.

Justice Clarence Thomas wrote separately, and reached further back. To him, the words public use meant use by the public, and nothing looser. Reading them as public purpose, he argued, quietly erased a limit the Founders had written down on purpose. And that erasure, he warned, would fall hardest on the poor and the powerless, whose neighborhoods have always been the easiest ones to condemn.

Here is the strange, uncomfortable center of this whole story. The dissent lost. The city won. The Court did not rule that taking the pink house was illegal. It ruled that the city was allowed to do it. The fight the four dissenters were having was not really about what the law was that morning. It was about what the law ought to be. And on that, they were outvoted, four to five.

So the city could take the homes. And it did. The holdouts lost. The neighborhood was cleared, the houses pulled down, the point of land scraped flat and made ready for the grand plan.

Now here is what got built on it.

Nothing.

The developer could never line up the financing. The grand plan stalled, and then it fell apart. And in 2009, the anchor that had started all of it, Pfizer, announced it was leaving New London altogether, closing the very research center the whole scheme had been designed around, taking more than a thousand jobs with it, right as the tax breaks that lured it were set to expire.

The land where Susette Kelo's pink house had stood became an empty lot. For years it grew weeds and produced not one dollar of the tax revenue the city had gone all the way to the Supreme Court to win. After a hurricane in 2011, the cleared neighborhood was used as a dumping ground, a place to pile up storm debris and broken branches.

The pink house itself was the one thing spared. A preservationist had it taken apart, board by board, and rebuilt on another lot elsewhere in New London, a small stubborn monument to a fight its owner had lost.

But the largest thing Kelo left behind was never in Connecticut at all. The decision landed like a match in dry grass. People across the whole political spectrum, left and right, were shaken that the government could take a family's home and hand the ground to a developer on a promise. And their state governments heard them. In the years after the ruling, more than forty states passed new laws to rein in eminent domain, to make it harder to do to their own citizens what New London had been allowed to do to Susette Kelo.

Some of those laws were sweeping, and some were barely more than gestures, and lawyers still argue over how much they really changed on the ground. But the direction was impossible to miss. A decision the Court had handed down as a victory for struggling cities had, within just a few short years, set off one of the largest waves of property-rights lawmaking in a generation, almost all of it aimed at undoing exactly what the Court had just permitted.

So go back to the question at the pink house. Can the government take the home you own, and give the ground under it to someone else? After 2005, the Supreme Court's answer is that, in the right circumstances, yes, it can. Five justices said the Constitution permits it.

But a country that recoiled from that answer spent the years afterward writing a different one, state by state, house by house. The Court said the city could. Most of America looked at a weed-grown vacant lot where a little pink house used to be, and decided that it should not.
