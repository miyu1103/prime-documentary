## 1. HOOK
【~0:00–0:20. Concrete-scene cold open, ~2s cuts. Symbols only: a phone face-up on a car dashboard on a bright Saturday; a convenience-store sign; a thumb over a Snapchat send button; a countdown ticking from 24 hours; no face, only hands and the back of a shoulder.】

It is a Saturday. A fourteen-year-old girl is standing in a convenience store parking lot, a few miles from her school, on her own time, on her own phone.

The court records keep her name as two initials, so we will do the same. B.L.

She did not make the varsity cheer team. She is angry about it, the ordinary, burning way a person is angry at that age when the thing they wanted goes to someone else. So she does what almost every teenager you know has done at least once. She lifts her phone, and she posts. A picture, a raised middle finger, a few furious words about school and cheer, sent to her friends on Snapchat, set to vanish in a day.

Not a speech. Not a protest. A weekend vent, off campus, meant to disappear.

【beat】

And that disappearing little post climbs, somehow, all the way to the Supreme Court of the United States. The question it carries is one you should ask about the phone in your own pocket.

Can your school reach off its own grounds, into a Saturday, into your private feed, and punish you for what you posted there?

## 2. OPENING
【OPENING_SEC=3.50. Gold BrandOpening after the hook, not at frame zero. Then title card: "THE WEEKEND SNAP".】

This is a small town in Pennsylvania, a junior varsity cheer squad, and a Snap that was built to last twenty-four hours and nothing more.

What that Snap decided is far bigger than any of that. For the first time in more than fifty years, the Supreme Court looked straight at the words a student says when they are nowhere near a classroom, and asked a simple question with no easy edges. How far past the schoolhouse door does the school's authority actually run?

## 3. BODY

### ACT 1 — A Saturday, a phone, a squad
【Shortest, fastest act. Present tense. Restrained. Symbols only. R2: B.L. is never shown. Use the parking lot, the phone screen, empty bleachers, a cheer uniform on a hook, a locker. Constraint 4: dignity, she was a minor, no distress sensationalism.】

Start with what actually happened, because the facts are smaller than the fight they started.

B.L. is a student at Mahanoy Area High School. She is a cheerleader, but only on the junior varsity squad, and she wants varsity. She tries out. She does not make it. To sharpen the sting, a spot on the varsity team goes instead to an incoming freshman, while she is told to wait another year on JV.

So that weekend, at a convenience store called the Cocoa Hut, she takes out her phone and makes two posts to her Snapchat story, the kind that show for a day and then erase themselves. She sends them to her friends, a private circle of a couple hundred people who followed her there.

The first is the angry one. A photo of her and a friend, middle fingers up, over a caption cursing school and softball and cheer and, in her words, everything. The second is quieter, just text, complaining that she and another girl were told they needed a year of JV before varsity while someone else skipped the line.

【beat】

No teacher was tagged. The school was not named. It is a frustrated kid, venting to her friends, on a Saturday, off campus.

But a post to a couple hundred teenagers is not a secret. Screenshots move. Word reaches the cheerleading coaches. And the school decides that the angry Snap has broken the squad's rules about respect and about avoiding negative posts.

The punishment is not a detention. B.L. is suspended from the junior varsity cheerleading squad for the entire upcoming year.

For a weekend post that was supposed to be gone in a day, she loses a full season.

### ACT 2 — The schoolhouse gate
【Medium pace. The turn: from one squad's rule to a fifty-year-old constitutional line. Symbols: a courthouse hallway, a 1969 photo frame with black armbands implied by silhouette only, an appellate seal, a map narrowing from a town to Washington. Constraint 2: Tinker is the anchor and must be stated precisely.】

Her parents do not let it go. They take the school to federal court, and they say the suspension punished their daughter for her speech, which the First Amendment protects.

To understand why that argument has teeth, you have to go back to 1969, to the case that every student-speech fight since has been measured against. Tinker versus Des Moines.

〔CARD: Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School Dist., 393 U.S. 503 (1969). confidence: high.〕

In Tinker, students wore black armbands to school to protest the Vietnam War, and the school punished them for it. The Supreme Court said the school was wrong, and it wrote a sentence that has anchored this entire area of law ever since. Students, the Court said, do not shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate.

That is the promise. You keep your rights when you walk in the door.

But Tinker did not hand students an unlimited megaphone. It drew a line in the same breath. A school can still restrict student speech when that speech would materially disrupt the work of the school or collide with the rights of other students. Inside the gate, disruption is the test.

【beat】

Here is the problem the internet created. Tinker was about armbands worn inside a school, during school. B.L.'s Snap was made on a Saturday, at a store, on her own phone. So which world does it belong to? Is a weekend post to private friends inside the schoolhouse gate, or a thousand miles outside it?

The lower courts sided with B.L., and the federal appeals court went furthest of all. It ruled that Tinker simply does not reach off campus. Off school grounds, that court said, a school has no special power over a student's speech at all.

That was a clean, bright line. And it was that clean, bright line, more than B.L.'s single Snap, that carried the case up to the Supreme Court. Because a rule that broad worried a lot of people, on every side.

Think about what a rule like that would actually mean. If a school truly had no power over anything a student said off campus, then a threat typed at home against a teacher, a campaign of harassment run from a bedroom phone, a plan to cheat coordinated over a weekend, all of it would sit beyond the school's reach, no matter how much damage it did once Monday came. The appeals court had drawn its line to protect students like B.L. But the very same line would have tied a school's hands in cases far uglier than a frustrated cheerleader's Snap. That was the knot the justices agreed to untangle. The school district asked them to step in, and they took the case.

### ACT 3 — Diminished, not gone
【Longest, slowest act. Doctrinal core. Present tense at the ruling. Constraint 1: no overclaim. Constraint 3: vote 8-1, neutral attribution. Keep the human thread: return to B.L. inside the doctrine.】

In June of 2021, the Supreme Court ruled, and the vote was lopsided. Eight justices to one.

〔CARD: Mahanoy Area School District v. B. L., 594 U.S. ___ (2021). Decided June 23, 2021. 8–1. confidence: high.〕

Justice Stephen Breyer wrote for the Court. And the first thing to understand is what the Court did not do, because this is exactly where the headlines get it wrong.

The Court did not say schools can never touch what you post off campus. It said the opposite, plainly. A school's power to regulate student speech does not simply vanish the moment a student steps off the property. In some off-campus situations, the Court said, the school's interest stays very real.

And it gave a list. Schools may still reach off-campus speech that is serious bullying or harassment aimed at a particular person. Threats aimed at teachers or other students. Cheating, and breaking the rules on lessons, papers, and school computer use. Breaches of the school's own security. That list matters, so hold onto it. The Court left that door open on purpose.

So B.L. did not win because off-campus speech is a free-fire zone. She won for a narrower, more careful reason.

【beat. The doctrinal center. Constraint 1, 3.】

Breyer said that while a school's interest in off-campus speech is not gone, it is usually diminished, and he gave three reasons why.

First, when you speak off campus, the school is rarely standing in for your parents. That job, off school grounds, on a weekend, belongs to your family, not your principal.

Second, think about the clock. Your on-campus hours plus your off-campus hours are simply your whole life. If a school could police both, it could police everything you say, all day, every day. So courts, the Court warned, have to be more skeptical when a school tries to reach the twenty-four-hour world outside its walls.

And third, the reason with the most weight. A school has its own interest in protecting a student's unpopular opinions, especially off campus. Because, in Breyer's words, America's public schools are the nurseries of democracy. The place we are supposed to learn that even the crude, the angry, and the unpopular voice gets to speak. Popular ideas, the Court noted, have little need for protection. It is the unpopular ones that do.

【beat】

Now put B.L. back inside that frame. Her post was outside school hours, from outside the school. She did not name the school or aim her words at any one person. She sent it privately, to her own friends. And the disruption the school pointed to? The record showed a few minutes of chatter in one Algebra class, over a couple of days, and some upset cheerleaders. Under Tinker's demanding standard, that is nothing close to enough.

So the Court held that punishing her crossed the line. The school violated B.L.'s First Amendment rights when it suspended her for a weekend Snap.

Two of the justices in the majority wanted to say more. Justice Samuel Alito, joined by Justice Neil Gorsuch, agreed with the result but wrote separately to sketch how the line should be found. When you enroll your child in a public school, Alito reasoned, you hand that school a slice of your own authority as a parent, but only a slice, and only for the things a school is actually for. A weekend post to your friends, aimed at no one in particular, falls far outside anything a parent quietly hands over at the schoolhouse door.

And one justice disagreed altogether. Justice Clarence Thomas, writing alone, looked back at more than a century of history in which schools disciplined students for off-campus speech that reached back into the schoolhouse. He pointed to old cases where schools punished words spoken blocks away from campus whenever that speech tended to feed back and undermine the school's authority. B.L.'s Snap, he argued, was aimed squarely at her own teammates and coaches and traveled straight back onto school grounds, which to him placed it well within a school's traditional reach.

Eight of his colleagues went the other way. And the practical result stood: the courts ordered the school to put B.L. back on the squad and to wipe the suspension from her record.

## 4. ENDING
【Emotional payoff → resolve the cold-open question → one earned CTA in the last 30 seconds. ENDCARD_SEC=9.00.】

Go back to that parking lot. A Saturday, a phone, a post built to vanish in a day.

The reason this reached the highest court in the country is not that one teenager was rude about her cheer squad. It is that her small, disappearing Snap sat exactly on the fault line the internet opened under a rule written in 1969. When school is a building you leave at three o'clock, the schoolhouse gate is easy to find. When school follows you home in your pocket, on a device that is with you every hour you are awake, where is the gate now?

The Supreme Court's answer was not a wall and it was not an open field. It was a line, drawn with care. Your school does not lose all authority the second you log off its property. It can still act against genuine threats, real harassment, cheating, the things that reach back and actually wound the school or the people in it.

But for an ordinary, angry, off-campus vent, a bad mood posted to your friends on a weekend, the school's reach is weakest, and your right to speak is strongest. The Court would not turn every student's private phone into school property. It would not let the schoolhouse gate quietly expand until it wrapped around your whole life.

So, the question we started with. Can your school punish you for what you post on a weekend, off campus, to your friends? After 2021, the honest answer is a careful one. Usually not, unless what you posted crosses into the narrow zone the Court left open. The right you keep at the schoolhouse gate, it turns out, mostly follows you back out through it.
