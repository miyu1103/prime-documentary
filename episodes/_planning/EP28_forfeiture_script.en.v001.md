## OPENING  (金 BrandOpening の後 · thesis)
This is the story of how a city could seize your home without ever proving you did a single thing wrong — and how one family painting contractor decided to make them stop.
【OST: PHILADELPHIA · CIVIL FORFEITURE】

## ACT I — 家  (普通の一日が壊れる)
Christos Sourovelis painted houses for a living. He and his wife, Markela, owned a modest two-story home in the northeast corner of Philadelphia — the kind of block where people retire, worth a little over three hundred thousand dollars, most of it earned one wall at a time.

In March of 2014, their twenty-two-year-old son, Yianni, made a decision that would follow the whole family. He sold about forty dollars of heroin to a stranger — a stranger who turned out to be a police informant. Within days he was arrested. He'd later be ordered into court-supervised rehab.

Forty dollars. A small, ugly mistake by a young man in trouble. His parents didn't even know he was involved with heroin until the police came.

And here is where you'd expect the story to end — a son in the system, a family shaken, life going on.
Except the city wasn't finished. Because the city had noticed something else.
It had noticed the house.
【OST: $40】

## ACT II — 機械  (正体の開示)
On the eighth of May, prosecutors went to a judge — quietly, with no one from the family in the room — and came back with an order to **seize and seal** the Sourovelis home.

No warning. No hearing. A knock, and then: pack a bag, and get out. The door of the house Christos had paid for was sealed by the city he paid taxes to.

Now, how is that possible — to take a family's home when the family has been charged with nothing? The answer is a strange and powerful tool called **civil forfeiture**. In a normal case, the government accuses a person, and has to prove it. In civil forfeiture, the government accuses the *property* — the house itself becomes the defendant. And a house has no right to a lawyer, no presumption of innocence. The owners are almost an afterthought.
【OST: THE HOUSE ON TRIAL】

So the Sourovelises did what you would do. For about a week, they slept on a relative's couch — homeless in their own city, while the government held the deed to their home. They were finally let back in on one condition: Christos had to sign a paper promising to bar his own son from the house.

To get their home back, they had to give up their son at the door.

And if they wanted to fight it? They would have to go to a room called **Courtroom 478**, high up in Philadelphia's City Hall. There was no judge in Courtroom 478. The proceedings were run by the same prosecutors trying to take your property. There were no free lawyers. And if you missed a date — if you couldn't get off work, if you didn't understand the form — you could lose your home by default.
【OST: COURTROOM 478 · NO JUDGE · NO LAWYER】

This was not a courtroom. It was a collection department.

## ACT III — 抵抗  (これは他人事じゃない → 反撃)
And the Sourovelis family was not special. That is the part that should stop you.

Between 2002 and 2014, Philadelphia used civil forfeiture to take more than **twelve hundred homes**. More than thirty-five hundred cars. Over fifty million dollars in cash. And the typical cash seizure? About **a hundred and seventy-eight dollars**.
【OST: 1,200+ HOMES · $50M+ CASH】

Think about that number. A hundred and seventy-eight dollars. This machine was not built to catch kingpins. It was built to take small amounts from ordinary people — the people least able to hire a lawyer and fight back.

And here is the engine underneath it. The money the police and prosecutors seized went **back into their own budgets** — nearly six million dollars a year, paying the salaries of the very people doing the seizing. Over a decade, Philadelphia took in more this way than Brooklyn and Los Angeles combined. The more they took, the more they had.
【OST: THE MONEY PAID THEIR OWN SALARIES】

It was so brazen that even the Supreme Court had started to notice. In 2017, Justice Clarence Thomas wrote that civil forfeiture had produced "egregious and well-chronicled abuses" — a system that, in his words, "frequently target[s] the poor."

But you cannot appeal to the Supreme Court from a relative's couch. So the Sourovelises joined with a public-interest law firm, the Institute for Justice, and did something that firm had never done in its history: they filed a class action — for everyone Philadelphia had done this to. Christos Sourovelis, and three neighbors named Doila Welch, Norys Hernandez, and Nassir Geiger, put their names on a lawsuit against the entire machine.
【OST: SOUROVELIS v. CITY OF PHILADELPHIA】

Now the question was no longer whether one family could save one house.
It was whether a city could be forced to stop.

## ACT IV — 決着 / ペイオフ
It took four years.

The Sourovelises got their own home free within about a year — the city dropped its case against their house, and they kept it. But they didn't drop the class action. And in 2018, Philadelphia surrendered the machine itself.

The city signed federal agreements that dismantled the program. No more seizing homes over simple drug possession. No more taking small sums of cash with no arrest. Real judges — not prosecutors — would run the hearings. The police and district attorney were **banned from spending forfeiture money on their own budgets**, killing the incentive at its root. And now the government would have to prove the owner actually knew about a crime. They also created a **three-million-dollar fund** to pay back the people they had taken from.
【OST: THE MACHINE WAS DISMANTLED】

Courtroom 478 was closed.

## ENDING
Remember how this started. Not with a guilty man — with a guilty *house*, and a family who had done nothing, told to pack a bag and leave.

They did nothing wrong. And for a while, that wasn't enough to keep their home. The whole idea of American justice is that the government has to prove it — before it takes your freedom, before it takes your things, before it takes your home. In Philadelphia, for years, that order was reversed: they took first, and made you prove your way back.

A painter and his neighbors put it right side up again.

If you didn't know your government could do this — hit like, so more people do.
【OST: PROOF BEFORE PUNISHMENT】
