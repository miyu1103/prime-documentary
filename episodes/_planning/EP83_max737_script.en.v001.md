## ACT_1 — THE FLIGHT BEFORE, AND THE FLIGHT AFTER

There is a version of this story where everybody lives.

It happened the day before. Same aeroplane. Same fault. Same wheel.

On the evening of the twenty-eighth of October, 2018, a Boeing 737 MAX left Denpasar for Jakarta. In the jump seat behind the two pilots sat a third man, an off-duty first officer from the same airline, rated on the same aircraft, riding home. <!-- MX-706 -->

Just after the wheels left the ground, the control column in the captain's hands began to shake. <!-- MX-706 -->

That shake is called a stick shaker. It is the aeroplane telling the pilot he is about to stall.

He was not about to stall. The aeroplane was flying normally. One sensor on the outside of the fuselage was reporting the wrong angle, and it was wrong by about twenty-one degrees, and it stayed wrong. <!-- MX-702 -->

The stick shaker ran for the whole flight. Ninety-six minutes. <!-- MX-706 -->

Imagine a fire alarm you cannot switch off, in a room where there is no fire, while you do your job.

The captain worked out that his own instruments were lying to him and handed the aeroplane to the first officer. <!-- MX-707 -->

Then the nose went down on its own.

The third man, the one in the jump seat, said it out loud: the aircraft was diving. <!-- MX-707 -->

The nose came down again. And again. Three times, the trim moved without anyone asking it to, and each time the pilots pulled back against it, and the first officer said the column was too heavy to hold. <!-- MX-707 -->

And then the captain named it.

He decided this was a runaway stabiliser — a fault every 737 pilot has trained for, with a fix that lives in the memory rather than in a book. Two switches on the console between the seats. Cut the electricity to the trim. <!-- MX-707 -->

At eight minutes past two in the afternoon, Greenwich time, the automatic trim stopped. <!-- MX-707 -->

He flew the rest of the way to Jakarta trimming the aeroplane by hand, with a wheel he turned himself, with an alarm shaking his controls the entire time. <!-- MX-707 -->

They landed. Everybody walked off.

The investigators wrote it down plainly afterwards. That crew was able to land the aeroplane while experiencing the same conditions as the flight that crashed. <!-- MX-709 -->

Hold that sentence. Everything else in this film is the distance between that sentence and the next morning.

Because the next morning, the same aeroplane took off again. <!-- MX-709 -->

Lion Air Flight 610 left Jakarta at twenty past six in the morning, local time, on the twenty-ninth of October. <!-- MX-001, MX-901 -->

The left stick shaker started at rotation and continued for most of the flight. <!-- MX-703 -->

Nine seconds after that, the two airspeed indicators disagreed with each other. Fifty seconds later, so did the altimeters. <!-- MX-703 -->

Then the flaps came up, and something else woke up with them. The automatic nose-down trim ran for about ten seconds and moved the stabiliser from six point one units to three point eight. <!-- MX-704 -->

The pilots trimmed back up. It came down again. They trimmed up. It came down again.

More than twenty times, that wheel turned itself down, and two men turned it back up, and neither of them knew what they were fighting. <!-- MX-704, MX-111 -->

Near the end the first officer was flying, pulling on the column with ninety-three, then a hundred and three pounds of force. Think of holding a full suitcase off the floor with your fingertips, while it argues with you. <!-- MX-705 -->

At thirty-one minutes and fifty-three seconds past eleven, Greenwich time, the system commanded the nose down one more time. One second later the flight data recorder stopped. One second after that, so did the voice recorder. <!-- MX-705 -->

All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board died. <!-- MX-001 -->

Eleven minutes, from the runway to the sea. <!-- MX-705 -->

The day before, the same failure had lasted ninety-six minutes and everybody had gone home. <!-- MX-706 -->

So what was different in the cockpit that morning?

## ACT_2 — WHY THIS WAS NOT SUPPOSED TO BE POSSIBLE

Nothing was different in the cockpit. That is the part that takes a while to sit down.

Same aeroplane. Same broken sensor, still off by the same twenty-one degrees. Same shaking column. Same switches on the same console, four inches from the captain's right hand. <!-- MX-702, MX-707 -->

To understand why those switches were not enough, you have to understand what everybody in this story was trying to do — and it is worth saying that the thing they were trying to do was not sinister. It was ordinary, and it is the whole economics of building aeroplanes.

Airlines do not really buy aeroplanes. They buy fleets. And a fleet is only cheap if every pilot can fly every aircraft in it.

If a new model is different enough, the regulator says: your pilots must be retrained. And retraining does not mean a booklet. At the deep end it means a full-flight simulator — a box on hydraulic legs, an instructor, hours per pilot, thousands of pilots.

The regulator has a scale for this. The Federal Aviation Administration's Aircraft Evaluation Group decides how different a new aeroplane is, on a ladder from Level A to Level E. Level B is generally computer-based training — a course a pilot can do on a laptop. Level D generally means the simulator. <!-- MX-101 -->

The 737 MAX was designed to land on Level B.

Not by accident. In December of 2011, Boeing signed a contract with Southwest Airlines, the launch customer, that obliged Boeing to discount every MAX it delivered by at least one million dollars if the FAA required simulator training. <!-- MX-612 -->

Southwest had ordered two hundred of them, with options for nearly two hundred more. Had Boeing failed to get Level B or better, the Committee that later investigated all this calculated the bill at between two hundred million and nearly four hundred million dollars. <!-- MX-613 -->

So there was a number attached to a training decision. Write that down and leave it there for now, because a number is not a motive, and this film is not going to pretend it is one.

Here is what the aeroplane actually needed.

The MAX had bigger, more efficient engines than the model before it, mounted further forward and higher on the wing. In certain conditions — nose high, turning hard — that changes how the aeroplane feels as it approaches a stall.

So Boeing wrote a piece of software to push the nose back down in those conditions and make the aeroplane feel like the old one. Its name was the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System. MCAS. <!-- MX-204 -->

Read that name again slowly, because it is honest. It is not a safety system. It is not an anti-stall system. It is a system for augmenting the characteristics of the aeroplane. Its job was to make a difference disappear.

And a piece of software whose job is to make a difference disappear is, by definition, a piece of software the regulator must be told about — because the regulator's entire question is: how different is this aeroplane?

The Aircraft Evaluation Group was told about MCAS. Early. And it agreed that MCAS was small enough not to matter for training. <!-- MX-103 -->

It agreed on the basis of one specific thing. As the group understood it, MCAS could only activate in a high-speed, wind-up turn — a manoeuvre a passenger will never be in, that pilots do in testing. <!-- MX-103 -->

Which was true when they were told it.

The engineer at Boeing who managed that relationship wrote the qualification down himself, in August of 2016. The Level B decision, he said, was a provisional approval, assuming no significant systems changes to the airplane. <!-- MX-104 -->

Provisional. Assuming.

That is the hinge of the whole film, and it is written in his own email, months before anyone died.

Because there was a change coming. And when it came, the assumption underneath the approval quietly stopped being true — and nobody went back to the regulator and said so.

Why not? There is an answer to that, and it is in writing too, from three months later.

In November of 2016, discussing a different change to the MAX's flight controls, the same man wrote to colleagues that one of the programme directives they had been given was to not create any differences. This, he wrote, is what we sold to the regulators who have already granted us the Level B differences determination. To go back to them now, and tell them there is in fact a difference, would be a huge threat to that differences training determination. <!-- MX-105 -->

Nobody in that sentence is planning a crime. Read it again and you will hear something more ordinary and much harder to fix.

He is describing a door that only opens one way. You may walk through it with an approval. You may not walk back through it with a correction.

And it is worth saying who he was, in the machine. Boeing's 737 MAX Flight Technical Team was principally responsible for identifying and giving to the regulator all of the information relevant to that determination. <!-- MX-102 -->

The people whose job it was to tell the regulator what had changed were the people for whom telling the regulator what had changed was, in their own words, a huge threat.

So how does a change like that go unmentioned?

## ACT_3 — THE ANATOMY OF A THING NOBODY HAD TO MENTION

Before the failures, the reasons. This was not built by people who wanted an aeroplane to crash.

Start with what MCAS read.

An angle-of-attack sensor is a small vane on the side of the nose that turns in the airflow and reports the angle at which the aeroplane is meeting the air. The 737 has two of them, one on each side.

MCAS listened to one. <!-- MX-614 -->

Not one at a time. One. In 2015, one of Boeing's own authorised representatives asked the question out loud — whether MCAS was vulnerable to single sensor failures. The aeroplane shipped that way anyway. <!-- MX-614 -->

Then, in March of 2016, one year before the FAA certified the aeroplane, Boeing test pilots found the MAX did not handle well approaching a stall at lower speeds — and Boeing redesigned MCAS to work down there too. <!-- MX-615 -->

Two things changed in that redesign, and both matter.

The first is where it could act. When the aeroplane registered a high angle of attack, the change expanded the range in which MCAS could fire from roughly Mach zero point six to zero point eight — the high-speed corner — to roughly Mach zero point two to zero point eight. Which is nearly the entire speed range of the aeroplane. <!-- MX-106 -->

That includes the speed at which you take off. That includes the speed at which two hundred people are still fastening their seatbelts.

The second is how hard it could push. As originally designed, MCAS could move the horizontal stabiliser a maximum of zero point six degrees. The redesigned version could move it two point five. <!-- MX-615 -->

More than four times the authority, on a system that listened to one vane, in the part of the flight closest to the ground.

Hours after that redesign was approved, Boeing asked the FAA to remove the references to MCAS from the Flight Crew Operations Manual — the book in the cockpit. The FAA approved it. <!-- MX-616 -->

And the officials who approved that removal remained unaware of the redesign until after the Lion Air crash. <!-- MX-616 -->

Not misled about it, in that moment. Simply not told. They said yes to taking a name out of a book, without knowing the thing behind the name had just become four times stronger and could now fire almost anywhere.

So the aeroplane went to the airlines. And the pilots who flew it for Boeing's customers were provided no information about MCAS in their manuals or their training materials. Not a paragraph. Not a line. <!-- MX-111 -->

The pilots were never told the system existed. <!-- MX-111 -->

So did anybody inside the company notice what had been left out?

Somebody did. Two people, in fact, and this is the part the company later admitted, in a document it signed, in a federal court.

On the fifteenth of November, 2016, one of Boeing's two 737 MAX technical pilots was flying the simulator, and he felt MCAS operate at low speed — and recognised that this was different from what Boeing had briefed and described to the regulator. <!-- MX-107 -->

That same day, he messaged his colleague. The messages are quoted in the agreement, word for word.

Oh shocker alert. MCAS is now active down to point two. It's running rampant in the sim on me. <!-- MX-108 -->

The colleague replied that this meant they had to update the speed trim description in volume two.

And then: so I basically lied to the regulators — unknowingly. <!-- MX-108 -->

It wasn't a lie, the colleague wrote back. No one told us that was the case. <!-- MX-108 -->

Listen to that exchange as what it is: two employees discovering, in real time, that a thing they said in good faith has become untrue.

The word unknowingly is doing a great deal of work in that sentence. Their own company's agreement records what happened next: from that point on, both men understood that the regulator was under the misimpression that MCAS could only fire in a high-speed turn. <!-- MX-109 -->

And the group was left in that misimpression. The agreement uses a hard verb for it. It says the two men deceived the Aircraft Evaluation Group into believing that the basis on which it had agreed to remove MCAS from the report still held. <!-- MX-110 -->

Two weeks after the first crash, an FAA employee was told by one of the same men that he had been previously unaware of MCAS's expanded scope — and was otherwise misled about what he had known. <!-- MX-112 -->

And around the same time, Boeing put a presentation in front of the Aircraft Evaluation Group. It said that during the training evaluation, Boeing and the group had discussed and agreed on the removal of MCAS from the report. <!-- MX-113 -->

Which is true, as a sentence about a meeting. The agreement calls the presentation misleading anyway, and says exactly why: the chat was not disclosed, and the group had been deprived of relevant information about MCAS. <!-- MX-113 -->

That is a precise and useful distinction, and it is the one this film keeps coming back to. The words were accurate. The impression was false. Nothing in the sentence had to be a lie for the people reading it to end up believing something untrue.

And there is one sentence in that agreement that the company wrote about itself, and it is the reason this film can use the word it uses. From at least around November 2016 through at least around December 2018, the agreement says, Boeing — through those two employees — knowingly, and with intent to defraud, conspired to defraud the Aircraft Evaluation Group. <!-- MX-114 -->

By the end of 2016, Boeing knew what the 737 MAX could do at low speed, and the Aircraft Evaluation Group did not. <!-- MX-114, MX-109 -->

Boeing knew. That is not a jury's word for it. It is the company's own, in a signed agreement, filed in a federal court. <!-- MX-114 -->

The two men, the agreement adds, were acting within the scope of their employment, with the intention, at least in part, to benefit Boeing. <!-- MX-115 -->

And there is one more institution in this chain, quieter than the rest.

The sensor that lied to the Lion Air crew was a replacement. It had been mis-calibrated during an earlier repair, and the mis-calibration was not detected during that repair. <!-- MX-701 -->

Somebody bench-tested a part, and the test passed, and the part was wrong by twenty-one degrees. <!-- MX-701, MX-702 -->

The Indonesian investigators did not write a single cause for this accident. They wrote nine contributing factors, and they read like a list of separate rooms in which nobody could see the next room. Assumptions about how a crew would respond, consistent with the industry's own guidelines, that turned out to be incorrect. A system relying on one sensor. An alert that would have told the pilots the two sensors disagreed — which was not correctly enabled. The repair nobody caught. A logbook that did not record what the previous crew had lived through. And multiple alarms, repeated activations and radio calls that could not be effectively managed at once. <!-- MX-701 -->

Nine rooms. No villain in any of them.

There is a temptation here, and this programme is going to decline it.

The temptation is to make these two men the story. They are the smallest people in it. One of them was later tried, and a federal jury acquitted him on all four counts in less than two hours, and he is not named here, and nothing in this film says he lied. <!-- MX-405 -->

The company was charged. Not the engineers who moved a limit from zero point six to two point five. Not the managers who set the directive that there were to be no differences. Not the regulator who signed where the manufacturer pointed.

And about that regulator, one number tells you the shape of the thing. In 2013 the FAA delegated twenty-eight of eighty-seven certification tasks to Boeing itself. By November of 2016 — four months before the MAX was certified — it was seventy-nine of ninety-one. <!-- MX-619 -->

The Committee that investigated it put the shape into a sentence: career FAA officials documented cases where FAA management overruled the determination of the FAA's own technical experts at the request of Boeing. <!-- MX-618 -->

So after the first crash, with the sensor known and the software known and the wreckage counted — what did the people who could ground this aeroplane actually do?

## ACT_4 — FIVE MONTHS

They did the arithmetic. That is the part almost nobody knows.

On the seventh of November, 2018, nine days after Lion Air, the FAA issued an emergency airworthiness directive. It told operators what to do if the trim ran away. <!-- MX-607 -->

It did not mention MCAS. <!-- MX-607 -->

Not once, by name. Boeing's own bulletin, the day before, had not mentioned it either. <!-- MX-607 -->

The agency's officials later said that at the time, they were unaware of the full details of MCAS. <!-- MX-608 -->

So the world's 737 MAX pilots were handed a procedure for a symptom, and were not given the name of the cause.

Then, on the third of December, 2018, the FAA finished a risk analysis on its own aeroplane.

The method has a name that sounds like nothing: Transport Airplane Risk Assessment Methodology. What it does is put a number on how many people a known defect will kill if you leave it alone.

The number it produced is the reason this film exists.

Even with the emergency directive in place, but without a fix to MCAS, the analysis indicated there could be more than fifteen fatal 737 MAX crashes over the estimated thirty-year lifetime of the fleet — a fleet then projected at four thousand eight hundred aircraft — resulting in over two thousand nine hundred deaths. <!-- MX-602 -->

The Committee translated it into the sentence a person can hold. Statistically, the FAA was predicting one fatal 737 MAX accident every two years, for thirty years. <!-- MX-603 -->

And here, this programme has to stop and be careful, because the easy version of this story is wrong.

That analysis did not say: ground the aeroplane.

The Inspector General's office, looking at the same document later, recorded that even without the directive, the analysis still would not have recommended grounding — because the calculated individual risk, two point six eight fatalities per million flight hours, remained below the guideline the method itself uses, of one fatality per hundred thousand flight hours. <!-- MX-609 -->

Read those two numbers together, because they are the whole of modern safety regulation in one line. Fifteen crashes. Below the threshold.

The Inspector General added something else, and it is fair to the people who wrote the analysis: these figures, the office said, should not be viewed as predictive values. <!-- MX-610 -->

They were not a prophecy. They were a machine for comparing risks.

The machine compared the risk to the rule, and the rule said: keep flying.

And so, despite the analysis, the FAA permitted the 737 MAX to continue flying. <!-- MX-605 -->

In those five months, Boeing delivered nearly one hundred and fifty more aircraft to its customers, and the global fleet grew to three hundred and eighty-seven. <!-- MX-606 -->

So what happens to the next crew, the one that has been told everything the world knows?

On the tenth of March, 2019, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 lifted off from Addis Ababa at thirty-four minutes and thirty-four seconds past five in the morning. <!-- MX-802 -->

Ten seconds later, the left sensor began to lie. <!-- MX-802 -->

This crew knew more than the crew before them. They had the bulletin. They had the directive.

And they did the procedure.

On the recording, the first officer says it twice: stab trim cut out. The captain answers, yes, yes, do it. <!-- MX-803 -->

The switches went to cut-out at about forty minutes and thirty-eight seconds past five. Five seconds later, the system commanded the nose down again — and the stabiliser did not move, because they had cut its power. <!-- MX-803 -->

They had beaten it.

And then they could not fly.

With the electric trim dead, the stabiliser has to be turned by hand, with the wheel beside the knee, and at that speed, with the aeroplane already trimmed against them, two people could not move it.

At about eleven minutes past five forty-three, they put the switches back. It is the decision anybody would make. It is the only way to get the trim to move at all.

About five seconds after their last manual input, the automatic nose-down came one final time. <!-- MX-804 -->

The aeroplane hit the ground twenty-eight nautical miles south-east of Addis Ababa, near Ejere, at five forty-four. All one hundred and fifty-seven people aboard died. <!-- MX-802 -->

Six minutes. <!-- MX-802 -->

Three days later, on the thirteenth of March, the 737 MAX was grounded in the United States — the FAA's first grounding of a transport aeroplane fleet since the 787 in 2013. <!-- MX-620, MX-003 -->

And now the record splits, and this programme will not pretend it doesn't.

Ethiopia's investigators concluded that the repetitive, uncommanded nose-down inputs from MCAS, from an erroneous sensor, and what they called its unrecoverable activation system, were the most probable cause. <!-- MX-801 -->

The American investigators disagreed — in writing. The National Transportation Safety Board said it agreed MCAS should be part of the probable cause, but that the draft indicated MCAS alone made the aeroplane unrecoverable, and that the cause also needed to acknowledge that appropriate crew management of the event, using the procedures that existed at the time, would have allowed the crew to recover. <!-- MX-805 -->

They went further than a disagreement about emphasis. They asked for two specific things to be added as causal: the flight crew's inadequate use of manual electric trim, and their management of thrust. And they asked for a contributing factor about the operator — that it had failed to ensure its crews were prepared to respond properly to uncommanded stabiliser movement, in line with the bulletin and the emergency directive. <!-- MX-805 -->

They also disputed how the sensor broke in the first place. Where the Ethiopian report reached for an electrical explanation, the Board wrote that the erroneous signals were caused by the separation of the sensor vane after an impact with a foreign object — most likely a bird. <!-- MX-806 -->

Those comments were not appended to the final report. <!-- MX-806 -->

Two national investigation authorities, looking at the same recorder data, wrote down two different endings. One of them was published and one of them was not. This film is not going to pick.

It is worth being exact about what the disagreement is, because it is not a technicality. One report says the aeroplane could not be recovered. The other says it could have been, by the book that existed. If the second is right, then the crew of the flight before — the one that landed — is the proof. And if the first is right, then that crew was lucky, and the book was never enough.

When the Committee finished, eighteen months after the second crash, it did not name a culprit either. It named five themes, and they are worth hearing in the order it wrote them. <!-- MX-617 -->

Production pressures. Faulty design and performance assumptions. A culture of concealment. Conflicted representation. And Boeing's influence over the FAA's oversight structure. <!-- MX-617 -->

Not one of those is a person. Every one of them is a condition — the kind of thing that is nobody's job to fix, because it is not on anybody's list.

So what happened to the company when this became the record?

On the seventh of January, 2021, Boeing signed an agreement with the United States Department of Justice.

It agreed to pay two billion five hundred and thirteen million six hundred thousand dollars, in three parts. <!-- MX-202 -->

A criminal penalty of two hundred and forty-three million six hundred thousand. One billion seven hundred and seventy million in compensation to its airline customers. And five hundred million to the heirs, relatives and legal beneficiaries of the people who died on the two flights. <!-- MX-202 -->

Look at the shape of that. The airlines received more than three times what the families received.

And the criminal penalty was not chosen from the air. The agreement states what it represents: Boeing's own assessment of the cost of implementing full-flight simulator training for the 737 MAX. <!-- MX-203 -->

The fine was the price of the training. The exact thing the whole design had been arranged to avoid was, in the end, the number written on the invoice.

The charge was deferred, not tried. Then, in May of 2024, the government told the court that Boeing had breached the agreement — by failing to design, implement and enforce a compliance and ethics programme to prevent and detect violations of United States fraud laws throughout its operations. <!-- MX-301 -->

In July, the parties brought a guilty plea. In December, the court rejected it. <!-- MX-302, MX-303 -->

In May of 2025 the government moved to dismiss the case entirely, having made a new agreement: one point one billion dollars, and no prosecution. <!-- MX-304, MX-305 -->

The government's own words for it were that the agreement secures meaningful accountability, delivers substantial and immediate public benefits, and brings finality to a difficult and complex case whose outcome would otherwise be uncertain. <!-- MX-306 -->

The families objected. Their objection is on the record and it is a technical one, which is what makes it land. They said the new agreement was unenforceable, because the statute of limitations had run — meaning that even if Boeing breached this one, the government could no longer bring the charge back. <!-- MX-307 -->

They said it exempted Boeing from independent monitoring of its own compliance and safety work. <!-- MX-307 -->

And there is one more thing in this file, from earlier, that belongs here.

Back in February of 2023, when the families first asked a court to set aside the original agreement, the judge found something in their favour. He found that the Department of Justice had violated their rights under the victims' rights law — by denying them the right to confer before it made its deal with Boeing. <!-- MX-313 -->

He also found it had not done so in bad faith, and he let the agreement stand. <!-- MX-313 -->

That shape has appeared in this film before.

An agreement was reached about something that had happened to them. The people it had happened to were not told until it was done. And a court looked at that and said: yes, that happened, and no, it does not change anything.

On the sixth of November, 2025, the court granted the motion. The case was dismissed. <!-- MX-308 -->

The families went to the Fifth Circuit. On the thirty-first of March, 2026, that court denied them, holding that it lacked jurisdiction to review the dismissal substantively — and declining to read into the victims' rights law an unlimited right for victims to appeal the dismissal of a criminal prosecution, because non-parties have no judicially cognizable interest in the prosecution of another. <!-- MX-309, MX-311 -->

On the twenty-second of May, 2026, rehearing was denied. No judge asked for a vote. <!-- MX-312 -->

That is where the record stops.

Three hundred and forty-six people died, and one rescue diver died recovering them from the Java Sea. <!-- MX-622 -->

Nobody was convicted of anything.

## ENDING

Go back to the wheel.

It sits beside the pilot's right knee, and it has been in that spot, in that shape, on this aeroplane's family since before most of the people in this story were born. When the trim moves, it turns. It is the aeroplane telling you what it is doing.

On the flight before, it turned by itself, and a captain named what it was and cut the power to it, and turned it by hand for an hour, and landed. <!-- MX-707 -->

On the next morning, it turned by itself more than twenty times, and two men turned it back, and nobody in that cockpit had the word for what they were fighting. <!-- MX-704, MX-111 -->

Five months later, two more men had the word, and cut the power exactly as the procedure said — and found they could not move the wheel by hand at all. <!-- MX-803 -->

⟨HELD⟩

The whole point of the system was that it should not need to be mentioned.

That is not a metaphor. That is the design goal, in a contract, with a price in it. Make the new aeroplane feel like the old one, so that no one has to be told anything, so that no one has to sit in a simulator, so that the fleet stays one fleet. <!-- MX-612 -->

And it worked. Nobody was told. <!-- MX-111 -->

⟨HELD⟩

You will get on one of these. They are flying now, everywhere, and they have been fixed, and they are — by every measure anyone applies — extremely safe. <!-- MX-621 -->

The thing to carry off this aeroplane is not fear of it.

It is the sentence that was true in the Java Sea and true over Ejere and true in a courthouse in Fort Worth, and that nobody in any of those places said out loud.

Somebody, somewhere, decided you did not need to know.
