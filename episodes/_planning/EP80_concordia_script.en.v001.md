A cruise ship is a building that floats, and the thing that tells you a building is in trouble is the floor. <!-- CC-025 -->

On a January night off the Italian coast, four thousand two hundred and twenty-nine people felt a floor stop being level. <!-- CC-002 -->

## ACT_1 - FAVOURABLE CONDITIONS
Start with the weather, because it removes an excuse before anyone can reach for it.

The sea and the weather that night were favourable. <!-- CC-003 -->

No storm. No fog. No wind. Whatever happened here, the weather did not do it.

It is the thirteenth of January, 2012. The Costa Concordia is on a weekly Mediterranean cruise, working her way round a circuit of ports, picking people up and setting them down as she goes. <!-- CC-001 -->

There are four thousand two hundred and twenty-nine people on board. Three thousand two hundred and six passengers. One thousand and twenty-three crew. <!-- CC-002 -->

Hold that second number. A thousand crew is not a skeleton staff. It is a workforce the size of a factory.

Now the number that decides this film.

Of the three thousand two hundred and six passengers, one thousand two hundred and seventy had attended a muster. <!-- CC-004 -->

A muster is the drill. You put on the orange jacket, walk to a marked point on a specific deck, and stand there while somebody counts you. It exists so that on the worst night, your legs already know where to go.

One thousand two hundred and seventy had done it. Those were the people who had joined the ship at Savona, at the start of the circuit. <!-- CC-004 -->

Everyone else received their safety instructions by video. <!-- CC-004 -->

Four hundred and sixty-three of them at Toulon. Four hundred and fifty-six at Barcelona. More at the ports after that. <!-- CC-005 -->

That is not a scandal, and it matters to say why first. A ship on a circuit takes passengers on at every stop. You cannot run a full muster daily for a few hundred arrivals without emptying the decks of everyone else, and the rules of the time allowed the briefing another way.

The arrangement was legal, ordinary, and used by the whole industry.

It also meant that on the night this happened, nearly two thousand people on that ship had never once walked to their muster station.

The ship is running south of Giglio Island. At four minutes and five seconds past nine forty-four, she is three tenths of a mile from a set of rocks called Le Scole, making sixteen knots. <!-- CC-011 -->

And she is half a mile south-west of her planned route - much closer to the coast than the plan says. <!-- CC-011 -->

Half a mile. On a chart, a finger's width. On the water, at sixteen knots, under two minutes.

At seven seconds past nine forty-five, the hull strikes the rock furthest east of Le Scole, on the left side. <!-- CC-012 -->

Fifty-one seconds later, the recorder captures the blackout. The rudder and the bilge pumps lose power. <!-- CC-013 -->

Seven seconds after that, the emergency generator starts.

It runs for forty-one seconds. <!-- CC-014 -->

Then it stops being able to supply the ship's essential services continuously - and the two essential services named in the report are the rudder and the bilge pump. <!-- CC-014 -->

The rudder is how you steer. The bilge pump is how you get water out.

Both of them, gone, inside a minute and a half.

The master gives rudder orders at forty-three and forty-six seconds past nine forty-six. The report states plainly that those orders would have had no effect even if they were executed. <!-- CC-015 -->

There is nothing to execute them with.

And that is not bad luck. It is geometry.

A ship this size is divided across its length into watertight compartments, and the machinery is distributed among them. Compartment five held the propulsion electric motors, and the fire and bilge pumps. Compartment six held three of the main diesel generators. <!-- CC-404 -->

Those are the two the rock opened. <!-- CC-108 -->

The pumps and the generators were in the compartments that filled.

So the water came in through the hole, and the two things that could have fought the water - the power and the pumping - were standing in the water.

The size of the hole was not known that night. It was worked out in the days afterwards.

Fifty-three metres. <!-- CC-403 -->

Longer than the wingspan of an airliner, opened along the side of the hull in a few seconds.

## ACT_2 - WHAT A MUSTER IS FOR
Go back to the drill, because everything that follows runs through it.

A muster station is not a safety theatre. It is a piece of engineering that happens to be made of people.

Here is what it does. It takes several thousand strangers spread across fifteen decks, and converts them into countable groups standing in known places, next to the boats.

That conversion takes time. That is the point of it. You do the walking in advance, in daylight, so that the walking is already done when the lights go out.

And a ship the size of this one has muster stations that the investigators describe as capacious and suitable - big enough, and in the right places. <!-- CC-202 -->

The infrastructure was there. The rehearsal, for most of the people aboard, was not.

Now put the two facts beside each other.

Nearly two thousand passengers had watched a video. <!-- CC-004, CC-005 -->

And the ship had lost all electrical power to its essential systems inside ninety seconds. <!-- CC-013, CC-014 -->

What happens to a video briefing in a corridor with no lights in it?

The report answers this, and its answer is the bluntest passage in the whole document. Passengers were not directed to muster stations according to procedure. There was chaos and confusion, and a lack of communication. In the report's own words: a complete disorganisation, mainly because nobody on the bridge coordinated the emergency. <!-- CC-106 -->

That sentence names the failure precisely, and notice where it puts it. Not on the passengers. Not on the crew on the decks. On the coordination.

Because the same paragraph contains the other half, and any film that leaves this out is not telling you the truth.

Passengers testified that despite the chaos, and despite how little they had been prepared, crew members supported them with humanity and effective action - and ferried them to the boats and off the ship. <!-- CC-107 -->

A thousand crew, most of them with no instruction coming from anywhere, improvised an evacuation. And the people they carried off said so afterwards, on the record, to investigators.

That is not the story this accident is usually told as.

Two hundred and seventy-two of that thousand had joined the ship since the first of December. A quarter of the workforce, turned over in six weeks. Twelve of them had embarked that same day. <!-- CC-401, CC-402 -->

And the investigators looked hard at that, and then set it aside.

The turnover was almost entirely in the hotel and service departments. Very few key personnel were new. The Body's conclusion is that the replacements are to be considered irrelevant to how the crew performed. <!-- CC-807 -->

That finding is worth respecting, because it closes off an easy answer. This was not a green crew.

The Body's own assessment is the opposite. Taken as a whole, it says, the crew gave an adequate performance in the core phase of the evacuation - moving passengers off the ship until about four in the morning, with the vessel listed at eighty degrees, in conditions it describes as very dangerous. <!-- CC-808 -->

Four in the morning. At eighty degrees.

So if the people were not the problem, look at what was reaching them.

There is a rule that the company operating a ship must establish a language of work, so that safety performance is effective. <!-- CC-405 -->

Somebody wrote that rule because somebody, once, could not be understood.

The crew of this ship were of thirty-eight different nationalities. The passengers came from twenty-six countries. <!-- CC-501 -->

The company had chosen Italian. <!-- CC-502 -->

The Body is careful here, and its care is the point. Formally, it says, the choice is not open to censure - the ship was Italian-flagged and the paperwork had to be in Italian. But it also says that choosing a more widespread international language would have given, in its words, concrete advantages, above all in situations where understanding each other is of fundamental importance. <!-- CC-503 -->

Then it lists what happened.

The safety trainer testified that in practice both Italian and English were used, and that training was usually carried out in English. <!-- CC-504 -->

Many American passengers testified that during the emergency, several crew members on duty at the muster stations could not speak English. <!-- CC-505 -->

And on the bridge, in the last phases, it was proved that the helmsman did not understand the master's orders in English. Twice. <!-- CC-506 -->

There is one more finding about language and it is administrative, which is why it is easy to skip.

When the flag authority audited the company in March 2012, it recorded a formal non-conformity: the recruitment procedure for deck and engine personnel does not assess the applicant's work language. Nor does the procedure for the service departments. <!-- CC-507 -->

The company had chosen a language of work, and had no step anywhere in its hiring that checked whether the person could speak it.

So what is a muster list worth, in a corridor where the person holding it and the person reading it do not share a language?

It is worth something. Just not what the paperwork says.

That is the pattern here. Nothing is absent. Everything is thinner than it looks.

There was a language of work. Nobody tested for it.

There was a muster list. Some assignments were inconsistent.

There were muster stations, big ones, correctly placed. Most passengers had never walked to them.

There was a drill schedule. The master had written to say the drills were getting worse.

Every one of those exists on paper. Not one of them had ever been pulled on hard enough to find out what it would hold.

The investigators also went through the crew certification, the muster list and the familiarisation training, and found inconsistencies in the assignment of duties to some crew members. <!-- CC-104 -->

A muster list is the document that says who does what in an emergency. Which crew member takes which station. Which boat. Which stairwell.

It is the whole plan, on paper, in advance.

And it only works one way. Somebody has to start it.

If the assignments are inconsistent, then on the night, two people think somebody else has the job.

And the report records the consequence of no orders arriving: the lack of direct orders from the bridge left the initiative to individuals, and the different backgrounds and basic training among the crew may have played a part in how the emergency was handled. <!-- CC-105 -->

Read that carefully. It is not saying the crew were untrained. It is saying that when nothing comes down from the bridge, a thousand people fall back on their own judgement - and a thousand separate judgements is not an evacuation plan.

And someone had said so, in writing, three months earlier.

On the fifteenth of October, 2011, this ship held an abandon-ship drill. Afterwards, the master warned the company that his crew's performance was decreasing, and that the drill had shown critical findings. <!-- CC-601 -->

The safety trainer, when he joined, found the same thing from a different direction: the crew's training was unaligned, and there was no evidence on board of the training the company's own safety-management procedure required. He told his superiors. The master told him the company had asked the ship's management to improve the programme. <!-- CC-602 -->

So the warning existed, it came from the top of the ship, and it went to the shore.

Now the other half of the training question, which is about the bridge rather than the decks.

There is a course called Bridge Resource Management. Its entire subject is the problem the investigators would later describe on page eleven of their report - how a bridge team is supposed to speak up, challenge, and cross-check the person in command.

The master of this ship had never attended it. Not the junior version, not the senior version, not the one for staff masters. <!-- CC-603 -->

That is not a rule he broke. He held the passenger-ship certificate the regulations required, obtained in 2008, and he had nearly seventeen years of sea service behind him. <!-- CC-603 -->

The course simply was not mandatory.

Several of his officers had taken it - a staff master in 2010, and the chief mate, the safety officer, a second officer and two third officers during 2011, at the group's training centre in Amsterdam. <!-- CC-604 -->

The people trained to challenge were on the bridge. The person they would have been challenging had not taken the course.

From the first of September 2012 - eight months after this night - the parent corporation made that course mandatory, along with electronic chart training, ship handling and stability. The master and the staff master have to attend all of them. <!-- CC-605 -->

## ACT_3 - SEVENTY-FIVE MINUTES
Here is the clock. Every time in it comes off the ship's own recorder.

Nine forty-five and seven seconds: the contact. <!-- CC-012 -->

At five to ten - ten minutes after the contact - compartments four, five, six and seven are ascertained as flooded.

No distress message has been sent. No securite message either. <!-- CC-701 -->

At thirty-four seconds past nine fifty-seven, the master informs the company's designated person ashore, who leaves for the company building to form a crisis team. <!-- CC-702 -->

So the shore knows something. Which shore, and what they were told, is the whole of the next twenty minutes.

At seven minutes past ten the rescue organisation at Civitavecchia contacts the ship. The ship tells them about the black out, and that it is managing to solve the failure. <!-- CC-703 -->

Only the black out.

What does a rescue centre do with that?

It does what it did. It notes a ship with an electrical fault, off an island, in calm weather, and it waits.

The investigators put a phrase in their own table against that line: very serious scenario is held back. <!-- CC-703 -->

At eleven minutes past ten the ship is adrift towards the coastline. <!-- CC-704 -->

At twelve minutes past ten, the public address makes its first announcement to the passengers. It tells them to go to the lounges. <!-- CC-705 -->

Not the muster stations. The lounges.

And the report records what actually happened next, which is the strangest line in the document: crew and passengers moved towards the muster stations by themselves. Without an order. <!-- CC-705 -->

Several thousand people, most of whom had only watched a video, worked out on their own where they were supposed to be.

Ten past ten, near enough: the maritime rescue coordination centre at Livorno is contacted. <!-- CC-016 -->

Twenty-nine minutes after the rocks.

At fourteen minutes past ten, Livorno finds the ship on its own tracking system. The ship tells the rescue centre that it has had a blackout for about twenty minutes.

And makes no request for assistance. <!-- CC-017 -->

Stay with that one. A shore station with helicopters and patrol boats has a cruise ship on the screen, has it on the radio, and is told: we have had a power failure.

Not: we have struck rocks. Not: we are taking water. Not: we have four thousand people on board and I need everything you have.

At twenty-five minutes and fifteen seconds past ten, contacted again, the master tells Livorno there is a hull breach on the left side causing a gradual heel. <!-- CC-018 -->

That is forty minutes after the contact, and it is the first time the shore hears the word breach.

At twenty-two minutes past ten the ship asks for the help of two tugs. <!-- CC-707 -->

Four minutes later, the chief mate tells the rescue authority, for the first time, that there is a breach and that the ship is flooding. <!-- CC-708 -->

The Body's comment on that line in its own table is the harshest sentence in the report, and it is aimed at the delay rather than at any person: this was an evident and very serious untruth, because the breach had been known since twenty-three seconds past nine forty-nine. <!-- CC-708 -->

Known on board for thirty-seven minutes. Told to the shore at twenty-six minutes past ten.

Why would anybody hold that back?

The report does not say, and this programme will not guess. But it is worth knowing what the word costs.

Declaring distress is not a phone call. It is a legal act.

It hands the situation to the shore. It launches helicopters, patrol boats, tugs, and other ships pulled off their routes. It becomes an incident with a file attached.

So there is a pull, in the first minutes of anything, towards finding out a bit more before you say it. Towards solving it yourself.

That pull is not unique to ships. It is in every building that has ever had an alarm somebody hesitated to break.

Here it lasted fifty-one minutes.

At half past ten the flooding reaches Deck Zero, the watertight deck. And passengers begin boarding the lifeboats by themselves. <!-- CC-709 -->

At twenty-six seconds past ten thirty-three, the general emergency is announced. <!-- CC-710 -->

Forty-eight minutes after the contact. <!-- CC-710 -->

Three minutes after that comes something that sounds like an order to abandon ship. It is not one. The words were never spoken. What the announcement actually said was to reach the muster stations calmly and follow the crew's instructions. <!-- CC-711 -->

At thirty-six minutes and thirty-four seconds past ten, Livorno calls again. The bridge says the heeling is increasing.

And only then, after the rescue centre presses, does the ship declare that it is in distress. <!-- CC-019 -->

Fifty-one minutes.

In that same call the bridge gives the number of people on board as three thousand two hundred and eight passengers. The true figure was three thousand two hundred and six. And it was different again from the figure the ship had given on departure from Civitavecchia, which was three thousand two hundred and sixteen. <!-- CC-020 -->

Three numbers for the same people. On the night that a shore station needs to know how many to look for.

Sixty-nine minutes after the hull struck the rock.

It was not preceded by an effective general emergency alarm. Several passengers testified that they never caught the signal, or the voice announcement. <!-- CC-022 -->

## ACT_4 - WHAT THE INVESTIGATORS WORKED OUT
Now the physics, because it is the only part of this night that behaved exactly as designed.

A large ship is divided into watertight compartments, and the rule she is built to is that she must survive a given number of them flooding. Beyond that number, no ship of this kind can be made unsinkable. The investigative body states it directly: no measure is sufficient to render the ship unsinkable when more than two contiguous watertight compartments are flooded. <!-- CC-109 -->

The rock opened compartments five and six. Two contiguous compartments, violently flooded - compartment five filled completely in a very short time. <!-- CC-108 -->

So the ship was going to end up on her side. That was decided at the moment of contact and nothing on board could have changed it.

Which raises the only question that matters about the rest of that night. If the ship could not be saved, what could?

The answer is the people, and the answer is arithmetic.

A ship going over is not a ship going down. It leans, and it leans slowly.

At thirteen degrees you can still walk, badly.

At thirty the decks are ramps.

At forty, which is where she was around midnight, walking is climbing. <!-- CC-025 -->

At eighty, where she ended, the corridors are shafts. <!-- CC-025 -->

Everything had to happen in the early part of that scale.

But here is the thing about a ship that is going over rather than going down.

It takes time.

The heel is gradual. The master describes it that way himself to the rescue centre, forty minutes in. <!-- CC-018 -->

And that gradual heel is the resource. It is the only resource anybody had that night, and there was more of it than anyone used.

Which is exactly what the investigators went and calculated.

They ran the alternative timeline. Suppose the general emergency signal had been given at thirty-nine minutes past ten - the point at which the ship grounded a second time, and the first rescue vessel reached her. Suppose the passengers had been gathered and ready at the boats, waiting for the order. <!-- CC-201, CC-306 -->

On that timeline the abandon ship would have followed thirty-six minutes after the signal - which the investigators say is adequate to gather everybody into muster stations they describe as capacious and suitable. <!-- CC-202 -->

Passengers would have started leaving the ship while she had thirteen degrees of heel. <!-- CC-203 -->

And thirty-three minutes after that, when the ship reached thirty degrees, at twelve minutes past eleven - all of them could have reached safety. <!-- CC-204 -->

From the same position. With the ship already stopped. Where thirty-two people instead died. <!-- CC-204 -->

That is not a campaigner's claim. That is the official investigation, writing down in its own report that everybody could have got off.

And it names how those thirty-two died: most were trapped, and the others were thrown into the sea. <!-- CC-205 -->

Thirty people were found. Two were never found - one passenger, one crew member. Twenty-six passengers and four crew are recorded as deceased. The last was found on the twenty-second of March. <!-- CC-301, CC-302, CC-304 -->

A hundred and fifty-seven passengers claimed injuries. <!-- CC-303 -->

Two more findings, and they belong together because they are the same failure seen from both ends.

Below decks, the engine and electrical departments worked the emergency from the first minute. Under the chief engineer they fed the bridge a continuous, detailed, systematically updated picture of the flooding, the propulsion and the emergency power. The chief engineer spoke to the master directly, several times. <!-- CC-802 -->

They went into flooded compartments to try to restore vital equipment, at serious risk to themselves, and it did not work. And they stayed on the bulkhead deck after the water reached it - knowing the ship was lost - and left only when the bridge cleared them to go. <!-- CC-803 -->

That is the information arriving.

Now what was leaving.

Some crew members, meeting passengers in the corridors, told them to go back to their cabins. That it was only a black out. That it would be sorted out soon. <!-- CC-804 -->

They were not inventing that. It is what the ship had told the rescue services twice.

Passengers were not gathered from their cabins, as the procedure requires. And the investigators offer one piece of proof for that which does not need any interpretation at all.

Two passengers were found alive on deck eight, three days later. <!-- CC-805 -->

Deck eight has no lounges and no public rooms. It is cabins.

The passengers also testified to something that explains why a cabin was the worst place to be. The public spaces stayed lit throughout by the emergency lighting. The cabins were completely dark. People found their lifejackets in the wardrobe by the light of a torch, or a mobile phone. <!-- CC-806 -->

And one last absence, recorded flatly.

Nobody called the roll. Nobody attempted to. Nobody counted the passengers as they boarded the lifeboats and the rafts. <!-- CC-809 -->

Not one of the several thousand people who left that ship was counted leaving it.

The Body explains why, and its explanation is not about character. The lack of orders in accordance with the muster list disoriented the crew who had been assigned by that same muster list. <!-- CC-810 -->

They had been given jobs by a document, and then never told to start.

⟨HELD⟩

So what does the report conclude, at the top, in one line?

That the human element is the root cause of the Costa Concordia casualty - in the action that caused the contact, and in the management of the emergency afterwards. <!-- CC-101 -->

That sentence is going to be read, by most people who read it, as being about one man on a bridge.

An honest film has to say what else is in the same document.

On the bridge team, the investigators write: nobody seemed to have urged the master to accelerate the turn, or to give warning of the looming danger. And they say the accident may lead to a wider discussion about the adequacy of bridge teams - how they are organised, and what the people standing on them are actually for. <!-- CC-102 -->

That is not one man. That is a room.

On the alarm: the general emergency alarm was not activated immediately after the impact, and that delayed the management of everything that came after it - the flooding, and the abandon ship process. <!-- CC-103 -->

That is not one man either. That is a procedure that did not start.

And underneath all of it: nearly two thousand people who had never walked to a muster station, because the operation they had bought a ticket from showed them a video instead. <!-- CC-004 -->

The human element is the root cause. It is just not one human.

## ENDING
Go back to the floor.

It is level. It is carpeted, and warm, and there are several thousand people standing on it who have no reason to look down. <!-- CC-002 -->

That is what a ship feels like when everything is correct. It feels like a building.

What was left was time, and there was more of it than the night needed. Seventy-five minutes to the order. Another twenty-two before the heel reached thirty degrees. <!-- CC-021, CC-204 -->

Thirteen degrees. Thirty. Forty. Eighty. <!-- CC-203, CC-204, CC-025 -->

Somewhere in the middle of that, the investigators say, every person aboard could have been standing on a rock in the Tyrrhenian Sea, cold and frightened and alive. <!-- CC-204 -->

They needed a sound.

Given once. In the first minutes. On a system that reaches every room.

And a walk that most of them had never taken.

Tonight, on ships all over the world, several thousand people at a time put on an orange jacket and stand in a marked square on a deck, feeling faintly silly, while somebody counts them.

That is the whole difference.
