## ACT_1 — THE MORNING THE WALLS WENT

Everyone remembers the hurricane. Almost nobody remembers the arithmetic.

New Orleans sits lower than the water around it, and it stays dry because of a ring — earth banks, concrete walls, pumps — about three hundred and fifty miles of protective structures, fifty-six miles of which are floodwalls. Most of those floodwalls are a type called an I-wall: a slab of concrete standing on a line of steel sheet piling driven down into the earth bank beneath it. <!-- KT-405 -->

Think of a fence post in a lawn. The part you see is the wall. The part you do not see is what holds it.

On the twenty-ninth of August, 2005, the water came up. And what happened next divides cleanly into two stories that get told as one, and should not be.

Story one is the story of the walls being beaten. On the Industrial Canal, on the east side of the city, the surge peaked at fourteen point two feet — about one foot eight inches above the top of the floodwalls and levees there. Water went over the top, poured down the back of the concrete, dug out the earth behind it, and the walls fell backwards into the hole they had made. <!-- KT-509 -->

That is a system meeting more than it was built for. It is a bad day. It is not a scandal.

Story two happened at the drainage canals, and it is a different thing entirely.

The 17th Street Canal and the London Avenue Canal are not rivers. They are urban drainage channels — narrow, straight, with houses standing within a few metres of the wall.

Here is the sentence the federal investigation wrote about them, and this programme exists to put it in front of you.

Floodwalls at the 17th Street and London Avenue Canals **were not overtopped, but failed at water levels below their design height.** <!-- KT-501 -->

Not overtopped. Below design height.

The water never reached the top of the wall. The wall failed anyway.

Now the numbers, because they are the film.

At the 17th Street Canal, the top of the floodwall was supposed to be fourteen feet. Because of an out-of-date datum and settlement of the ground it stood on, it was actually somewhere between about twelve and twelve and a half. The water that broke it was around ten to eleven. <!-- KT-503 -->

The breach began to develop at about six in the morning and was fully developed before nine. <!-- KT-503 -->

And at the London Avenue Canal, the south breach happened between about seven and eight in the morning **when the water level in the canal was eight point two to nine point five feet.** <!-- KT-505 -->

The design height there was fourteen point four. <!-- KT-505 -->

Read those two together. Eight feet of water. A wall built for fourteen.

Nine hundred and fifty feet up the canal, on the opposite bank, another stretch of wall moved and tilted — and held. The investigators put it carefully: it **must have been close to failure.** The other team called it a failure frozen in place. <!-- KT-507 -->

So the same design, on the same day, in the same canal, was a few inches of movement from doing it twice.

And before the mechanism, one number that has to be handled honestly, because it is the number everyone asks for and the sources do not agree on it.

How many breaches were there?

The federal task force identified **fifty major breaches** — a major breach being a place where the structures failed, causing a dramatic reduction in protective elevation and losing the ability to keep the water out. Of those fifty, **four were caused by foundation failures** and the rest by a combination of overtopping and scour. **Three of the four foundation breaches were in the drainage canals.** <!-- KT-401, KT-403 -->

But the same report's own appendix lists **twenty-nine**, because adjacent breaches were aggregated together for the purposes of repair. The engineering review panel counts **four** — only the walls that failed before the water went over them. The Louisiana team says **over fifty**. And the independent university team gives no system-wide total at all. <!-- KT-402 -->

Four different numbers, and none of them is wrong. They are counting four different things. This programme will say whose number it is using, every time.

What is not in dispute is the scale of the damage. **Over two hundred and twenty miles of the protective structures were damaged**, and about forty-one miles were judged severely damaged. <!-- KT-404 -->

Out of about three hundred and fifty.

What broke?

## ACT_2 — WHAT THE WATER ACTUALLY DID

Before the failure, the mechanism. And before the mechanism, the thing that makes this story matter to somebody who does not care about soil.

Two-thirds. In Orleans East Bank and St. Bernard, approximately two-thirds of the flooding — and half of the economic losses — came from water flowing through breaches in the floodwalls and levees, not over them. <!-- KT-801 -->

For the east bank of Orleans Parish specifically, the outfall canal breaches and the west-side Industrial Canal failures were responsible for about seventy percent of the flooding. <!-- KT-802 -->

And the investigators ran the alternative. They modelled the same storm with no breaching and working pumps.

Flooding in some areas **may have been as little as one-third** of what actually happened. And then they wrote the line that separates a bad night from a catastrophe. **Overtopping was extensive, but would have only occurred for a matter of hours. Breaching allowed water to enter the protected areas for days.** <!-- KT-803 -->

Hours, against days. That is the difference between a flooded ground floor and a drowned city.

So: what broke.

Start with the 17th Street Canal, because the answer there is strange and physical and entirely mechanical.

The federal team found instability caused by shear failure within the clay in the foundation beneath the levee and the wall. And then they named the key factor: **the formation of a gap between the wall and the levee fill on the canal side of the wall, allowing water pressure to act on the wall below the surface of the levee.** <!-- KT-504 -->

A gap. Between the concrete and the earth it was standing in.

As the water rises, it pushes the top of the wall. The wall leans, very slightly, away from the canal. And on the canal side, at the base, a crack opens between the concrete and the soil.

Water gets into that crack.

And now the water is not just pushing on the part of the wall you can see. It is pushing on the buried part too, all the way down the gap — and it is doing something worse than pushing. It is cutting the earth bank in half. The half on the canal side is no longer helping to hold up the half on the city side.

When it let go, the wall and the levee it stood on **moved about forty feet sideways**, and finished tilted at about forty-five degrees. <!-- KT-504 -->

Forty feet. Not crumbled. Translated — pushed bodily across the ground like a shunted railway carriage.

At London Avenue the ingredient underneath was different, and worse. Sand.

The gap opened there too — the investigators call it the key factor the two canals had in common — but at London Avenue the water in the gap **flowed down into the underlying sand.** Pressure built in that sand under the earth bank. It lifted the marsh layer on the dry side of the levee, and then it ran, concentrated, eroding, taking away the ground that was supporting the wall from behind. <!-- KT-506 -->

The wall was not pushed over. The ground it stood in was carried out from under it.

And here the record splits, and this programme is going to show you the split rather than tidy it.

The federal team, IPET, describes the 17th Street failure as largely rotational, shearing through soft grey clay. The independent university team, ILIT, examined the same ground and found something else: a largely **translational** failure sliding along a thin, continuous seam of weak, highly sensitive organic clayey silt inside the marsh layer. <!-- KT-510 -->

They also disagree about the Lower Ninth Ward, and it matters more there. The federal team concluded that three of the four Industrial Canal breaches were overtopping and scour — but that **one of the east side breaches occurred before the wall was overtopped**, and that this one was **the likely source of the early flooding in the Lower 9th Ward.** <!-- KT-509 -->

The university team says both east-bank failures were principally **underseepage** — water travelling under the sheet piling because the steel was too short to cut it off — and that overtopping **was not the principal cause of either.** <!-- KT-510 -->

Two teams. The same ground. Two mechanisms.

They agree on the thing that matters: at the drainage canals, the water never got over the wall.

The independent team put the whole set in one sentence. **None of these failures were the result of overtopping; surge levels in all three drainage canals were well below the design levels, and well below the tops of the floodwalls.** <!-- KT-508 -->

Well below. Twice over — below what the walls were designed for, and below where the concrete actually ended.

And the federal team's own summary agrees, in the language of a report that does not want to be dramatic: **four major floodwall breaches, three in the outfall canals and one on the Industrial Canal, resulted from shear failure or erosion and piping through the foundation soils at water elevations below the original design elevations.** <!-- KT-502 -->

At water elevations below the original design elevations. That clause is the case.

So how does a modern wall come to have a gap behind it that nobody designed for?

## ACT_3 — THE TEST IN THE SWAMP

Before the failure, the reasons. And the reasons here begin with somebody doing something right.

In the 1960s and 70s, New Orleans needed higher protection, and there was no room for it. A levee is a pyramid of earth; to make it taller you must make it wider, and there was nothing to widen into — the canals had houses along both banks.

So the Corps of Engineers made a choice that is still the standard answer to that problem: put a concrete wall along the crest of the existing earth bank, and hold it up with steel piling driven into the levee.

And then they did something better than that. They decided to test it.

The independent investigators, who are not gentle with the Corps anywhere else in their report, are unambiguous about this decision. The New Orleans District, they wrote, **made an admirable decision to construct a test section and perform a full-scale test of this type of design.** <!-- KT-601 -->

Not a computer model. A real wall, in real ground, loaded with real water.

They needed ground that behaved like the ground in New Orleans, and they found it about eighty miles west, in the Atchafalaya basin. The investigators note why the site was chosen, and the sentence has a chill on it in hindsight. **The swampy foundation soils at this test site were remarkably similar to those at the north end of the 17th Street canal in New Orleans.** <!-- KT-602 -->

They built the wall. They raised the water against it. And they watched what happened.

So what does a full-scale test, on the right soil, actually tell you that a calculation cannot?

This was 1977. <!-- KT-602 -->

Twenty-eight years before the storm.

Here is what the test showed, in the investigators' words.

**A gap opened between the sheetpile curtain and the outboard side earthen embankment during loading, and then water penetrated into this gap. This effectively cut the supporting embankment in half, and the water pressures applied against the lower sheetpile sections helped to push the inboard half of the embankment, as well as the I-wall and its supporting sheetpile curtain, towards the inboard — the protected — side.** <!-- KT-603 -->

Every word of that is a description of what happened at the 17th Street Canal on the twenty-ninth of August, 2005.

And the investigators add the clause that turns a technical finding into the spine of a film. This was **a failure mechanism that had not traditionally been considered in the local design of floodwall systems in the New Orleans District.** <!-- KT-603 -->

Not considered — until this test showed it. Which is exactly what a test is for.

There was a second lesson too: the shape of the failure surface was more curved than the flat, wedge-shaped surfaces the district's standard method of analysis assumed. <!-- KT-603 -->

So: a well-designed, expensive, deliberately commissioned full-scale field test, on the right soil, produced two specific findings about how this exact kind of wall fails.

And then.

**Unfortunately, despite publication of these important findings in both internal Corps reports as well as in professional journals — and despite the fact that these studies had been undertaken to facilitate the design of the challenging floodwalls along the drainage canals — neither of these lessons were then incorporated in the subsequent design of the floodwalls along the 17th Street, Orleans and London Avenue drainage canals, nor along the Industrial Canal.** <!-- KT-604 -->

Read that once for the facts and once for the shape.

They ran the test in order to design these walls. The test told them how these walls fail. They designed these walls without it.

The investigators put it into a formal finding: the mechanism the field test disclosed **proved to be the actual field failure mechanism at this site**, and it **was not among the suite of cases and mechanisms analysed in the original design studies.** <!-- KT-605 -->

And they add a sentence that removes the last available excuse. It was **not an unexpected mode of failure**, they write, because it **had been clearly evinced** in that 1977 test. And then, plainly, about themselves: **we had read the E-99 full-scale test section reports, and were aware of the likelihood of this mechanism.** <!-- KT-606 -->

The people who worked out what killed those walls in 2006 did it, in part, by reading a report from 1977.

There is more in the same section, and it is the same shape twice.

Two permeability tests had been run on the ground under these canals, and both concluded that underseepage — water travelling beneath the sheet piling — was not a risk worth designing against. The investigators call that **a very serious error**, and say it resulted in sheet piles throughout the system **routinely being far too short.** <!-- KT-609 -->

And underneath all of it, a number. These walls were designed to a factor of safety of **one point three** — meaning they were calculated to be thirty percent stronger than the load they were expected to carry. The investigators call that **far too low a value for a system protecting a large urban population**, and they trace where it came from: standards developed for levees protecting **agricultural land**, in the first half of the twentieth century. <!-- KT-608 -->

A city was being held up to a standard written for a field.

And it is worth being exact about what a factor of safety is, because the number sounds reassuring and is not. It is a ratio: the load the structure can take, divided by the load it is expected to see. One point three means thirty percent of headroom — on soil measurements taken from boreholes, in ground that varies between one borehole and the next, under a storm nobody has met yet.

Aircraft do not fly on one point three. Buildings full of people do not stand on one point three. It is a farm number, and it was holding up a city of half a million.

And the standard the whole system was built to had a history of its own.

Congress had directed the Corps to design for **the most severe combination of meteorological conditions that are considered reasonably characteristic of the region** — a definition that became something called the standard project hurricane. <!-- KT-701 -->

The criteria for it came from a report published in **1959**, and were **based on historic hurricanes from 1900 to 1956.** <!-- KT-702 -->

In 1979, the weather service issued revised criteria that took in later storms — **including Betsy, which had directly hit New Orleans.** <!-- KT-703 -->

The review panel's finding on that is one flat sentence. **It does not appear that the USACE used the updated SPH criteria, however.** <!-- KT-703 -->

And the panel points out something about the standard itself that is easy to miss: the phrase **reasonably characteristic** means, by definition, that this is **not an extreme hurricane event.** Their own section heading reads: *Hurricane Protection System Under-designed.* <!-- KT-704 -->

Within even that standard, the Corps **chose meteorological parameters at the low end** of the range that had been given as representative maximum winds for a hurricane striking New Orleans. <!-- KT-705 -->

And one design decision matters more than all of them for what happened at the Industrial Canal. **Levees and I-walls were designed to provide protection up to the estimated water elevations. They were not designed to withstand overtopping. There was no armoring or uniform use of erosion resistant soils.** <!-- KT-706 -->

Not designed to survive being overtopped at all. Which is why, on the east side of the city, once the water came over the top, the walls came apart from behind.

For scale: the review panel put Katrina's probability-based recurrence interval **in the range of fifty to five hundred years.** <!-- KT-707 -->

So when the water came, and the walls did what a test had already shown they would do — what did the law say about it?

## ACT_4 — THE COURT

It said, at first, exactly what you would hope a court would say.

Thousands of people sued the United States. The case that reached trial was brought under the Federal Tort Claims Act, and it was not about the floodwalls at all — those were already immune, and the plaintiffs did not contest it. <!-- KT-201, KT-207 -->

It was about a shipping channel. The Mississippi River-Gulf Outlet, MR-GO, cut in the 1960s from the city out to the Gulf through virgin coastal wetlands, at a depth that **exposed strata of so-called fat clay, a form of soil soft enough that it will move if made to bear a load.** <!-- KT-102 -->

The channel's designers **considered and rejected armouring its banks**, leaving them vulnerable to erosion. <!-- KT-103 -->

They eroded. MR-GO widened to **an average of three times its design width**, destroying the marsh that had protected the levee behind it, and adding open water for waves to build across. <!-- KT-106, KT-107 -->

Bank protection for the critical stretch had been authorised in **1967** and charged to the project in **1968**. And then, in the court record's own dry phrasing: **from 1968 until 1980, it is not evident why the protection was not implemented.** <!-- KT-104 -->

Twelve years of nothing, with no reason recorded.

In November of 2009, after trial, the judge ruled. And the language he chose is why people still quote this case.

He found that the negligence of the Corps — by failing to maintain MR-GO properly — **was not policy, but insouciance, myopia and shortsightedness.** <!-- KT-203 -->

**For over forty years**, he wrote, **the Corps was aware that the Reach II levee protecting Chalmette and the Lower Ninth Ward was going to be compromised by the continued deterioration of the MRGO.** <!-- KT-204 -->

And then the sentence that mattered legally, because it was aimed straight at the government's defence. If **the Corps' monumental negligence here would somehow be regarded as 'policy'**, he wrote, **then the exception would be an amorphous incomprehensible defense without any discernable contours.** <!-- KT-205 -->

He was talking about the discretionary function exception — the rule that the United States cannot be sued over the exercise of a discretionary policy judgement. And he held it did not apply here. <!-- KT-206 -->

Three of the seven plaintiffs at that trial won on all claims. Four did not. <!-- KT-208 -->

And there is a piece of this that the film has to correct, because it is the most repeated thing anybody knows about MR-GO and it is wrong.

For twenty years the popular account has been that MR-GO acted as a funnel — that the channel gathered the storm surge from the Gulf and delivered it, amplified, into the heart of the city.

The federal task force modelled it and killed it. The channel, **presumed to be a major factor in propagating storm surge into the IHNC, was demonstrated to have little impact on storm water levels for large storms.** <!-- KT-708 -->

So the funnel is a myth. And the lawsuit about MR-GO was still correct — for a completely different reason.

The case was never about surge. It was about **erosion**: a channel allowed to widen to three times its design width, eating the marsh and the banks that had stood between the open water and the levee behind them. <!-- KT-106, KT-107 -->

The popular version blamed the channel for delivering the water. The court record blames the Corps for letting the channel eat the defence. Only the second one is supported, and only the second one was tried.

The government appealed.

And on the second of March, 2012, the Fifth Circuit affirmed. **We AFFIRM each of the judgments**, the panel wrote. <!-- KT-301 -->

For six months, the finding stood.

Then the same court reheard it. And on the twenty-fifth of September, 2012, the same three judges wrote a new opinion, and the first paragraph reads: **We REVERSE each judgment for the plaintiffs, AFFIRM each judgment for the government.** <!-- KT-302 -->

Not because the facts had changed. Nothing in the record moved.

The court applied the exception. Under that rule, the government cannot be sued over a discretionary function **whether or not the discretion involved be abused.** <!-- KT-303 -->

And the test is not whether anyone actually weighed policy. It is only whether the decision was **susceptible to policy analysis** — and the very existence of a regulation granting discretion **creates a strong presumption** that it was. <!-- KT-305 -->

The court did not say the Corps had been careful. It acknowledged the other side directly: **The Robinson plaintiffs and amici point to record evidence suggesting that the Corps flatly failed to gauge the risks posed by leaving MRGO's banks unarmored.** <!-- KT-306 -->

And then it answered that much of that evidence went to negligence in the **original design** — which was already immune, and had never been challenged. <!-- KT-306 -->

The holding is one sentence. **Whatever the actual reasons for the delay, the Corps's failure to armor timely Reach 2 is shielded by the DFE.** <!-- KT-304 -->

Whatever the actual reasons.

Twelve years with no recorded reason, and a rule that does not require one.

So what did all the reports conclude, once the courts had finished?

They had already concluded it, years before, and in almost the same words as each other.

The engineering review panel wrote that the hurricane protection system **is a system in name only. In reality, it is a disjointed agglomeration of many individual projects that were conceived and constructed in a piecemeal fashion. Parts were then joined together in 'make-do' arrangements.** <!-- KT-901 -->

And the federal task force, working separately, arrived at the identical phrase: the piecemeal development of the system **provided a system in name only.** <!-- KT-902 -->

The independent team gave three causes — the hurricane, localised engineering failures and questionable judgements, and **more global organizational and institutional problems** — and then declined to hand anybody the villain: **no one group or organization had a monopoly on responsibility.** <!-- KT-904, KT-905 -->

And the Louisiana team wrote the sentence that this film would put on the last card if it had one.

If every failure had come with overtopping, they said, **few would regard the destruction of New Orleans as anything but another sad reminder of the destructive and unavoidable forces of nature. But this was not the case.** Because the breaches were documented and reconstructed, the flooding of New Orleans is now regarded **not solely as a natural disaster, but also as a catastrophic engineering failure — a man-made catastrophe.** <!-- KT-906 -->

⟨HELD⟩

One thousand one hundred and eighteen people died in Louisiana, the vast majority in the five parishes around New Orleans. As of the second of August, 2006, one hundred and thirty-five were still missing. <!-- KT-1001 -->

Almost seventy percent of the dead in those five parishes were sixty-one or older. Forty-five percent were seventy-six or older. <!-- KT-1006 -->

The poor, the elderly and the disabled — **the groups least likely to be able to evacuate without assistance** — were disproportionately among them. <!-- KT-1008 -->

And the independent team closed its report with a list rather than an argument. New Orleans, it noted, has now been flooded by hurricanes six times in a century — **1915, 1940, 1947, 1965, 1969, and 2005** — and then allowed itself one sentence of opinion: **It should not be allowed to happen again.** <!-- KT-909 -->

Six times. The city is not unlucky. The city is downstream of a decision that keeps being made.

There is one more thing the reports found, and it is the quietest and possibly the worst.

The engineering panel looked at how the decisions had been made and named the disease: the process had become **self-referential** — meaning **designers were forced to look to their own organizations to help guide decisions, not to the greater engineering community.** <!-- KT-908 -->

A closed loop. The people checking the work and the people doing the work drawing on the same file.

And one of the reports permits itself a note of irony that is worth hearing exactly. **Ironically, the structures that ultimately breached performed as designed, providing protection until overtopping occurred and then becoming vulnerable to catastrophic breaching.** <!-- KT-903 -->

Performed as designed. That is not a defence of the walls. It is an indictment of the design — because at the drainage canals, they did not even get that far.

So what is left, when the reports are filed and the case is closed?

## ENDING

⟨HELD⟩

Go back to the gap.

It is not a dramatic thing. It is a crack that opens between a concrete wall and the earth it is standing in, when water pushes on the wall — a few inches at the base, invisible from the street, and it fills.

In 1977, in a swamp in the Atchafalaya basin, that gap opened in a test that had been built and paid for in order to design the walls of New Orleans, and it was written down, and it was published. <!-- KT-601, KT-603, KT-604 -->

In August 2005 it opened again at the 17th Street Canal, in ground the investigators had already noted was **remarkably similar**, and a wall built for fourteen feet came apart under ten. <!-- KT-602, KT-503 -->

And in a courtroom in 2012, a different kind of gap held open: between a finding that the Corps had been negligent for forty years, and a rule that made the finding unenforceable **whatever the actual reasons.** <!-- KT-204, KT-304 -->

⟨HELD⟩

The independent report ends on a list, and it is only six numbers.

New Orleans has now been flooded by hurricanes six times in a century — **1915, 1940, 1947, 1965, 1969, and 2005.** <!-- KT-909 -->

And then the last line those engineers allowed themselves. **It should not be allowed to happen again.** <!-- KT-909 -->

Somewhere there is a test that was run for the thing protecting you, and a file that says what it found.
