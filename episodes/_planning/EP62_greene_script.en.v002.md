## HOOK (0:00–0:50 · voice from frame 0)

A man went to an apartment door in Louisville, Kentucky, and knocked. Nobody answered.

So he took a piece of paper and fixed it to the outside of the door. A thumbtack, or a strip of tape. That was the whole of it.

The paper said the tenant was being taken to court over the apartment. Under Kentucky law, once it was on the door, the tenant had been served. The clock started. The hearing would happen whether or not anyone inside ever read a word of it.

## OP (0:50–1:05)

The case they brought is called *Greene v. Lindsey*. Greene is the sheriff. Lindsey is a tenant.

What follows is not really a story about eviction. It is a story about a piece of paper, and about what a State is allowed to assume once it has let go of one.

---

## ACT_1 (1:05–6:45)

In 1975, the Housing Authority of Louisville began proceedings against three of its tenants. The Court's opinion names them once: Linnie Lindsey, Barbara Hodgens and Pamela Ray, tenants in a Louisville housing project.

That is very nearly everything the record says about them as people. No ages. No jobs. No families. No rent figures, no arrears, no account of what they were supposed to have done. Three names and a shared address.

That absence is not sloppiness. It is what this kind of case looks like from the inside. The question in front of the courts was never whether these three women were good tenants. It was whether a particular piece of paper, fixed to a particular door, counted.

The proceeding was a forcible entry and detainer action — the summary process a landlord uses to get possession back quickly. Speed is the point of it. Kentucky, like most States, wanted these cases resolved in weeks rather than months, and built the procedure accordingly.

Service of the court's notice fell to the sheriff's office. Joseph Greene was the Jefferson County Sheriff. His deputies carried the writs. Also named in the suit were the Housing Authority itself and several public officials responsible for particular Louisville projects.

The deputies are named the way the record names them, which is to say not at all. The appellants' own brief describes the job in one clause: the officer of the court who is charged with serving notice in a forcible entry and detainer action, usually a Jefferson County Deputy Sheriff. Usually a deputy. Never a name.

So on one side of this case: three tenants. On the other: a city housing authority, a county sheriff, and the Commonwealth's own statute.

There is a temptation, in a story like this, to reach for the landlord who wants somebody out. Reach for it here and you have the facts wrong. The body that started these proceedings was a government body. The men who carried the paper were sworn officers. Every step of what follows was done by the State, to people the State was housing.

⟨HELD⟩
Which is why it became a constitutional question at all.

The question the Supreme Court agreed to answer: whether this statute, as applied to tenants in a public housing project, fails to afford those tenants the notice of proceedings initiated against them required by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

As applied. Not in the abstract.

Consider what a summary eviction actually requires of the person on the receiving end. A date. A courtroom. A day off work, or a shift swapped, or a child minded. And before any of that, a single piece of information: that the thing is happening.

That information is what the law calls notice. Everything else in a court proceeding — the arguments, the evidence, the judgment — is downstream of it. A hearing that a person does not know about is not a hearing they failed to attend. It is a hearing that happened to them.

The Supreme Court put it in one line, borrowed from a 1914 case called *Grannis v. Ordean*: the fundamental requisite of due process of law is the opportunity to be heard. Then it added the part that carries this case, from a 1950 decision called *Mullane* — that the right to be heard has little reality or worth unless one is informed that the matter is pending, and can choose for himself whether to appear or default, acquiesce or contest.

Little reality or worth. Not a technicality. The whole substance of the thing.

The Kentucky statute at issue was section 454.030. It is three sentences long and it is worth hearing in full, because almost everything in this case turns on the order of its clauses.

If the officer directed to serve notice on the defendant in forcible entry or detainer proceedings cannot find the defendant on the premises mentioned in the writ, he may explain and leave a copy of the notice with any member of the defendant's family over sixteen years of age. And if no such person is found, he may serve the notice by posting a copy on a conspicuous part of the premises.

Read it slowly and it sounds careful. Three steps, descending. Find the person. Failing that, find an adult in the household. Failing that, put it on the door.

Look at the middle step for a moment, because it tells you what the legislature pictured. A member of the defendant's family over sixteen years of age. Somebody home. Somebody old enough to understand a court paper and to pass it on. The statute even tells the officer to explain it — he may explain and leave a copy. That is a conversation on a doorstep, in daylight, with a person who lives there.

The statute is written for a household with somebody in it. And the third step is what happens when there is not.

There is one more sentence in section 454.030, and it is the shortest. The notice shall state the time and place of meeting of the court.

That sentence is the reason the paper exists. A date and an address. It is the only thing in the procedure that tells a tenant where to go and when.

The appellants — the sheriff and the officials — described the ladder exactly the way the statute reads. Personal service first. A family member second. Posting as a last resort.

For all three women it went all the way down. In each instance, the opinion says, notice took the form of posting a copy of the writ of forcible entry and detainer on the door of the tenant's apartment. Three doors. No conversation on any doorstep.

The Supreme Court would not accept the appellants' description. Not because anyone had lied about the words of the statute, but because of what the record showed actually happened when a deputy walked up to a door in a Louisville housing project in the middle of a working day.

## ACT_2 (6:45–10:30)

Start with what posting physically is. The Court defined it in a footnote, because it had to: posting refers to the practice of placing the writ on the property by use of a thumbtack, adhesive tape, or other means.

A thumbtack. Adhesive tape. Or other means.

That is the mechanism by which a citizen of Kentucky was informed that the State had begun a proceeding to remove them from their home.

Now the sequence, in the appellants' own words, from their brief. First, the officer goes to the apartment in an effort to effectuate personal in-hand service. Second, if the named tenant is absent or will not appear at the door, personal in-hand service is made on any member of the tenant's family over sixteen years of age. Finally, if no one answers the door, a copy of the notice is posted on the premises, usually the door.

And then the sentence that undoes it. If no one is at home at the time of that visit, as is apparently true in a good percentage of cases, posting follows forthwith.

Forthwith. The same visit. The deputy knocks, nobody comes, and there is no household member standing behind the person who did not answer the door — because nobody answered the door. So step two evaporates, step three happens, and the officer is back in the car.

The Court set this out plainly. Neither the statute, nor the practice of the process servers, makes provision for even a second attempt at personal service — perhaps at some time of day when the tenant is more likely to be at home.

Not a second visit the next morning. Not one after five o'clock. Not one at the weekend. One attempt, at whatever hour the route happened to reach that door, and the paper went up.

So when the Court came to the appellants' account — posting only as a last resort — it wrote: we reject appellants' characterization of the procedure.

⟨HELD⟩
The ladder had one rung.

【reset beat: hold on the walkway, no narration, 4s】

There is a detail in the phrase *a good percentage* that is easy to skate past. It is not the Court's estimate. It is the appellants' own. The people defending the system are the ones telling you how often the first knock finds nobody home.

Think about who is behind the doors of a public housing project on a Tuesday at eleven in the morning. People at work. People at a second job. People taking a child somewhere, or standing in a queue at an office, or asleep after a night shift. The population most likely to miss a single daytime knock is the population the procedure was aimed at.

None of which, on its own, makes posting unconstitutional. The Court was careful about this, and the care is worth keeping. Short of providing personal service, it wrote, posting notice on the door of a person's home would, in many or perhaps most instances, constitute not only a constitutionally acceptable means of service, but indeed a singularly appropriate and effective way of ensuring that a person who cannot conveniently be served personally is actually apprised of proceedings against him.

Singularly appropriate. Singularly effective. That is the Court defending the practice it is about to hold inadequate.

Many or perhaps most. The Court is not hostile to a piece of paper on a door. In the ordinary case, a notice on your own front door is a perfectly sensible way to reach you. It is where you live. You will be back.

The rule underneath that instinct is old and the Court restated it approvingly: it is reasonable to assume that a property owner will maintain superintendence of his property. You watch your own door.

So the question narrows to something much smaller and much harder than *is posting good enough*. The question is whether, in these buildings, on these doors, the paper stayed up long enough to be read.

That is a question of fact. And there was a record.

## ACT_3 (10:30–15:10)

The record was made of depositions from the process servers themselves. The men who put the papers on the doors, under oath, describing their own work.

One of them was asked whether he was aware of there being any problem with children ripping the writs off. His answer: oh, we had plenty of trouble.

The questioner checked it. You had trouble? With kids, yeah. Yeah.

Asked whether he had ever seen it, he said he had. I have seen them take them off of the door, and I would go back and tell them to put it back. They don't know. They didn't know. Asked where, he named one place. Well most of that was in Village West.

A second server gave a smaller answer to the same question. Did you ever see kids pulling them off? Yes. Did you see many? No, not too many — I did see it in one place over there. Where was that? Village West. How many times did you see that happen? Well, probably a couple of times.

Two men, deposed separately, naming the same development. The dissent gives that second man a name: Carter Bacon.

A third described the arrangement almost administratively. The children — we had problems with children. They would take them off. They never took them off when we were present, but the Housing Authority told us that they would take them off, so we always put them up high.

⟨HELD⟩
The Housing Authority told us.

Not a discovery made by the tenants' lawyers. Not something dragged out of a reluctant witness. The landlord's own staff had told the men doing the posting that the papers came off the doors, and the men doing the posting kept posting them.

The Court's summary of all this was one sentence: as the process servers were well aware, notices posted on apartment doors in the area where these tenants lived were not infrequently removed by children or other tenants before they could have their intended effect.

Two words in that sentence carry the case. *Well aware.* The people responsible for the notice knew the notice did not stay put.

And notice what the Court did not write. It did not write a percentage. It wrote *not infrequently*, and later, *a significant number of instances*. There is no count anywhere in this opinion, because nobody had counted. What the record supported was a description, and the Court used the words the record supported and no stronger ones.

It would be dishonest to leave the record there, and this film is not going to. The testimony was not all one way, and the dissent said so directly.

One server told the court he had been warned beforehand — that Carter Bacon had told him he suspected, though he was not certain, that on some occasions the writs had been torn off the doors by kids. Then the witness stepped back from it. This is what he told me. Whether that is true or not, I don't know. And I don't think that he observed that.

Then his own account: and the six months I was working at it there was no occasion where I saw anyone tear the writs off of the door.

Six months. Not once.

That man was Gilbert Brutscher, and the dissent set his answer directly against Bacon's. Same job, same doors, opposite answers.

Another server said the crews always tried to put the paper up above where a small child could not reach — and that he had never had a complaint about children ripping notices off, and had never seen a child try.

So: a handful of men, describing a practice they had each seen differently, in different buildings, over different years. That is the entire evidentiary base of this case, and how much weight it could bear became the argument that split the Court.

What the tenants themselves said was narrow, and it needs to be repeated exactly, because it is the single most likely thing to be got wrong about this case.

They claimed never to have seen the posted summonses. They stated that they did not learn of the proceedings until they were served with writs of possession, executed after default judgments had been entered — and after their opportunity to appeal had lapsed.

Claimed. Stated. Those are the Court's verbs, and the Court kept them. It wrote that appellees claim to have suffered precisely such a failure of actual notice. Not *did suffer*. Claim to have suffered.

⟨HELD⟩
Nobody ever tried that question. It never got that far.

The case arrived at the Supreme Court on summary judgment, which means the courts below were deciding whether the procedure could stand at all — not whether these three women were telling the truth about their own doors. What is established is the practice. What is alleged, and never tested, is what happened at three particular apartments in 1975.

That distinction is not a hedge. It is the difference between a documentary and a story.

## ACT_4 (15:10–20:05)

The three tenants had no way to reopen the evictions in the state courts. The Court's phrase for their position is four words long: thus without recourse in the state courts. The judgments were final. The appeal window had closed. In the system that had taken the apartments, there was no door left to knock on.

So they went to federal court, filing a class action in the Western District of Kentucky under section 1983 — the Reconstruction-era statute that lets a citizen sue a state official for violating a constitutional right. They asked for declaratory and injunctive relief: not money, but a ruling that the practice was unlawful and an order to stop it.

The notice procedure, they said, did not satisfy the minimum standards of constitutionally adequate notice described in *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank and Trust*.

A class action, because the point was never three apartments. If posting was constitutionally inadequate on these doors, it was inadequate on every door in the project, and on every door in every project served the same way.

They lost.

On cross-motions for summary judgment, the District Court granted judgment for the sheriff and the officials, in an unreported opinion. But it did something in that opinion that ends up mattering more than the result.

What it leaned on was old. Some seventy years earlier, in *Weber v. Grand Lodge of Kentucky*, the Sixth Circuit had upheld posting under the predecessor statute to section 454.030 — on the ground that it was reasonable for the State to presume that a notice posted on the door of the building in dispute would give the tenant actual notice in time to contest the action.

That was 1909. The presumption was doing all the work.

Then the District Court conceded that the ground had moved. It acknowledged that conditions had changed since *Weber*, and that there was undisputed testimony in this case that notices posted on the apartment doors of tenants are often removed.

Undisputed testimony. Often removed. The judge who ruled against these women wrote that down, and then ruled against them anyway — on the basis that posting only comes into play after the officer cannot find the defendant on the premises.

Which is the appellants' description of the statute. The one the Supreme Court would later refuse.

Hold those two things together, because they are the strangest page in the file. The trial judge found that the testimony was undisputed. The trial judge found that the notices often came off. And the trial judge ruled that the procedure was constitutionally sufficient — on the ground that posting only happens after the officer cannot find the defendant.

That is not a contradiction in the judge's logic. It is the same assumption the appellants made and the same one the statute makes: that a step described as a last resort is in fact a last resort. Read the words of the law and it is. Read the depositions and it is not.

The Sixth Circuit reversed, and overruled *Weber* to do it. Its own decision, seventy years old, pointing the other way — and the court of appeals said the ground underneath it had shifted. Its line is the sharpest sentence in the whole history of the case: there may have been a time when posting provided a surer means of giving notice than did mailing. That time has passed. The uncontradicted testimony by process servers themselves establishes it.

What it did with the case matters. It reversed the grant of summary judgment and remanded the case for further proceedings. Reversed and remanded. Not decided. Sent back.

The court of appeals also did the arithmetic that constitutional cases usually avoid. Requiring Kentucky to provide notice by mail when personal service proves infeasible will not be overly burdensome. The cost will be minimal.

And it pointed at a State that had already done it — noting with approval the provisions of the New York counterpart to the Kentucky statute, under which a copy is also mailed when notice is served by posting.

So this was not a hypothetical remedy invented by judges. It was a stamp, and another State was already buying them.

The Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction in 1981, heard argument on the twenty-third of February 1982, and decided it on the seventeenth of May, as number 81-341 on its docket.

The people in the room are worth naming, because they tell you who thought this mattered. William Hoge argued for the sheriff and the officials. Robert Frederick Smith argued for the tenants. Briefs came in from the National Housing Law Project, and from the Antioch School of Law, urging the Court to affirm. A dispute about a thumbtack in Jefferson County had become a question about how every summary eviction in the country begins.

Before the holding, one more thing the appellants conceded, because it shows how narrow the ground under them had become. If the landlord had been seeking past-due rent rather than possession, personal service would have been required by Kentucky law.

Money got a person served. The apartment did not.

The State took more care to find you when it wanted your money than when it wanted your home.

## ACT_5 (20:05–28:20)

The opinion was delivered by Justice Brennan, and the standard he reached for was not new. It came from that 1950 case about notifying beneficiaries of a trust, and it has governed notice ever since: an elementary and fundamental requirement of due process in any proceeding which is to be accorded finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action.

Reasonably calculated. Under all the circumstances. Two conditions, and the second is where this case lives.

The Court then said what was at stake in the plainest words it used all day. In this case, appellees have been deprived of a significant interest in property — indeed, of the right to continued residence in their homes.

And it set the test: the sufficiency of notice must be tested with reference to its ability to inform people of the pendency of proceedings that affect their interests. A procedure's effect must be judged in the light of its practical application to the affairs of men as they are ordinarily conducted.

Practical application. Not the words of the statute. What the thing does.

Then the turn. But whatever the efficacy of posting in many cases, it is clear that, in the circumstances of this case, merely posting notice on an apartment door does not satisfy minimum standards of due process. In a significant number of instances, reliance on posting under this statute results in a failure to provide actual notice to the tenant concerned.

⟨HELD⟩
And so posting on the apartment door could not be considered a reliable means of acquainting interested parties of the fact that their rights are before the courts.

The Court closed the assumption that had held the practice up. Failure to reach the tenant on one visit, it wrote, hardly suggests that the tenant has abandoned his interest in the apartment such that mere pro forma notice might be constitutionally sufficient.

Missing someone once tells you nothing about them. It tells you something about the time of day.

On the mails, the Court was careful and comparative rather than absolute. The Postal Service is an efficient and inexpensive means of communication. Notice by mail in the circumstances of this case would surely go a long way toward providing the constitutionally required assurance. And it noticed the shape of the thing: where the subject matter of the action also happens to be the mailing address of the defendant, and where personal service is ineffectual, notice by mail may reasonably be relied upon.

The apartment they were trying to take was the place they would have got the letter.

Then the Court named the failure exactly. The State's continued exclusive reliance on an ineffective means of service is not notice reasonably calculated to reach those who could easily be informed by other means at hand.

Continued exclusive reliance. Not the paper on the door — the decision to let the paper be the only thing.

The holding: in failing to afford appellees adequate notice of the proceedings against them before issuing final orders of eviction, the State deprived them of property without due process of law.

Now the part that gets misremembered, and this film is going to spend real time on it.

The Court did not ban posting. It said so itself, in terms, and it underlined the limit: in light of the findings of the courts below, we hold only that posted notice pursuant to section 454.030 is constitutionally inadequate.

Only. In light of the findings below.

It did not order the mail either. It is not our responsibility to prescribe the form of service that the Commonwealth should adopt. And it gave up more ground, in a clause almost nobody quotes: even conceding that process served by mail is far from the ideal means of providing the notice the Due Process Clause requires. Far from the ideal means. The majority, about its own preferred remedy.

The furthest it would go was comparative — that posted service accompanied by mail service is constitutionally preferable to posted service alone.

And the case was not over. What the Supreme Court affirmed was a judgment that had reversed a summary judgment and sent the whole thing back for further proceedings. Affirmed does not mean three tenants walked out holding a key. What happened on remand is not in this opinion, and neither is anything else about them. What became of Linnie Lindsey, Barbara Hodgens and Pamela Ray is not in the opinion. The record simply stops.

Three Justices did not agree, and the dissent is not a footnote. It was written by Justice O'Connor, joined by the Chief Justice and by Justice Rehnquist. The opinion prints no tally of the votes on the other side.

It opens: today, the Court holds that the Constitution prefers the use of the Postal Service to posted notice — and it reaches this conclusion despite the total absence of any evidence in the record regarding the speed and reliability of the mails.

Then the charge. The sole ground for the Court's result is the scant and conflicting testimony of a handful of process servers in Kentucky. On this flimsy basis, the Court overturns the work of the Kentucky Legislature.

And then the point a lawyer notices first. The Court, the dissent said, does not cite a single case, other than the decision below, supporting its position that notice by posting is constitutionally inadequate in summary eviction proceedings.

Not one case.

The dissent counted the legislatures involved: at least eleven States authorizing notice in summary eviction proceedings solely by posting, or by leaving the notice at the tenant's residence. It listed them in a footnote — Alabama, Colorado, Florida, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Hampshire, North Carolina and West Virginia.

And it read the statute the way the appellants had: personal service first, a family member over sixteen second, posting only as a last resort. Both opinions read the same three clauses. One read the words. The other read the depositions.

It also offered the counter-image that has outlived everything else in it. It is no secret, after all, that unattended mailboxes are subject to plunder by thieves. Posting, at least, gives assurance that the notice has gotten as far as the door.

That is a real argument. A letter can be stolen, or misdelivered, or thrown out with the rest of the post. The majority never proved the mails were better. It proved this door was worse.

The dissent had a second line of attack, and it went to the purpose of the procedure itself. A forcible entry and detainer action is a summary proceeding for quickly determining whether a landlord has the right to immediate possession. Speed is not a defect in it; speed is the design. So, the dissent argued, the means chosen for service have to be measured against that purpose — and it quoted the Court's own earlier reasoning back at it: speedy adjudication is desirable to prevent subjecting the landlord to undeserved economic loss and the tenant to unmerited harassment.

The majority answered that directly, and its answer is the sentence that decides the case. From the perspective of the tenant, it wrote, it is difficult to see how a means of serving process that fails to afford actual notice in a not insubstantial number of cases can be deemed adequate — whatever the proceeding is called.

Speed is a reason to keep a procedure short. It is not a reason to keep it from working.

There is one more thing the Court declined to do, and the declining is deliberate. Notice law had long turned on a category: whether an action was against a person or against a thing — in personam or in rem. Which box a summary eviction belonged in had been argued for decades. The Court refused to decide it, exactly as it had refused in 1950, saying it would not resolve the constitutional question on that basis.

⟨HELD⟩
The category was not the question. What the paper did was the question.

---

The dissent's last move was institutional, and it is the oldest objection in constitutional law. The Court, it said, seems to forget that we have long since discarded the concept that due process authorizes courts to hold laws unconstitutional when they believe the legislature has acted unwisely. It went further, and accused the majority of doing the very thing it disclaimed — giving lip service to the principle that it is not our responsibility to prescribe the form of service, and then going on to do just that.

The majority answered in one line: the dissent misconstrues the constitutional standard.

⟨HELD⟩
That is where the two opinions stop talking to each other.

## ENDING (28:20–30:00)

What the case leaves is smaller than its reputation and more durable than its remedy. Not a rule about envelopes. A method. Before the State may treat a person as having been told something, it has to ask whether its way of telling them actually works — here, in these buildings, on these doors, under the conditions the State itself has described in its own brief.

The answer to that question is a matter of evidence. In this case the evidence was seven or eight men describing their rounds, and the Housing Authority telling those men that the papers came off the doors.

That is a very small pile of paper to set against a statute, and the three dissenting Justices said so. It is also, on this record, the only evidence anybody brought. The majority did not weigh the servers' testimony against a study, or a survey, or a count. There was nothing else to weigh it against.

Which leaves the case resting where it started: on the difference between what a procedure is written to do and what it was observed doing. The statute described a ladder with three rungs. The men who climbed it described one. Both descriptions were in the record, and the Court chose the one made by the people who had actually walked up to the doors.

【callback: the taped corner】

Somewhere in Louisville in 1975 a deputy pressed a strip of tape onto a painted door and drove to the next address. He did what the statute told him to do. The paper was on the premises. The service was complete. Everything after that — the date, the courtroom, the judgment, the order of possession — followed correctly from a single assumption.

That the paper would still be there.
