## 5. WHAT EMTALA REQUIRES — IN THE COURT'S OWN WORDS

| ID | Claim | Grade | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EM-01 | This case is the First Circuit's first interpretation of the statute: "This appeal requires us to interpret, for the first time, the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act (EMTALA), 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | ¶1 |
| EM-02 | The court had previously been asked an EMTALA question in *Wilson v. Atlanticare Med. Ctr.*, 868 F.2d 34 (1st Cir. 1989), and refused to reach it because the plaintiff had not preserved the issue. | ✓ | ¶1 n.1 |
| EM-03 | Why Congress acted: Congress became concerned "about the increasing number of reports that hospital emergency rooms are refusing to accept or treat patients with emergency conditions if the patient does not have medical insurance." | ✓ **VERBATIM** (H.R. Rep. No. 241(I), 99th Cong., 1st Sess. 27 (1986), quoted by the court) | Part III |
| EM-04 | The enforcement design: "Needing a carrot to make health-care providers more receptive to the stick, Congress simultaneously amended the Social Security Act, conditioning hospitals' continued participation in the federal Medicare program — a lucrative source of institutional revenue — on acceptance of the duties imposed by the new law." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III |
| EM-05 | Linchpin one: "it requires that a participating hospital afford an appropriate medical screening to all persons who come to its emergency room seeking medical assistance." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III |
| EM-06 | Linchpin two: "if an emergency medical condition exists, the participating hospital must render the services that are necessary to stabilize the patient's condition… unless transferring the patient to another facility is medically indicated and can be accomplished with relative safety." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III |
| EM-07 | Enforcement: EMTALA "establishes monetary penalties for noncompliance" and "authorizes private rights of action against those who transgress its mandates." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III |
| EM-08 | The three elements of an EMTALA claim, as the court states them: (1) the hospital is a participating hospital covered by EMTALA that operates an emergency department or equivalent; (2) "the patient arrived at the facility seeking treatment"; and (3) the hospital either (a) "did not afford the patient an appropriate screening in order to determine if she had an emergency medical condition," or (b) "bade farewell to the patient (whether by turning her away, discharging her, or improvidently transferring her) without first stabilizing the emergency medical condition." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III |
| EM-09 | **Holding — no emergency condition need be proved for a screening claim:** "the failure appropriately to screen, by itself, is sufficient to ground liability as long as the other elements of the cause of action are met." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III |
| EM-10 | The court rejected the contrary dictum of other courts: "This suggestion finds no purchase in the statute's text, and we reject it." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III n.5 |
| EM-11 | But the damages remedy still requires harm: the statutory damage remedy requires a showing of "personal harm as a direct result of a participating hospital's violation of [EMTALA]"; the court adds it is "difficult to imagine a case in which a patient who does not present an emergency medical condition will meet the statute's causation requirement." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part III n.5 |
| EM-12 | **Holding — no motive requirement:** "We hold, therefore, that EMTALA, by its terms, covers all patients who come to a hospital's emergency department, and requires that they be appropriately screened, regardless of insurance status or ability to pay." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(d) |
| EM-13 | The court aligned with every other circuit on motive: "Every court of appeals that has considered this issue has concluded that a desire to shirk the burden of uncompensated care is not a necessary element of a cause of action under EMTALA." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(d) |
| EM-14 | On the Sixth Circuit's *Cleland* rule requiring some improper motive, the court agreed with the Fourth Circuit that such a range of motives "is so broad as to be no limit at all, and as a practical matter amounts to not having a motive requirement." | ✓ **VERBATIM** (quoting *Power*, 42 F.3d at 857) | Part IV(B) n.9 |
| EM-15 | Statutory text quoted in the appendix, § 1395dd(a): the hospital "must provide for an appropriate medical screening examination within the capability of the hospital's emergency department, including ancillary services routinely available to the emergency department, to determine whether or not an emergency medical condition… exists." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Appendix |
| EM-16 | § 1395dd(a) applies to "any individual (whether or not eligible for benefits under [Medicare])" who "comes to the emergency department and a request is made on the individual's behalf for examination or treatment for a medical condition." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Appendix |
| EM-17 | § 1395dd(h), quoted in full by the court: "A participating hospital may not delay provision of an appropriate medical screening examination required under subsection (a) of this section or further medical examination and treatment required under subsection (b) of this section in order to inquire about the individual's method of payment or insurance status." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Appendix |
| EM-18 | § 1395dd(e)(1)(A) defines "emergency medical condition" as one "manifesting itself by acute symptoms of sufficient severity (including severe pain) such that the absence of immediate medical attention could reasonably be expected to result in" serious jeopardy to health, serious impairment to bodily functions, or serious dysfunction of any bodily organ or part. | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Appendix |
| EM-19 | § 1395dd(d)(1)(A): "A participating hospital that negligently violates a requirement of this section is subject to a civil monetary penalty of not more than $50,000… for each such violation." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Appendix |
| EM-20 | § 1395dd(d)(2)(A): an individual harmed may "obtain those damages available for personal injury under the law of the State in which the hospital is located, and such equitable relief as is appropriate." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Appendix |
| EM-21 | The appendix also reproduces the transfer rules, § 1395dd(b)(1) and (c)(1)–(2), including that an unstabilized patient may not be transferred absent a written request or a physician certification that the benefits outweigh the risks, and that an "appropriate transfer" requires a receiving facility with space and personnel that has agreed to accept the patient, transmission of records, and qualified personnel and equipment. **The court decided nothing under these provisions in this case (see PR-08).** | ✓ | Appendix; Part IV(A)(2) n.7 |

## 6. WHAT "APPROPRIATE MEDICAL SCREENING" WAS HELD TO MEAN

This is the doctrinal spine of the episode. Every row is the court speaking.

| ID | Claim | Grade | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AS-01 | The statute does not define the term: "EMTALA requires an appropriate medical screening, but does not explain what constitutes one. The adjectival phrase is not self-defining." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-02 | The court quotes the Sixth Circuit on the word itself: "'Appropriate' is one of the most wonderful weasel words in the dictionary, and a great aid to the resolution of disputed issues in the drafting of legislation. Who, after all, can be found to stand up for 'inappropriate' treatment or actions of any sort?" | ✓ **VERBATIM** (quoting *Cleland*, 917 F.2d at 271) | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-03 | And adds its own: appropriateness, like nature, is "a mutable cloud which is always and never the same" (Ralph Waldo Emerson, *Essays: First Series* (1841)). | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-04 | **The operative test adopted:** "A hospital fulfills its statutory duty to screen patients in its emergency room if it provides for a screening examination reasonably calculated to identify critical medical conditions that may be afflicting symptomatic patients and provides that level of screening uniformly to all those who present substantially similar complaints." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-05 | **The test compressed:** "The essence of this requirement is that there be some screening procedure, and that it be administered even-handedly." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-06 | **The limit:** "EMTALA does not create a cause of action for medical malpractice." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-07 | **The line between the two:** "a refusal to follow regular screening procedures in a particular instance contravenes the statute… but faulty screening, in a particular case, as opposed to disparate screening or refusing to screen at all, does not contravene the statute." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-08 | **How this case crossed the line:** "In this case, HSF's delay in attending to the patient was so egregious and lacking in justification as to amount to an effective denial of a screening examination." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-09 | **The question expressly left open:** "Thus, we need not decide whether mere negligence in failing to expedite screening would itself violate the federal statute." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-10 | The yardstick used was HSF's own written rules. Its policy statement "required its emergency room personnel, inter alia, promptly to take the vital signs of every patient who visited the facility, to make a written record of all such visits, to treat patients suffering from chest pains as critical cases, and to refer all critical cases to an in-house physician immediately." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-11 | What the jury could infer: from Angel's testimony and "the Hospital's utter inability to produce any records anent Ms. Gonzalez's visit," the jury "reasonably could have inferred that the Hospital did not measure up to the parameters it had established, and that the decedent was denied the screening (monitoring of vital signs, compilation of a written chart, immediate referral to an in-house physician) that HSF customarily afforded to persons complaining of chest pains." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-12 | The uniformity requirement: "under EMTALA § 1395dd(a), the same screening examination must be made available to all similarly situated patients." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-13 | Result on this issue: "the jury's finding that HSF denied Ms. Gonzalez an appropriate screening examination is unimpugnable." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(b) |
| AS-14 | **Waiting can itself be the violation:** "we think that regardless of motive, a complete failure to attend a patient who presents a condition that practically everyone knows may indicate an immediate and acute threat to life can constitute a denial of an appropriate medical screening examination under section 1395dd(a)." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(c) |
| AS-15 | **The court's own caveat against over-reading it:** "Much depends upon circumstances; we recognize that an emergency room cannot serve everyone simultaneously." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(c) |
| AS-16 | The finding was permissible only "absent any explanation or mitigating circumstances": the jury could rationally conclude "that the Hospital's inaction here amounted to a deliberate denial of screening." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(c) |
| AS-17 | **The sentence the episode is named for:** "EMTALA should be read to proscribe both actual and constructive dumping of patients." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | Part IV(A)(2)(c) |

## THE SHAPE THE FACTS ALREADY HAVE

The opinion needs no embellishment. This is the sequence, in order, with IDs.

1. On the morning of 6 September 1991, a sixty-five-year-old widow wakes feeling terrible — chills, cold sweat, dizziness, chest pains — and asks her son to drive her to the emergency room she has used before. **CR-01, CR-02, CR-04**
2. She is inside Hospital San Francisco by 1:00 p.m. Her son asks the receptionist to take care of his mother because she has chest pains. The hospital would later say it was told only dizziness and nausea. **CR-05, CR-07, CR-08**
3. An employee does three things: gives her the number forty-seven, tells her to wait, and checks her insurance card. **CR-09**
4. At some point that day — perhaps around 1:00 p.m., according to the doctor at the clinic her plan designates — someone at the hospital telephones ahead to say the patient will be coming there instead. **CR-17, CR-18**
5. An hour passes. Her son calls his sister to take over. **CR-10, TL-05**
6. At about 2:15 p.m. he leaves, and as he goes he hears the attendant call number twenty-four. **CR-11, TL-06**
7. She waits another forty-five to seventy-five minutes. The staff, in the court's word, "blithely" ignore her. No vital signs. No chart. No doctor. **CR-12, CR-14**
8. Between 3:00 and 3:30 p.m., weary of waiting, mother and daughter drive to the clinic. **CR-13, TL-08**
9. There, a physician finds her blood pressure at 90/60, starts fluids, gives her something for the vomiting, and has to resuscitate her soon after she arrives. **CR-21, CR-22**
10. The doctor tries to move her to another hospital and cannot get an ambulance. While a van is being prepared, at around 4:30 p.m., she dies. Hypovolemic shock. **CR-23, CR-24, TL-11**
11. Roughly three and a half hours have passed since she walked into an emergency room. About two of them were spent in its waiting area, unexamined. **TL-12**
12. Her three children and her four grandchildren — the children of a son who had died five months earlier and who had grown up in her house — sue the hospital under a federal statute the First Circuit has never yet interpreted. **PF-02, PF-06, PR-01, EM-01**
13. They allege two things: that she was never appropriately screened, and that she was improperly transferred. A malpractice claim is dismissed and never appealed. **PR-02, PR-04**
14. A jury returns special findings against the hospital on both EMTALA theories and awards $700,000: $200,000 for what she went through, and $500,000 split among the seven of them for what they lost. **PR-06 through PR-12**
15. The trial judge refuses to disturb any of it. The hospital appeals on eight grounds. **PR-13, PR-15**
16. The court of appeals starts with the statute. Congress passed EMTALA because emergency rooms were reportedly refusing patients without insurance, and it enforced the duty by hanging Medicare money on compliance. **EM-03, EM-04**
17. It holds that the duty to screen runs to everyone who walks in — you do not have to prove you were actually in an emergency to prove you were never screened. **EM-09, EM-10**
18. It defines the duty: a screening reasonably calculated to identify critical conditions, applied uniformly to everyone with similar complaints. Some procedure, administered even-handedly. **AS-04, AS-05**
19. It draws the line carefully. Bad screening is malpractice, not an EMTALA violation. Unequal screening, or no screening at all, is. **AS-06, AS-07**
20. It measures the hospital against the hospital's own written rules — take every patient's vital signs promptly, write down every visit, treat chest pain as critical, send critical cases to a doctor immediately — and notes that the hospital could not produce a single record of her visit. **AS-10, AS-11**
21. It concludes that this delay "was so egregious and lacking in justification as to amount to an effective denial of a screening examination," while expressly refusing to decide whether ordinary negligent slowness would violate the statute. **AS-08, AS-09**
22. To the argument that she was given a number and would have been treated had she waited, the court answers that a complete failure to attend a patient whose condition everyone knows may be an immediate threat to life can itself be a denial of screening — and that EMTALA reaches constructive dumping as well as actual dumping. **HA-08, AS-14, AS-17**
23. To the argument that only a money motive can make a hospital liable, it answers that no court of appeals has required one, and holds that the statute covers all comers regardless of insurance status or ability to pay. **EM-12, EM-13**
24. The hospital's best legal argument — that relatives cannot recover for their own grief under EMTALA — is never reached, because the hospital never raised it until after the verdict. Waiver, pure and simple. **DM-02, DM-03, DM-04, DM-06**
25. The damages stand. For the family, because the evidence showed the woman one witness called the trunk of the family tree. For her, because the hospital's failure to provide even rudimentary screening left her to spend the last hours of her life in agony, in terror, waiting for care that never came. **DM-13, DM-15, PF-08**
26. Affirmed. **CT-09**
