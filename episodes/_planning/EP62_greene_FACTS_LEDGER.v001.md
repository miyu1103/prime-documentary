## 6. WHAT THE COURT HELD

| ID | Claim | Grade | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GL-48 | The question presented: "whether this statute, as applied to tenants in a public housing project, fails to afford those tenants the notice of proceedings initiated against them required by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, opening paragraph |
| GL-49 | The starting principle: "The fundamental requisite of due process of law is the opportunity to be heard." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-A, quoting *Grannis v. Ordean*, 234 U. S. 385, 394 (1914) |
| GL-50 | Why notice is the hinge: the "right to be heard has little reality or worth unless one is informed that the matter is pending and can choose for himself whether to appear or default, acquiesce or contest." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-A, quoting *Mullane* at 314 |
| GL-51 | The constitutional floor: "An elementary and fundamental requirement of due process in any proceeding which is to be accorded finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-A, quoting *Mullane*, 339 U. S., at 314 (emphasis in original) |
| GL-52 | What was at stake, in the Court's words: "In this case, appellees have been deprived of a significant interest in property: indeed, of the right to continued residence in their homes." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-53 | The test: "The sufficiency of notice must be tested with reference to its ability to inform people of the pendency of proceedings that affect their interests." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-54 | Reality, not category: a procedure's "effect must be judged in the light of its practical application to the affairs of men as they are ordinarily conducted." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B, quoting *North Laramie Land Co. v. Hoffman*, 268 U. S. 276, 283 (1925) |
| GL-55 | **The Court first defends posting**: "Short of providing personal service, then, posting notice on the door of a person's home would, in many or perhaps most instances, constitute not only a constitutionally acceptable means of service, but indeed a singularly appropriate and effective way of ensuring that a person who cannot conveniently be served personally is actually apprised of proceedings against him." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-56 | **Then the turn**: "But whatever the efficacy of posting in many cases, it is clear that, in the circumstances of this case, merely posting notice on an apartment door does not satisfy minimum standards of due process. In a significant number of instances, reliance on posting pursuant to the provisions of § 454.030 results in a failure to provide actual notice to the tenant concerned." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-57 | Consequence: posting on the apartment door "cannot be considered a 'reliable means of acquainting interested parties of the fact that their rights are before the courts.'" | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B, quoting *Mullane*, 339 U. S., at 315 |
| GL-58 | Missing the tenant once proves nothing: "The failure to effect personal service on the first visit hardly suggests that the tenant has abandoned his interest in the apartment such that mere pro forma notice might be held constitutionally adequate." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-59 | On the mails: they are an "efficient and inexpensive means of communication," and "[n]otice by mail in the circumstances of this case would surely go a long way toward providing the constitutionally required assurance that the State has not allowed its power to be invoked against a person who has had no opportunity to present a defense despite a continuing interest in the resolution of the controversy." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B, quoting *Mullane* at 319 |
| GL-60 | The specific irony the Court noticed: "Particularly where the subject matter of the action also happens to be the mailing address of the defendant, and where personal service is ineffectual, notice by mail may reasonably be relied upon to provide interested persons with actual notice of judicial proceedings." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-61 | The failure named: "the State's continued exclusive reliance on an ineffective means of service is not notice 'reasonably calculated to reach those who could easily be informed by other means at hand.'" | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B, quoting *Mullane* at 319 |
| GL-62 | Answering the dissent's "summary proceeding" argument: "from the perspective of the tenant, it is difficult to see how a means of serving process that fails to afford actual notice in a 'not insubstantial' number of cases can be deemed either prompt or certain." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion n.4 |
| GL-63 | **The holding**: "We conclude that in failing to afford appellees adequate notice of the proceedings against them before issuing final orders of eviction, the State has deprived them of property without the due process of law required by the Fourteenth Amendment. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is therefore Affirmed." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part III |
| GL-64 | The majority was delivered by **Justice Brennan**. | ✓ | opinion byline |
| GL-65 | The dissent was by **Justice O'Connor**, joined by **the Chief Justice** and **Justice Rehnquist** — so at least three Justices dissented. The text of this opinion does **not** print a numeric vote and does not list which Justices joined Brennan. | ✓ | dissent byline + verified absence of any vote tally in the text |

## 7. WHAT THE HOLDING IS **NOT**

| ID | Claim | Grade | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GL-66 | It is **not** a ruling that posting is unconstitutional generally. The Court expressly limited itself: "In light of the findings of the courts below, we hold only that posted notice pursuant to § 454.030 is constitutionally inadequate." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion n.9 |
| GL-67 | It is **not** an order to use the mail: "It is not our responsibility to prescribe the form of service that the Commonwealth should adopt." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion n.9 |
| GL-68 | What the Court would say about mail is only comparative: "we have no hesitation in concluding that posted service accompanied by mail service, is constitutionally preferable to posted service alone" — and it conceded "that process served by mail is far from the ideal means of providing the notice the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion n.9 |
| GL-69 | It is **not** a decision on the in rem / in personam distinction: "As in *Mullane*, we decline to resolve the constitutional question based upon the determination whether the particular action is more properly characterized as one in rem or in personam." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B |
| GL-70 | It is **not** a rejection of the old property presumption in general — the Court reaffirmed it: "It is, of course, reasonable to assume that a property owner will maintain superintendence of his property, and to presume that actions physically disturbing his holdings will come to his attention." The presumption simply broke down on these facts. | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B, citing *Mullane* at 316 |
| GL-71 | It is **not** a final judgment for the three tenants. The Court affirmed a Sixth Circuit judgment that had "reversed the grant of summary judgment ... and remanded the case for further proceedings" (GL-34). What happened on remand is outside this opinion. | ✓ | opinion, Part I + Part III |
| GL-72 | It is **not** a case about rent owed. Appellants conceded that if the landlord had sought past-due rent, "personal service would be required by Kentucky law," and stated that here "the landlord claimed only a right to recover possession." | ✓ **VERBATIM** | opinion, Part II-B, citing Tr. of Oral Arg. 19-21 |

## 10. QUARANTINE — do not use

| ID | Forbidden | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q-01 | Any sentence of the form "today, a landlord can still just tape a paper to your door" or "this is how eviction notice works now." | The case was decided 17 May 1982 and construes a 1975 Kentucky statute. The opinion says nothing about any present-day rule. Use GL-R2 instead. |
| Q-02 | "Because of this case, every State now has to mail eviction notices." | The Court expressly refused to prescribe a form of service (GL-67) and held only that § 454.030 posting was inadequate (GL-66). |

| Q-05 | "They lost their homes." / "She was put out on the street." / any depiction of the three women's eviction, belongings, children or destination. | The opinion never says they were removed (GL-27). It says default judgments were entered and writs of possession served — and even that is their allegation (GL-24). |

## THE SHAPE THE FACTS ALREADY HAVE

1. Three women — Linnie Lindsey, Barbara Hodgens, Pamela Ray — live in a public housing project in Louisville, Kentucky. **GL-01, GL-02**
2. In 1975 their landlord, the Housing Authority of Louisville, moves to take their apartments back, filing forcible entry and detainer actions. **GL-03, GL-04, GL-14**
3. Kentucky law tells the deputy sheriff what to do: knock; if nobody, hand it to a family member over sixteen; if nobody, tack it to the wall. **GL-16, GL-18**
4. In all three cases it went to step three: a writ, on the door, by thumbtack or tape. **GL-17, GL-19**
5. In practice there is no step three-and-a-half. Nobody home in a "good percentage" of visits, and "posting follows forthwith" — no second try, not even at an hour when someone might be in. **GL-20, GL-21**
6. The women say they never saw the papers, and learned of the proceedings only when the writs of possession arrived — after default judgments, after the time to appeal had run out. This is their account; it is never tried. **GL-23, GL-24, GL-25**
7. With nothing left in the state courts, they file a federal class action under § 1983, arguing the notice failed *Mullane*. **GL-26, GL-28, GL-29**
8. The district court rules against them, on the authority of a 1909 case, while conceding in the same breath that conditions have changed and that the testimony that posted notices "are often removed by other tenants" is undisputed. **GL-30, GL-31, GL-32, GL-33**
9. The only evidence anywhere in the case is the process servers' own depositions. They talk about children. They put the writs up high. Village West. "Probably a couple of times." "Oh, we had plenty of trouble." **GL-39, GL-40, GL-41, GL-42**
10. The Sixth Circuit reverses, overrules its own 1909 precedent, and says the time when posting beat mailing has passed — and that mailing would cost Kentucky almost nothing. **GL-34, GL-35, GL-36, GL-37**
11. The Supreme Court takes the appeal. **GL-38**
12. Justice Brennan starts by defending posting: on the door of a person's home it is, in many or perhaps most cases, a singularly appropriate and effective way to reach someone. **GL-55**
13. Then he turns: whatever posting's efficacy in general, on these facts it fails. In a significant number of instances § 454.030 posting simply does not reach the tenant, and the servers knew it. A door is not a reliable means of telling someone their rights are before a court. **GL-43, GL-56, GL-57**
14. What was at stake is named plainly: a significant interest in property — the right to continued residence in their homes. **GL-52**
15. Holding: by not giving adequate notice before final orders of eviction, the State took property without due process. Affirmed. **GL-63**
16. The Court then draws its own border: it holds only that § 454.030 posting is inadequate, refuses to prescribe a remedy, and says merely that posting plus mail beats posting alone. **GL-66, GL-67, GL-68**
17. Three Justices dissent. O'Connor says the Court has made a constitutional rule out of a handful of conflicting depositions, has no evidence about the mails at all, and has overridden eleven legislatures — and that unattended mailboxes get robbed too, while a posted notice at least reached the door. **GL-65, GL-73, GL-74, GL-75, GL-80**
18. The Sixth Circuit judgment stood; the case had been remanded for further proceedings. What happened to Lindsey, Hodgens and Ray afterwards is not in this opinion, and the film does not say. **GL-71, GL-27, Q-05**
