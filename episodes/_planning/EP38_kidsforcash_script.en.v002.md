## ACT 1 — 普通の子供たち（不条理の提示） [S01 S02 S03 S04]

Luzerne County, Pennsylvania. A working town, built on coal that ran out a long time ago. The courthouse in Wilkes-Barre was supposed to be the place a kid who slipped got a second chance. [L]

The children who came through it had done small, ordinary things. A fight in a school hallway. A few dollars of shoplifting. A parody page online. A scuffle at a bus stop. The kind of thing that, almost anywhere else, ends with a stern warning and a ride home.

Here, it ended with handcuffs.

They stood in front of a juvenile judge named Mark Ciavarella. [L: name/role] And here is the detail that should stop you: most of them had no lawyer. They were handed a form that waived their right to one, and — children, frightened, trusting the adults in the room — they signed it. Before Ciavarella, roughly half of the kids who gave up that right were shipped off to a locked facility. Across the rest of the state, the figure was eight point four percent. [L: ~50% vs 8.4% state]

The hearings were fast. A minute, sometimes two. A boy could be found delinquent and pulled out a side door before his mother understood what had happened.

And the parents believed it was justice. Of course they did. Who questions a judge, in a courtroom, under the seal of the state?

That trust was the first thing being sold.

## ACT 2 — 仕組み（金の流れ・因果の解剖） [S05 S07 S08 S09 S15]

To understand what was really happening, you have to follow the money — and it starts one floor up.

There were two judges. Ciavarella, in the juvenile court. And Michael Conahan, the president judge, the man who controlled the county's purse. [L: names/roles]

The county had its own juvenile detention center — public, funded by taxpayers. Conahan quietly choked off its budget and helped push it toward closing. [L: defunding] Now, when Ciavarella wanted to send a child away, there was really only one place left to send them: a brand-new private, for-profit facility. There were two, in the end — PA Child Care, and a sister site to the west. [L: PA Child Care / Western PA Child Care]

A for-profit jail has one simple rule. It makes money only when the beds are full.

And the beds were filled, one child at a time, by the judge downstairs.

The man who built those facilities was a developer named Robert Mericle. The man who co-owned and ran them was an attorney named Robert Powell. Over about three years, the two of them moved roughly two point eight million dollars to the judges. [L: Mericle builder / Powell owner / $2.8M / ~3 yrs] They had names for it — a "finder's fee," a "referral." Money for helping the facilities get built and kept full.

Strip the language away and it is very plain. A child walked into a courtroom. A judge sent that child to a cell. And a payment followed. The waiver-of-counsel rate in that courtroom ran seven to eleven times higher than anywhere else — because a child without a lawyer is a child who does not fight back. [L: 7–11x]

Every hearing that felt too fast was not a bad mood on the bench. It was inventory.

## ACT 3 — 壊れた一家（人間の代償・最も暗い底） [S06 S10 S11 S18]

Numbers this large go numb. So look at one family.

Ed Kenzakoski was seventeen. A wrestler, with a father who had plans for him. He came before Ciavarella on a minor charge, and he was sent away. [L: Edward Kenzakoski, age, offense]

The boy who came back was not the boy who left. He carried it for years — the label, the lost time, the sense that the system had decided who he was at seventeen and would not let him become anyone else. At twenty-three, he took his own life. [L: age/outcome — 自死は事実のみ]

His mother is Sandy Fonzo. [L] And when Ciavarella walked out of a federal courthouse years later — this time as the defendant — she was on the steps waiting for him. She confronted him in front of the cameras, a mother's grief turned to fury, asking the man who ruined her son how he could live with it. He straightened his tie and looked away. There was no answer. There is no answer.

Now multiply that. Thousands of children told, at thirteen, at fifteen, that they were criminals — and, being children, believing it. A record that followed them into every job application, every college form. Some carried the shame for the rest of their lives. That is the part no appeal gives back, and no check ever covers.

## ACT 4 — 暴かれる（転回：司法が司法を裁く） [S12 S13 S14 S16 S17]

For years, it worked — because of who was complaining. A kid with a record. A parent who "should have raised them better." Easy people to ignore. Complaint after complaint went nowhere.

Then a mother made one more call — this time to a small nonprofit in Philadelphia, the Juvenile Law Center. [L] Their lawyers pulled the case files, expecting a handful of harsh sentences. What they found was a machine: hundreds of children, over and over, adjudicated in minutes, without counsel, funneled to the same private beds. [L]

They petitioned the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. And the court did something extraordinary. It reached back and erased Ciavarella's courtroom — vacating thousands of adjudications, wiping the records of as many as several thousand children clean. [L: ~2,251 vacated / up to ~4,000–6,500] The state, in effect, admitting: none of this should ever have counted.

Then the law turned on the men who had sold it. The FBI had been listening for a while. Michael Conahan pleaded guilty to racketeering and was sentenced to seventeen and a half years. [L] Mark Ciavarella went to trial, was convicted on multiple counts of racketeering and corruption, and was sentenced to twenty-eight. [L: 12/39 counts, RICO, 28y] Years later, the two were ordered to pay more than two hundred million dollars to the families they had harmed. [L: $200M+, 2022]

It was, finally, a kind of justice. And it changed the rules — Pennsylvania moved to guarantee that a child in a courtroom actually has a lawyer beside them. [L: reforms/right to counsel] But ask Sandy Fonzo whether any of it was enough.

## ED — 余韻＋射程＋登録CTA [S19 S20 Bookends]

We tell ourselves that a courtroom is the one room where money is supposed to stay outside the door. That the powerful answer to a higher standard than the rest of us.

For thousands of children in one American county, that promise had a price — and it came out to a few thousand dollars a head.

The judges are named and gone now. One went to prison for decades. The other, quietly, years later, had his sentence commuted and walked out early. [L: Conahan commuted 2024] The children keep their years. They don't get those back.

If a story like this should be seen and not buried, subscribe — the next case we tell begins the same way this one did: with an ordinary person, and a system that was never supposed to touch them. Because the line between a family like yours and a family like theirs is thinner than any of us wants to believe.
