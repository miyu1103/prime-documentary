## OPENING (0:49–1:10)

This is what happened in between.

Not the trial. The trial takes four minutes to describe and it is the least surprising part of it. What is worth the time is the seven years before the arrest, and the eleven years after it, and how little either of them required.

## ACT I — THE FIRST MORNING (1:10–6:30)

Connellsville sits in Fayette County, in the south-west corner of Pennsylvania, where the hills start to fold.

It is a small place. Small enough that a case does not stay quiet, because the people in it already know each other.

In the early hours of the twenty-seventh of January, officers from the Connellsville Police Department went to the apartment of a man named Curtis Haith.

They found him outside on the pavement. He had been beaten. He had been shot in the face.

Curtis Haith was twenty-one. He wanted to be a chef.

At the officers' request, the District Attorney came to the apartment herself.

Her name was Nancy Vernon, and she had been the elected District Attorney of Fayette County since the early nineties. She took part in the investigation and helped direct it, that morning and for years afterwards.

That detail matters later, and not for the reason it might seem to. A prosecutor who advocates in court is protected by something called absolute immunity — she cannot be sued for it, however wrong she turns out to be. A prosecutor who acts as an investigator is not protected in the same way. Where the line between those two things falls, in this case, took a federal appeals court twenty years to reach.

Inside, the search recovered DNA samples. It also recovered a significant amount of drug-related evidence.

That second finding gave the investigation its early shape. A man beaten and shot outside an apartment with drugs in it, in a small town, on a night when the apartment had been full of people.

The neighbours told police that Haith had been out that night, and that a dozen or more people had passed through his apartment between the evening of the twenty-sixth and the morning of the twenty-seventh.

So the officers began working through the list. Everyone who had been at those parties. One after another. Where they had been, who they had left with, what time they got home.

That is ordinary police work and there is nothing wrong with it. A dozen people is a dozen people. Some of them will remember badly. Some of them will have reasons of their own to be careful about what they say. The job is to keep going until the accounts either fit together or they do not.

An investigation that begins with a dozen equal names ends with one of them. Something has to do the narrowing.

Physical evidence can do it. A witness can do it. So can nothing at all, if one name simply stays in the file longer than the others and starts to look like an answer.

On the twenty-seventh of January nothing had narrowed anything. Everyone on that list had been somewhere the night before. Everyone had an account. Not one of those accounts had yet been set against anything outside itself.

Where the narrowing came from is the whole of this story.

One of the people on that list was a woman called Crystal Weimer. She had three young daughters, and she was raising the three of them on her own.

When they arrived at her house that morning, she was still wearing the clothes she had worn the night before.

She had a minor injury to her face. She had a minor injury to her foot. There was what looked like mud on her clothes, and what looked like blood.

She told them her account without being asked twice.

She had been at the party. She had given Haith a ride and dropped him at another party. After that she had gone to the housing community where her mother and her sisters lived, and stayed there.

Her cousin confirmed it. Her sisters confirmed it. Michael Gibson, the man she was seeing, confirmed it.

She explained the injuries too. The foot was from horseplaying with Gibson a few days earlier. The eye, and the blood on the shirt, were from a fight with Gibson.

That was the account she gave on the twenty-seventh of January, two thousand and one. Where she was. Who she was with. How she got the marks.

It is worth being precise about what happened to that account over the following fifteen years, because the answer is nothing. She did not revise it. She did not offer a second version when the first one stopped working, because it never stopped working. Every other account in this case moves. Hers does not.

Then the laboratory results came back.

The blood on Crystal Weimer's clothes was Michael Gibson's blood. Exactly as she had said.

None of the DNA collected from the crime scene matched her. Not one sample.

And the samples that were there suggested something else. They suggested a male profile.

That is the whole of the physical evidence, on the twenty-seventh of January, two thousand and one.

It points away from her. It does not point at her once.

And it corroborated her, twice over. She had said the blood was Gibson's, and it was Gibson's. She had said she had not been there when Haith was killed, and nothing at the scene carried her.

A laboratory result has a property that nothing else in this case has. It does not change with retelling, and it was produced before anybody had a theory to protect.

There are two findings inside it and they do different work.

The first is an exclusion. None of the crime-scene samples were hers, and what was there suggested a male profile. An exclusion is not proof that a person was somewhere else. It does mean the scene carried nothing of her.

The second is a confirmation, and it is the rarer of the two. She had named the source of the blood on her own shirt on the first morning, before any testing existed. The testing agreed with her.

A false account does not usually hand the laboratory the one thing that can break it.

⟨BEAT⟩

And then nothing happened.

The file stayed open. The investigation did not close and it did not move. Winter went. Spring came. The town did what towns do.

Nobody was charged. Nobody was cleared either, and those are not the same thing. Being uncharged is not a finding. It is an absence. The people who had been interviewed that January stayed interviewed, and stayed in a small town where everyone knew who the police had spoken to.

Curtis Haith's family waited. That is the part of an unsolved killing that does not get filmed. Somebody's son had been shot in the face on a pavement, and the months went past, and there was nothing to tell them.

Twenty months went by like that.

## ACT II — TWENTY MONTHS (6:30–12:00)

In October of two thousand and two, a man named Thomas Beal went to the police.

Weimer had dated Beal before she was seeing Gibson. What he told officers was that Weimer and Gibson had killed Curtis Haith.

According to Beal, Weimer had told him something specific. She had told him that the blood on her clothes was Haith's.

The DNA had already established, twenty months earlier, that the blood on her clothes was Gibson's.

So the first statement against her contained a fact that the evidence had already ruled out.

⟨HELD⟩

There was a second half to what Beal reported, and the two halves are not the same kind of claim.

The first half was the blood. That is checkable, and it had been checked, twenty months before he said it.

The second half was that Weimer and Gibson had killed Curtis Haith. Nothing can be set beside that. No sample answers it, and no record answers it, and no document answers it either way.

So the statement arrived in two pieces. One of them failed on the day it was made. The other could not be made to fail, because nothing could reach it — and that was the piece that stayed in the case for the next three and a half years.

⟨BEAT⟩

There is a straightforward test available at that moment, and it costs nothing. A witness reports a confession. The confession contains a checkable detail. Check it. If the detail is wrong, the witness either misremembered, or was told wrong, or is making it up — and you find out which before you build on him.

The detail was wrong. Beal was not set aside. The investigation now had a direction, and the direction was a woman whose physical evidence had cleared her twenty months earlier.

Around the same time, the Pennsylvania State Police Cold Case Squad began to assist.

A cold case unit exists because old files go stale in a particular way. The people who worked them move on, memories harden, and the evidence stops being re-examined. So a new team comes in with fresh eyes and looks again at what is already in the box.

The strength of that is obvious. The weakness is less obvious and it is this: a fresh look at old material is still a look at old material. Nothing arrives. What changes is who is reading it, and what they are reading it for.

New people. The same file. And now a name to test it against.

One of them went back through the autopsy photographs of Curtis Haith's body. And on his hand, she saw something she believed was a bite mark.

Those photographs were not new. They were taken at the autopsy, at the start of the case, and they had been in the file ever since.

The bite mark enters this case in the autumn of two thousand and two.

Nothing about the photograph changed in between. What changed was who was reading it, and what else was sitting in the file beside it by then.

That is not a criticism of looking again. Looking again is what a cold case squad is for, and sometimes the second reader is the one who is right. It only fixes the order of events. The accusation came first.

A bite mark is not a fingerprint, and it was not treated as one even then.

The comparison works by taking impressions of a suspect's teeth and matching the pattern to the injury. So it depends on how the mark was left, and on how the skin behaved afterwards. And it depends on the judgement of the person doing the looking.

It is the judgement that carries it.

A dentist in Fayette County was asked to analyse the injury.

She looked at it. She compared it against teeth impressions. And she reached a conclusion.

Her conclusion was that Michael Gibson had bitten Curtis Haith.

Then she was given impressions of Crystal Weimer's teeth as well, and asked to look again.

After that comparison, she reported that she could not identify which set of teeth had caused the mark.

Read that sequence slowly, because the order of it is the whole point. The first look produced a name. The second look, with more material, produced less certainty rather than more. The honest answer, and she gave it, was that the mark did not tell her who made it.

That should have been the end of the bite mark. An examiner looked at the physical evidence twice, with more information the second time, and reported that it did not identify anybody. A piece of evidence that cannot distinguish between two people is not evidence against either of them.

⟨HELD⟩

That is where the dentist stopped. She said she could not tell.

So a different expert was brought in.

⟨BEAT⟩

Here is what that expert was given to work with.

Photographs of the injury to Haith's hand. Teeth impressions from Gibson and from Weimer.

And Thomas Beal's statement.

He read the accusation, and then he examined the evidence.

That order is not a small thing. The point of a physical comparison is that it can disagree with the story. If the story arrives first, the comparison has been given somewhere to land before it has looked at anything.

Set the two examinations beside each other.

The dentist had the injury and two sets of impressions. She produced a name, then withdrew it, and ended with no identification at all.

The second expert had photographs of the same injury and the same two sets of impressions. One item in his materials was not in hers. It was Thomas Beal's statement, and a statement is not physical evidence. It cannot be compared with anything. What it can do is tell a reader which of the two available answers is the one being looked for.

Same injury. The same two sets of teeth. One extra document, and a different result.

His conclusion was that the bite mark matched Crystal Weimer.

⟨HELD⟩

One dentist had said Gibson. Then she had said she could not tell. The next expert said Weimer, and he had been given the name first.

That is the physical case. There is nothing else in it.

## ACT III — NOTHING NEW (12:00–18:00)

A problem surfaced with the bite mark, and it was a serious one.

Nobody could say when it had happened.

A bite on a hand could have been left hours before a man died. It could have been left days before. If it was days before, it proved nothing about the night of the murder at all.

So the question of timing was put back to the investigation. Vernon directed officers to look into it, and the expert was asked to update his opinion.

He did.

He determined that the bite had occurred seven to ten minutes before Curtis Haith's death.

⟨HELD⟩

He reached that conclusion without reviewing any additional evidence.

⟨HELD⟩

Nothing new had been found. Nothing new had been tested. The same photographs, the same impressions, the same man. A question was asked, and the answer moved.

Seven to ten minutes. Close enough to put her there at the moment he died.

Consider what that number would require.

Not that the bite happened around the same time as the killing. That it happened inside a window of about three minutes.

Identified from a photograph of a mark on skin. By a man who had already given his opinion once, and was being asked whether he could make it fit.

There is a version of this that would have been unremarkable. An expert goes back to his materials, finds something in them he had not weighed the first time, reports that it narrows the timing, and says what he looked at to get there. That happens. It is ordinary work.

The record does not describe that. It describes an opinion updated without additional evidence.

An opinion that can move without new material can move again. Whatever produced that number, it was not the material, because the material was identical before and after.

The record of this case does not say what the divers found in the pond. It does not need to. By the time they were in the water, the document that sent them there had already been disowned by the man whose name was on it, and it had already contradicted the man who produced it.

⟨HELD⟩

Three accusations now. Beal, who had described blood the DNA had already ruled out, and then added a man who was in prison. Blair, whose document contradicted him. Stenger, who had not written what was attributed to him.

None of it agreed with any of the rest of it.

And it is worth saying what was not there. No physical evidence tying Crystal Weimer to the scene — that had been settled in two thousand and one. No confession from her. No weapon. No witness who put her at the apartment when Haith was killed. What existed was three men who had come forward at different times with incompatible accounts, and one comparison whose timing had been adjusted after the fact.

There is a pattern in where those men were standing when they spoke. Beal came in of his own accord, twenty months late, with a detail that had already been disproved. Blair was in prison. Stenger was in prison. Everything that pointed at Crystal Weimer came from someone with a reason to be talking to the police, and nothing that pointed at her came from the scene.

None of that makes any of them liars. People in custody tell the truth all the time. It only means that each account needed checking against something outside itself — and each time one was checked, it failed.

A preliminary hearing is not a trial. It is a low bar, deliberately: the Commonwealth has only to show that a crime happened and that there is enough to believe this defendant did it. Cases are not supposed to end there.

She was defended by the Fayette County Public Defender's Office. A private defence would have cost around fifty thousand dollars, and she did not have fifty thousand dollars. That is not unusual and it is not a scandal on its own. It only means that everything that follows happened to somebody who could not buy her way out of any part of it.

At that hearing, the Commonwealth called Thomas Beal to the stand as a witness of fact.

The trial ran in Fayette County in the spring of two thousand and six.

Third-degree murder, in Pennsylvania, is a killing done with malice but without a specific intent to kill. Conspiracy means an agreement with somebody else to commit the crime — although in this case no other person was ever convicted of any part of it.

## ACT V — WHAT CAME OUT (24:00–28:00)

On the first of October, two thousand and fifteen, a judge vacated Crystal Weimer's convictions and granted her a new trial.

Vacating a conviction does not declare anybody innocent. It sets the verdict aside and returns the case to where it was before the trial. In principle the Commonwealth can simply try again.

So on the first of October, two thousand and fifteen, Crystal Weimer was not exonerated. She was back at the start, nine years older, with the case against her still notionally alive.

What that decision did do was open the file to people who had not seen it before. And over the next nine months, one piece at a time, the case came apart in public.

⟨BEAT⟩

Joseph Stenger recanted.

He conceded that he had known nothing about Curtis Haith's murder. And he said that police had walked him through his testimony.

⟨BEAT⟩

The bite-mark expert disavowed what he had told the jury.

The opinion he had given — that the mark on Curtis Haith's hand was Crystal Weimer's — he now described in two words.

⟨HELD⟩
"Junk science." His words, about his own evidence.

⟨HELD⟩

That is not this film's assessment. That is the man who gave the evidence, describing the evidence he gave.

⟨BEAT⟩

Then Weimer's lawyers went through the District Attorney's files, and they found letters.

The letters were from jailhouse informants. The same informants who had testified against her at trial.

At trial, those witnesses had told the jury that they were not receiving anything in exchange for their testimony.

The letters showed that they had asked for deals. And that they may have received them.

There is one question the law expects a jury to ask about a jailhouse witness. Are you getting anything for this.

That jury asked it, and they were given an answer, and the answer was not the whole of it.

⟨BEAT⟩

Set the withdrawals in order, because by the middle of two thousand and sixteen there was very little left holding the verdict up.

Beal took his account back in two thousand and four, on the stand, before the trial existed.

Stenger took his back, and said he had known nothing at all.

The expert took back the opinion he had given the jury.

And the men who told that jury they were getting nothing had written to ask for something.

Every one of those was removed by the person who had supplied it. Not by a defence lawyer finding a hole in it. Not by a new discovery about the night of the murder. By the source, in each case, saying it had not been so.

⟨BEAT⟩

And an expert was asked to do something nobody had done since the beginning.

He read Curtis Haith's autopsy report. Then he looked at the photographs taken of Crystal Weimer's injuries on the morning of the twenty-seventh of January, two thousand and one.

His conclusion was that her injuries were consistent with her account. The horseplaying with Gibson, a few days before the murder.

⟨HELD⟩

On the twenty-seventh of June, two thousand and sixteen, the charges against Crystal Weimer were dropped with prejudice.

That is the strongest form the ending of a case can take. A case can be dropped and refiled later; a case dropped with prejudice cannot. The Commonwealth gave up the right to bring that charge against her again.

⟨BEAT⟩

In September of two thousand and seventeen, Crystal Weimer filed a civil rights lawsuit.

She named Fayette County. The Office of the Fayette County District Attorney. The City of Connellsville. Several Connellsville police officers. One Pennsylvania State Police officer. And Nancy Vernon.

That case has taken years of its own, and most of the years have gone on a question that is not about what happened to her at all. It is about who can be sued.

In two thousand and twenty, a federal appeals court took up what it called "only a narrow sliver of this sweeping case". Not whether the allegations were true. Whether Vernon could be required to answer them at all.

The court drew the line where the law draws it. Approving the criminal complaint was prosecuting, and for that she was absolutely immune. But where Weimer alleged that Vernon had acted as an investigator — going to the scene, directing officers, assembling a dive team — absolute immunity did not cover it.

On two of the claims the court went the other way, and held that Vernon was entitled to qualified immunity, including for directing officers to investigate the timing of the bite mark.

Qualified immunity is a different instrument from the absolute kind, and the two are easy to run together. Absolute immunity attaches to a function. If the act was prosecuting, nothing about how it was done comes into it. Qualified immunity attaches to the state of the law. An official is shielded from a damages claim unless the conduct broke a rule that was already clearly established at the time.

So that half of the ruling is narrower than it sounds. It is not a finding that the conduct was proper. It is a finding about what the law had settled by then.

⟨HELD⟩

One more thing about that decision, and it matters for everything you have heard. On an appeal at that stage a court does not decide what is true. It takes the plaintiff's account as given and asks whether, if it were all true, the case could go forward. The court said so directly. It accepted the facts as pleaded and drew the inferences in her favour.

So the account in this film is the account a federal appeals court was working from, and the dates and the documents inside it — the DNA, the recantations, the letters, the disavowal — are on the record. What is not settled is what anybody intended.
