## HOOK
There is a gauge in this story. It is about the size of a pocket watch, it costs less than a good pair of boots, and it has one needle. <!-- AK-301 -->

For eleven minutes on a January afternoon in 2000, two pilots fought an aeroplane that would not let them climb, level, or slow down. <!-- AK-006, AK-013 -->

The thing that failed had been measured. Once. Two and a half years earlier. <!-- AK-401 -->

So why is there no record of what that measurement said?

## ACT_1 — ELEVEN MINUTES
Start with the mechanism, because the mechanism is simple enough to hold in one hand.

The tail of an airliner is not fixed. The whole horizontal surface at the back tilts, a few degrees either way, to trim the aeroplane so it will fly level without the pilot holding the controls.

On a McDonnell Douglas MD-80, that surface is moved by a single steel screw standing vertically inside the fin. A motor turns the screw. A nut travels along it. The nut is attached to the fin, the top of the screw is attached to the tail surface, and so the tail surface goes up and down as the screw turns. <!-- AK-301, AK-505 -->

Two threads run in a spiral along that screw. Each one makes sixteen full turns. Thirty-two turns of steel, in total, holding the tail of an aeroplane onto the rest of it. <!-- AK-505 -->

That is the whole system. One screw. One nut. Two threads.

It is the thirty-first of January, 2000. Alaska Airlines flight 261, an MD-83 registered November nine six three Alpha Sierra, leaves Puerto Vallarta in Mexico for San Francisco and then Seattle. <!-- AK-001 -->

The load sheet records ten passengers in first class, seventy in coach, and five crew. <!-- AK-002 -->

Eighty-eight people.

The aeroplane is carrying just under thirty-five thousand pounds of fuel. <!-- AK-003 -->

At about ten to four in the afternoon, over the Pacific, the autopilot disconnects. <!-- AK-005 -->

The tail has stopped moving.

Not slipped. Not drifted. Stopped. The crew press the trim switches on the control wheels and nothing happens. They try the alternate system, on the centre pedestal, and nothing happens there either.

Later, on the radio to the company, the captain describes it exactly as an engineer would want it described. It appears to be jammed, the whole thing. It spikes out when we use the primary — we get an electrical load that tells him the motor is trying to run, but the brake will not move it. When they use the alternate, nothing at all. <!-- AK-016 -->

That description is correct. It is also useless, because nobody on the ground can work out why.

The crew now have a problem that is not in any checklist. The aeroplane is flying, but the trim is stuck at a setting for cruise, and they need it at a setting for landing. They can hold it with the control column, but they cannot hold it for ever.

At nineteen minutes past four, the first officer tells a controller where they are and what is wrong, and the sentence is remarkably calm for what it contains. We have a jammed stabilizer and we are maintaining altitude with difficulty. Our intention is to land at Los Angeles. <!-- AK-007 -->

The controller offers them a lower altitude. The captain answers with a plan. I need to get down about ten, change my configuration, make sure I can control the jet — and I'd like to do that out here over the bay if I may. <!-- AK-008 -->

Read that again as an engineer would. He wants to descend, put the aeroplane into the shape it will be in when it lands, and find out whether it is still flyable — over water, away from anything, before he brings it near a city.

That is not panic. That is the correct order of operations.

What is happening inside the fin while he says it is that the nut has almost no threads left.

⟨HELD⟩

At about nine minutes past four, twenty minutes after the autopilot let go, the aeroplane pitches over for the first time. <!-- AK-006 -->

This is the part most accounts of this accident skip, and it is the part that decides everything afterwards.

They get it back.

Two pilots, in an aeroplane whose tail has begun to move on its own, pull it out of a dive and hold it. And then they keep flying it, at a lower altitude, for ten more minutes, while the tail sits at a nose-down setting that requires constant force on the controls to fight. <!-- AK-014 -->

Ten minutes is a long time to hold an aeroplane that is trying to go down.

There is no autopilot doing it. The autopilot let go twenty minutes ago and it is not coming back. <!-- AK-005 -->

So it is being done with two hands on a control column, against a tail that is set wrong and will not move.

That is arm strength, sustained, while running an approach checklist, talking to a controller, talking to the company, and deciding what to do.

Whatever else is true about the last eleven minutes of this flight, that is what the two of them were doing for all of it.

And it is long enough to have a conversation about what to do next — which is exactly what they do, and which is why the recorder has the exchange it has.

For those ten minutes the aeroplane holds. Then, in the last minute, there is a conversation on the recorder that this programme is going to quote in full, because paraphrasing it would be a kind of lie.

The captain suggests trying to unload the aeroplane — pitching up and letting the nose fall through — to see whether the tail will move when there is no weight on it.

The first officer answers: you mean use this again? I don't think we should if it can fly. <!-- AK-009 -->

Eighteen seconds later he says: I think if it's controllable, we oughta just try to land it. <!-- AK-010 -->

And the captain replies: you think so? OK, let's head for LA. <!-- AK-011 -->

They have decided to stop experimenting. They are going to fly it in.

About five seconds after that, the recorder captures at least four distinct thumps. <!-- AK-012 -->

Twenty seconds after the captain agrees, the aeroplane pitches over. <!-- AK-013 -->

The tail surface has moved to at least fourteen degrees leading edge up — which puts the nose straight down — and it has done it in less time than it takes to say so. <!-- AK-014 -->

The aeroplane remains near inverted, its nose oscillating downward, until it reaches the ocean about two and a half miles north of Anacapa Island. <!-- AK-015, AK-001 -->

Nobody survives.

## ACT_2 — WHAT WAS LEFT OF THE THREADS
The National Transportation Safety Board's finding is short, and every clause in it points at something other than the two people in the cockpit.

Probable cause: a loss of airplane pitch control resulting from the in-flight failure of the horizontal stabilizer trim system jackscrew assembly's acme nut threads. The thread failure was caused by excessive wear resulting from Alaska Airlines' insufficient lubrication of the jackscrew assembly. <!-- AK-101 -->

Then three contributing factors.

The airline's extended lubrication interval, and the Federal Aviation Administration's approval of that extension. <!-- AK-102 -->

The airline's extended end play check interval, and the FAA's approval of that extension. <!-- AK-103 -->

And the absence, on this type of aeroplane, of a fail-safe mechanism to prevent the catastrophic effects of total thread loss. <!-- AK-104 -->

One cause. Three contributing factors. Two of which are approvals.

Now the wreckage, because it says something that no interval on a piece of paper can.

The threads of the nut were recovered. They were wrapped around the screw. <!-- AK-501 -->

About ninety per cent of the thread thickness had worn away before what was left sheared off. <!-- AK-502 -->

Ninety per cent.

Here is the number that makes that one land. A jackscrew worn to the absolute maximum the rules allow in service — the point at which it must come off the aeroplane — has about twenty-two per cent of its thread thickness gone. <!-- AK-503 -->

Twenty-two per cent is the limit. This one was at ninety.

The inside of the recovered nut had no intact threads at all. Only small ridges where threads used to be attached. <!-- AK-504 -->

And to shear all thirty-two turns of a nut in that condition, all at once, would take loads of more than a hundred and ninety thousand pounds — far more than an aeroplane in flight can generate. <!-- AK-505 -->

Which means it did not happen all at once. It happened gradually, over years, and then the last of it let go.

That distinction is worth staying with, because it changes what kind of story this is.

A part that snaps is an event. Somebody made a bad batch of steel, or somebody hit it with something, and there is a moment you can point to and a person you can find.

A part that wears is not an event. It is an arithmetic problem running quietly in the background of an aeroplane that is otherwise completely normal. Every flight takes a little more off the threads. Nothing is ever wrong on any particular day.

And a wearing part is exactly the kind of failure that maintenance is designed to catch, because you do not need to predict the moment. You only need to look often enough, and measure well enough, to see the number moving before it arrives.

So there were two things standing between eighty-eight people and that nut.

The grease, which slows the wear down.

And the measurement, which tells you how far it has got.

Both of them were working exactly as designed on the day this aeroplane was built.

How does a part get four times past its own scrap limit without anyone seeing it?

## ACT_3 — THE INTERVAL
Before the intervals, the reason for the intervals — because the thing that went wrong here is a good idea, and it is important to say why it is a good idea before showing what it did.

An airline cannot take an aeroplane apart every week. Maintenance is downtime, and downtime is an aeroplane that carries nobody. So the industry does something sensible: it works out, from data, how often each task actually needs doing, and it does it that often and no more often. Over-maintenance is not free either — every time a person opens a panel, there is a chance of putting it back wrong.

So intervals get reviewed, and sometimes they get extended, and an extension is not by itself evidence of anything.

Now the intervals.

The manufacturer's original recommendation was to grease this screw every six hundred to nine hundred flight hours. <!-- AK-201 -->

In March 1985, Alaska Airlines greased it every seven hundred flight hours. That is inside the recommendation. <!-- AK-202 -->

In March 1987, they tightened it. Every five hundred hours. Better than the manufacturer asked for. <!-- AK-203 -->

Hold on to that, because 1987 is the high-water mark. Everything after it goes one way.

In July 1988, the check that carried the task was eliminated and the task moved elsewhere: every thousand flight hours. <!-- AK-204 -->

In February 1991, every twelve hundred. <!-- AK-205 -->

In December 1994, every sixteen hundred. <!-- AK-206 -->

And in July 1996, the task came off the flight-hour schedule altogether and went onto a card with a maximum interval of eight months — with no flight-hour limit attached to it at all. At the rate those aeroplanes were flying, eight months was about two thousand five hundred and fifty hours. <!-- AK-207 -->

The Board puts the whole staircase into one sentence. At the time of the accident, Alaska Airlines' lubrication interval for the jackscrew assembly was more than four hundred per cent greater than it was in 1987. <!-- AK-208 -->

And then it adds something quieter, which is the part that matters.

The investigation did not determine what information, if any, was presented as justification for the extensions of 1988, 1991 and 1994. <!-- AK-209 -->

The Board is not saying the justification was bad. It is saying it could not find one.

There is one more thing about that last step, the eight-month card, and it is the detail that separates this airline from every other airline in the country. At the time it was adopted, Alaska Airlines was the only United States carrier whose lubrication interval was calendar time alone — with no flight-hour limit, and no whichever comes first. <!-- AK-210 -->

The Board explains the problem in one line that anyone can check against common sense. Wear tracks flight hours. It does not track calendar time. <!-- AK-211 -->

An aeroplane that flies harder wears faster. A calendar does not know that.

And one detail about how the recommended interval itself came to be relaxed. When the industry group revised the maintenance guidance, the manufacturer's original six-hundred-to-nine-hundred-hour figure was not considered at all. <!-- AK-212 -->

The number everyone had moved away from was not weighed and rejected. It was simply no longer in the room.

Now, was any of this a secret? Was there some piece of knowledge that the industry did not have?

No. And this is the part that is hard to sit through.

On the twenty-ninth of May, 1984 — sixteen years before this accident — McDonnell Douglas issued a service letter to operators. It reported three cases where a jackscrew assembly had come off an aeroplane far earlier than expected, because of excessive play between the screw and the nut. <!-- AK-705 -->

The assemblies were sent back to the manufacturer and taken apart. Every nut showed severe wear on the thread surfaces. <!-- AK-705 -->

And the letter records what they found in the lubrication passages of two of them. The grease was dry, and without evidence of recent renewal. <!-- AK-705 -->

Dry. In 1984.

The letter concluded that inadequate lubrication was the most probable cause of the wear, and it asked operators to keep to a conscientious lubrication programme — at six-hundred-flight-hour intervals. <!-- AK-705 -->

That is the manufacturer, in writing, sixteen years early, describing the exact failure sequence that would eventually bring down flight 261, and naming the exact remedy.

In 1990 another airline reported a nut worn past the forty-thousandths limit after five thousand seven hundred and eighty flight hours — a measurable wear rate, in inches per thousand hours, that anybody could have multiplied out. <!-- AK-706 -->

None of this was hidden. It was circulated.

⟨HELD⟩

So much for the greasing. Now the checking — because there was a second line of defence, and it is the one this film is really about.

You cannot see the threads inside the nut without taking the whole assembly off the aeroplane. So the manufacturer designed a way to measure the wear without removing anything.

You clamp a fixture to the tail surface and apply a specified torque, which reverses the load on the screw. That lets the screw move slightly inside the nut — and the more worn the threads, the more it moves. You measure that movement with a dial indicator. <!-- AK-301 -->

The gauge with the one needle.

If the reading is between three thousandths of an inch and forty thousandths, the assembly stays on the aeroplane. Above forty, it comes off. <!-- AK-302 -->

That is the entire safety net. A number, in thousandths of an inch, read off a needle.

The manufacturer originally wanted that check every three thousand six hundred flight hours. <!-- AK-303 -->

Later guidance relaxed it to about seven thousand hours, or thirty months. <!-- AK-304 -->

Alaska Airlines, in March 1985: every five thousand flight hours. <!-- AK-305 -->

By July 1988: every twenty-six calendar months, about six thousand four hundred hours. <!-- AK-306 -->

By the time of the accident: every thirty months. About nine thousand five hundred and fifty flight hours. <!-- AK-308 -->

So the greasing interval had grown by four hundred per cent, and the check that would have caught the consequences of not greasing enough had grown alongside it.

And then there is the sentence that removes the floor from underneath all of it.

Before July 2000 - five months after this accident - an end play measurement that came out inside the limits was not required to be recorded anywhere. <!-- AK-309 -->

The thing that changed it has a number. Airworthiness Directive 2000-15-15, issued that July. After it, an in-limits reading goes in the file like any other. <!-- AK-902 -->

Read that slowly.

If the needle read thirty-nine thousandths, the aeroplane passed, and the number went nowhere. Nobody wrote it down, because nobody had to.

Which means there was no way to compare one check against the next. No way to see a number creeping up over the years. No way for the next mechanic, or the next inspector, or an investigator two and a half years later, to open a file and find out what the last person saw.

A pass produced silence, and silence is indistinguishable from a good result.

Think about what that does to the two intervals together.

The grease goes on less often. So the threads wear faster.

The check happens less often. So the wear runs longer before anyone looks.

And when somebody does look, the number is not kept. So the next person starts from nothing.

Three decisions. Each one defensible on its own. Each one signed off by somebody who was doing their job.

The first was about cost and downtime. The second was about cost and downtime. The third was not about cost at all — it was about paperwork, and about not burdening mechanics with recording results that were fine.

That third one is the quietest and it is the worst.

Because a maintenance programme does not actually run on inspections. It runs on trends. One reading tells you almost nothing. Two readings, years apart, tell you the rate. And the rate is the only thing that can warn you before the limit arrives.

Nobody in this system was collecting rates.

They were collecting pass and fail. And this assembly passed.

## ACT_4 — THE ONLY ONE THEY HAD
The accident aeroplane's last end play check was on the twenty-seventh of September, 1997. <!-- AK-401 -->

So what does that check actually involve, for the person doing it?

You are up a stand, inside the tail of an aeroplane. You clamp a fixture on. You apply a torque. Somebody else watches the dial while you do it, because you cannot pull and read at the same time. You do it several times, until the readings settle. <!-- AK-301 -->

It is a two-person job with one number at the end of it.

And the whole thing rests on an assumption so obvious that nobody states it out loud: that the fixture is the right shape.

If the fixture is the right shape, the torque produces the movement the manufacturer expects, and the needle tells the truth.

If it is not, the needle still moves. It still settles. It still gives you a number.

It just gives you the wrong one.

After the accident, investigators interviewed the mechanic who performed the check.

He said: I don't remember the exact number, but it was below forty thousandths, I believe. <!-- AK-402 -->

He said he had done about six of these checks before, and that the ones he had done were well below the limits. <!-- AK-403 -->

He could not recall how many measurements were taken. <!-- AK-404 -->

It would be easy, and wrong, to make this man the ending of the story. He is not. He was never charged with anything, and neither was anyone else. Hold that, because it comes back.

He was doing a procedure with the equipment his employer gave him. So look at the equipment.

At the time of the accident, Alaska Airlines had exactly one restraining fixture in its entire inventory — the tool you clamp to the tail to make the measurement possible. One, at its Oakland maintenance base. <!-- AK-405 -->

It had been made in-house. And it did not meet the manufacturer's required specifications. <!-- AK-405 -->

About a month after the accident, the airline made eleven more, to the same in-house design. <!-- AK-406 -->

The manager of tool control, who helped make them, told investigators what he thought of them at the time. What they were making was not even close to Boeing's engineering drawing requirements. And then he said the other half of it: we were directed to build the tools, and we did exactly what we were told. <!-- AK-407 -->

That is a man describing a system, not confessing to a crime.

On the second of August, 2000, Alaska Airlines notified the Federal Aviation Administration that its in-house fixtures might not be an equivalent substitute for the manufacturer's fixture — and that they could produce erroneous measurements. <!-- AK-408 -->

The airline then suspended their use and temporarily grounded eighteen aeroplanes. <!-- AK-409 -->

Eighteen aeroplanes, out of service, because of a doubt about a clamp.

⟨HELD⟩

There is one more thing the investigation could not do, and it is the sentence that closes this act.

The Safety Board could not test the actual fixture used for the accident aeroplane's last end play check. By the time they came to it, it was not in serviceable condition. <!-- AK-410 -->

So the number from 1997 was never written down, and the tool that produced it could no longer be examined.

Investigators asked the airline for information about that fixture on the twenty-sixth of June, 2000. The airline provided it on the second of August - the same day it told the regulator the fixtures might be wrong. <!-- AK-411 -->

A federal grand jury looked at all of this. In December 2001, prosecutors stated that no charges would be filed against Alaska Airlines. <!-- AK-901 -->

That is worth stating early rather than saving, because it changes what kind of story the rest of this is. Nobody was charged. Not the airline, not a manager, not the mechanic. Whatever failed here did not resolve into a person a prosecutor could name.

For its part, the airline's own submission to the Board argued that the tool used in 1997 had been made by the manufacturer after all. Its evidence for that was the mechanic's deposition, taken in 2002 in the civil litigation, in which he said he thought it was. <!-- AK-412 -->

Thought. Two and a half years after the check, five years after the tool was picked up.

That is not evasion. It is what is left when nobody wrote anything down.

Now, before this becomes a film about one airline, the investigation went and looked at other people's aeroplanes. What it found is the reason this story matters beyond Alaska Airlines.

Seventeen days after the accident, a jackscrew was pulled off a Hawaiian Airlines DC-9 after an on-wing reading of forty-three thousandths of an inch — past the limit. Cleaned up and measured properly on a bench, it read forty-eight. <!-- AK-702 -->

Then the laboratory looked at the grease from two Hawaiian assemblies and found pink, sandlike grains in it. Chemical analysis identified them. They matched the Australian garnet the airline used to blast corrosion off aircraft structure. <!-- AK-703 -->

Abrasive grit, in the grease, on the threads. Nobody put it there on purpose. It got in because that is what happens in a hangar where one job produces dust and another job requires a clean surface.

And then the widest number in the report. Boeing gave investigators the bench measurements of every jackscrew assembly sent for overhaul between 1996 and 1999. Two hundred and fourteen of them. <!-- AK-704 -->

Thirteen came in at forty-five thousandths or more. Five at fifty-five or more. And one, from an aeroplane originally delivered new to Delta, measured seventy-five thousandths — nearly twice the limit at which an assembly is supposed to leave service. <!-- AK-704 -->

None of those aeroplanes crashed. Every one of them was flying with a part further gone than the rules allowed, and the system found out about it only when the part happened to arrive on a bench.

⟨HELD⟩

After the accident, Alaska Airlines hired a consulting firm and had itself inspected. A team of thirteen specialists went through the airline from the tenth of April to the nineteenth of May, 2000. Their report is dated the nineteenth of June, and its stated purpose was to examine the airline's safety culture. <!-- AK-801 -->

An airline can commission that in eight weeks. The interval extensions took fifteen years.

And what does the design say about all this? Because a design is a set of assumptions, written down, and these assumptions are on the record.

The certification documents show the assembly was required to survive the loss of ninety per cent of its threads. That was achieved by giving it more threads than it structurally needed. <!-- AK-601 -->

It was also required to survive the total failure of one entire thread spiral — which is why there are two. A Boeing engineer put it plainly at the public hearing: if one thread fails, the other carries the load. <!-- AK-602 -->

Both assumptions are reasonable. Both were met.

But when the Board went back to the original fault analysis, written in April 1965 and revised as recently as 1997, it found no contingency at all for the complete loss of the nut threads. <!-- AK-603 -->

Ninety per cent gone: covered. One whole spiral gone: covered. All of it gone: not considered.

And the reason it was not considered is a piece of paperwork. The FAA's senior certification engineer testified that the assembly was part structure and part system — and that the nut was not classed as a system, and so was not required to meet the certification requirements that apply to systems. <!-- AK-604 -->

A part that holds the tail of an aeroplane on was, for the purposes of the rules, not a system.

That sounds like a trick. It is not. It is a boundary.

Aircraft certification splits the aeroplane in two. Structure is the metal that holds shape and carries load — wings, spars, frames. Systems are the things that do something — hydraulics, electrics, controls.

Structure is proved by strength. Systems are proved by asking what happens when they fail.

The rule that says a single failure must never be catastrophic belongs to the systems half.

And this assembly sits across the line. It is a moving mechanism. It is also load-bearing metal. The screw was treated as structure, and structure is not required to answer the failure question.

So nobody ever asked it.

Not because anyone decided the answer did not matter. Because the form the question comes on was for a different half of the aeroplane.

⟨HELD⟩

The Board's conclusion is one of the bluntest things in the report. Catastrophic single-point failure modes should be prohibited in the design of all future aeroplanes with these systems — regardless of whether some element of them is classed as structure rather than system. <!-- AK-605 -->

## ENDING
Go back to the gauge.

The needle sits near zero. The bezel is clean. It costs less than a good pair of boots and it has exactly one job, which is to tell a person how much of something is left. <!-- AK-301 -->

That is what it looks like when everything is correct. It looks like a needle, not moving.

Nobody was charged, and that is not a scandal. It is the shape of the thing. There was no single person who could have been charged, because the failure was distributed across fifteen years of decisions that were each, on their own, defensible.

The greasing interval was extended four times, and each extension was approved. <!-- AK-202, AK-207, AK-102 -->

The check that would have caught the wear was extended too, and that was approved. <!-- AK-308, AK-103 -->

The measurement was taken with the only tool the airline owned, and that tool did not meet the specification. <!-- AK-405 -->

Tonight, somewhere, a mechanic is clamping a fixture to the tail of an aeroplane and watching a needle settle.

And whatever it says, it goes in the file.

Somebody will be able to look it up.

That is the whole difference.
