## THE SHAPE THE FACTS ALREADY HAVE

Not a claim — a note for the writer. **The document is five pages long and the story is already in
order. The only structural work is refusing to add anything.**

```
A family member of a patient requiring extensive nursing care
  signed an agreement with a nursing home on behalf of the patient.        (MB-07)

Inside it: arbitrate everything —
  except claims to collect late payments owed by the patient.              (MB-23, MB-25)
And whoever files, pays the filing fee.                                    (MB-24)

Marchio's paper was harsher still: no exceptions at all.                   (MB-21)

The patient died.
A family member sued, alleging negligence caused injuries or harm
  resulting in death.                                                      (MB-08)

Brown's suit was dismissed. Taylor's was dismissed.
  Because of the agreements to arbitrate.                                  (MB-13)

West Virginia's highest court then said no —
  "an arbitration clause in a nursing home admission agreement
   adopted prior to an occurrence of negligence … shall not be enforced."  (MB-28)
It called this Court's reading of the FAA
  "tendentious," and "created from whole cloth."                           (MB-30)
Its reason: a service that is "a practical necessity
  for members of the public."                                              (MB-31)

Washington answered in five pages.
  "State and federal courts must enforce the Federal Arbitration Act."     (MB-32)
  "The statute's text includes no exception for personal-injury
   or wrongful-death claims."                                              (MB-38)
  "That rule resolves these cases."                                        (MB-42)
  The state court had misread and disregarded its precedents.              (MB-33, MB-36)
  A state court "may not contradict or fail to implement" federal law.     (MB-35)

Vacated.                                                                   (MB-50)

And then — the part almost everyone drops.
  The West Virginia court had ALSO called these particular clauses
    unconscionable.                                                        (MB-45)
  It was unclear how far that had been contaminated by the invalid rule.   (MB-47)
  So: on remand, decide whether, absent the public policy,
    Brown's and Taylor's clauses are unenforceable under
    state common law principles not specific to arbitration.               (MB-49)

The Supreme Court did not say the clauses hold.
The Supreme Court said West Virginia may not answer the question
  the way it did.                                                          (MB-51, MB-52, MB-53)
```

**MB-49 is the ending, and MB-25 is the opening.** Open on the one carve-out the home wrote for itself —
late payments owed by the patient — and close on a question that was sent back unanswered. The film's
title asks what the signed paper was. This opinion answers only that **it was covered by a federal
statute**, and hands everything else back to a state court. That is an honest ending, and it is the only
one this source supports.
