## ACT 1 — 4219 South Alton Street [S01 S02 S03 S04 S06]

Greenwood Village, Colorado. A suburb south of Denver where the lawns are edged and the streets curve for no reason. A two-story house on South Alton Street. Leo and Alfonsia Lech had bought it for their son John, and that June, John was living there with his girlfriend and her nine-year-old boy.

Nothing about that morning belonged to them. Somewhere north, in Aurora, officers had tried to arrest a man named Robert Seacat. He ran. He was armed. He broke into the house on South Alton Street. It wasn't his house. He had no connection to the family who lived there.

When Greenwood Village police arrived they learned there was a man inside, and that there was a child inside. The nine-year-old got out. The record puts it plainly and without drama: he was able to exit the home safely. The boy was out. The gunman was not.

Then a shot came from the garage and hit a police car, and the situation acquired a name. High-risk barricade.

[SOUND: for the negotiation, strip everything except the bullhorn. No score, no sirens, no interior sound. Five hours of one amplified voice and long gaps.]

For about five hours, negotiators tried to talk him out. They failed.

What came after is written down in a federal court record, in flat institutional language, which is the only language anyone uses for this kind of thing. The court's account of the night runs a few sentences. This is all of it.

[SOUND: the entry sequence plays with the sound pulled out from under it. Low rumble only, no impacts, until the roof line.]

Officers fired several rounds of gas munition into the house. An armored vehicle called a BearCat breached the home's doors so a robot could carry in a throw phone. Explosives were used to open sight lines and points of entry. Holes to see through, holes to move through. A tactical team went in. Seacat fired on them. They pulled back. Then the BearCat came again, and this time it punched multiple holes in the house, and the team went in through those, and it ended.

Nineteen hours. By then the officers had been given an instruction, recorded by the federal district court and quoted ever since: take as much of the building as needed without making the roof fall in.

The roof did not fall in.

*(silence — 2.0s: dawn, dust settling in the shafts of light through the openings)*

Nobody in this story did the wrong thing that day. The tactics weren't challenged. What Leo Lech would spend the next years arguing about was never whether the police should have destroyed the house. It was who pays for a house destroyed for the public's benefit.

## ACT 2 — What it costs to be the address [S07 S08 S10 S11 S12]

The city offered help with temporary living expenses. Five thousand dollars. 〔CARD: $5,000〕 That figure appears in the family's own petition to the Supreme Court, described in those words, as help with temporary living expenses.

Six years later, in a brief filed by his lawyers, Lech added something the appeals court's record doesn't contain. He says the five thousand came with a condition, that he give up his right to sue. That's his account and it should be heard as his account. What the Tenth Circuit's own document says is narrower and, in its way, colder: the city offered assistance with temporary lodging, denied any further responsibility, and refused additional compensation.

So he turned to the thing everyone turns to. He had homeowner's coverage. He'd paid for it for years, for exactly this, for the day something enormous happens to your house. When the policy was read, it excluded intentional acts by government officials.

〔CARD: INTENTIONAL ACTS BY GOVERNMENT OFFICIALS — EXCLUDED〕

*(silence — 2.0s, card held, no narration over it)*

Coverage exists for a fire. No policy covers the state deciding to come through a wall. He was paid for part of it. The rest was his.

Then came the walls themselves. Breaking open a house built in that era released what was inside them. Asbestos. According to his brief, the dust was through the structure, and he was walked into the rubble to pull out what was left of his family's belongings without being told what he was breathing. What was left to salvage fit in his hands. Somewhere in that pile was a nine-year-old's room, and the boy who had walked out of it at the start of all this never came back to it.

To rebuild, he borrowed two hundred fifty thousand dollars. 〔CARD: $250,000〕 He had been planning to retire. He pushed it back five years. Five years of a man's life, moved, because a stranger he'd never met chose that door.

And no one in this had done anything wrong. The city broke no law, and every officer performed the job the way the job is supposed to be performed. The entire cost of all that correctness landed on one family, who had contributed nothing whatsoever to the emergency except an address.

He sued. Of course he sued. He believed what most people believe, that the Constitution has a sentence for exactly this. It does. Nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation. Fourteen words at the end of the Fifth Amendment, and he thought fourteen words would be enough.

## ACT 3 — Police power [S13 S14 S15 S16 S17]

They were not.

In January 2018, a federal district court in Colorado ruled against him, and its reasoning had two moves. When the state acts under its police power rather than its power of eminent domain, its actions do not constitute a taking. And these officers were destroying the house in order to enforce the criminal law. Police power, not eminent domain. That was the ruling. Nothing owed.

Eminent domain is the state deciding it wants land, for a highway or a reservoir. It takes, and it pays. Police power is the state responding to a danger. It stops, it seizes, it destroys, and under the line the court drew, it doesn't pay. The same house. The same rubble. By the time a judge in Denver wrote that distinction down, the house on South Alton Street had been gone for more than two years.

Lech appealed. On October 29, 2019, the Tenth Circuit affirmed, a panel of Judges Holmes, McKay and Moritz, with Judge Nancy Moritz writing. The document is not an opinion. It's an Order and Judgment, and this is its own first-page footnote.

〔CARD: NOT BINDING PRECEDENT — MAY BE CITED FOR ITS PERSUASIVE VALUE〕

*(silence — 2.0s)*

The panel didn't pretend the outcome was comfortable. It conceded that the police here acted for the benefit of the public. It reached back to an 1887 case, Mugler v. Kansas, for the principle. And then it borrowed a line from another federal appeals decision. As unfair as it may seem, the court wrote, the Takings Clause does not entitle all aggrieved owners to recompense.

Lech argued the obvious thing, that he was innocent, and that innocence should count. The court answered that this position has considerable appeal as a matter of policy.

Considerable appeal.

*(silence — 1.5s)*

And then it quoted the rule anyway. The innocence of the property owner does not factor into the determination.

The court was careful to say the police power isn't unlimited. Due process constrains it, and under Colorado law an officer who willfully or wantonly destroys property can be sued personally. Neither of those doors was open to a man whose house was destroyed correctly. He asked the Supreme Court to take the case. On June 29, 2020, it declined. A denial of review decides nothing. It just ends things.

## ACT 4 — The address is the only qualification [S18 S19 S20 S21 S22]

If this happened once, it would be a tragedy about one house.

Indiana. June 2022. Police believed a murder suspect had used the internet address at Amy Hadley's house. They had a warrant, an IP address, and the wrong home. Amy Hadley's fifteen-year-old son was the only person in the house, and he surrendered. Thirty canisters of tear gas had already gone in through the broken windows. The suspect wasn't there. The damage came to about sixteen thousand dollars, and the city and the county each refused to pay it. In October 2025 the Seventh Circuit rejected her claim.

Texas, July 2020. A fugitive took a fifteen-year-old girl hostage inside a home belonging to a woman named Vicki Baker. Police fired dozens of gas canisters, blew the front and garage doors, and pushed down the backyard fence with an armored vehicle. When they entered, he had killed himself. Her insurer covered none of the police damage. A federal jury said she was owed nearly sixty thousand dollars, and the Fifth Circuit erased it, holding that when destroying property is objectively necessary during an ongoing emergency to prevent imminent harm to persons, the Takings Clause requires no compensation. Baker was eventually told she was owed it, but not by the federal Constitution. A court said so under the constitution of the state of Texas. A different document, in a different building, saying a different thing.

Tennessee, 2022. Deputies came for the Slaybaughs' son, who was staying with them, and put about thirty-five gas canisters into the house. Roughly seventy thousand dollars of damage. In 2024 the Sixth Circuit called a categorical police-power exception questionable, then dismissed the claim anyway on an old common-law privilege attached to searches and arrests.

In November 2025 the Ninth Circuit ruled that Carlos Pena gets nothing. Thirteen hours earlier, in August 2022, an armed fugitive had shoved Pena out of his own print shop in Los Angeles and locked the door behind him. SWAT put gas through the walls, the doors and the roof. Nobody disputes the police acted lawfully.

*(silence — 2.0s: four addresses, no narration)*

Then Nevada. August 2025, Las Vegas. A SWAT raid hit a home over a suspect who had lived at that address more than five years earlier. About thirty thousand dollars of damage. The department denied the claim, and then, after the story ran, reversed itself and covered it. Same kind of emergency, same kind of damage, and the difference was not a rule. It was a news cycle.

That's the argument Leo Lech's lawyers now make. Whether you are made whole is not decided by the Constitution. It's decided by which city you happen to live in.

In November 2024, when the Supreme Court declined to hear Vicki Baker's case, Justice Sotomayor wrote a statement respecting that denial, joined by Justice Gorsuch. It carried no legal weight. It wasn't the Court's opinion and it wasn't a dissent. It was filed alongside the refusal itself.

She wrote that the case raises an important question that divides the courts of appeals, whether the Takings Clause requires compensation when the government damages private property under its police power. She wrote that all agree the McKinney police acted properly that day. She mapped the split: the Seventh Circuit and the Federal Circuit on one side, the Sixth reaching the same result by another road, the Fourth Circuit holding that government action under the police power is not automatically exempt from the Takings Clause. She cited Lech by name. And she said the question would benefit from further percolation in the lower courts before the Court steps in, adding that the denial expressed no view on the merits.

## ENDING — 感情ペイオフ ＋ CTA [S23 S24 S25]

There is one more filing. In May 2026, eleven years after his son's house was opened with explosives and six years after the Supreme Court declined to hear him, Leo Lech signed his name to a brief in someone else's case. Two cases, actually. Hadley's and Pena's, both filed on April 6, 2026, both distributed for conference on September 28. Both still pending. Two cases from two states, asking the question the Tenth Circuit answered in a document it refused to call precedent. Nobody knows how they end, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing.

He gets nothing from those cases. He isn't a party. His own claim was over in 2020. He wrote it anyway, alongside two strangers from Las Vegas whose house was raided over a man who had moved out five years before.

Justice Sotomayor and the parties on every side of this quote the same sentence, from a 1960 case called Armstrong. The Takings Clause was designed to bar Government from forcing some people alone to bear public burdens which, in all fairness and justice, should be borne by the public as a whole.

Some people alone.

Right now, in most of this country, the answer is the address.
