## 1 · The grounding

| id | fact | source |
|---|---|---|
| VZ-001 | At about **0009 on 24 March 1989**, the U.S. tankship EXXON VALDEZ grounded on **Bligh Reef** in Prince William Sound, near Valdez, Alaska | S1 p1 |
| VZ-002 | She was loaded with about **1,263,000 barrels of crude oil** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-003 | **At the time of the grounding the vessel was under the navigational control of the third mate** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-004 | **There were no injuries** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-005 | About **258,000 barrels of cargo were spilled when eight cargo tanks ruptured**, resulting in catastrophic damage to the environment | S1 p1 |
| VZ-006 | Damage to the vessel was estimated at **$25 million**; the lost cargo at **$3.4 million**; the 1989 cleanup at about **$1.85 billion** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-007 | A footnote in the same letter: **the cost of cleanup is expected to exceed $2 billion** | S1 p4 |

## 2 · The third mate's twenty-four hours

| id | fact | source |
|---|---|---|
| VZ-101 | The third mate **had probably had very little sleep the night before the grounding and had worked a stressful, physically demanding day** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-102 | Deballasting and cargo handling were going on while the ship was at the terminal, so he was **unlikely to have obtained a full off-watch period of rest** when he went to bed some time after 0100 on 23 March | S1 p1 |
| VZ-103 | **He may have been called as early as 0520** to relieve the second mate | S1 p1 |
| VZ-104 | According to the second mate, **he and the third mate were covering the chief mate's watch essentially on a 6-hours-on, 6-hours-off basis** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-105 | The third mate was seen on deck during the afternoon watch, conducted a salinity test, and **relieved the chief mate during supper** | S1 p1 |
| VZ-106 | **The Board concludes he could have had as little as 4 hours sleep before beginning the workday on 23 March and only a 1- to 2-hour nap in the afternoon — as little as 5 or 6 hours of sleep in the previous 24 hours** | S1 p1–2 |
| VZ-107 | **He was working beyond his normal watch period** | S1 p2 |
| VZ-108 | The Board sets out the bind he was in. **If he made the turn too early the vessel would meet glacial ice at manoeuvring speed and possibly damage the hull. If he waited too long she would ground on Bligh Reef.** | S1 p2 |
| VZ-109 | The Board's own counterweight, stated fairly: the significance of the course change **should have increased his resistance to fatigue, at least for that limited period**. But **"the insidious nature of fatigue is such that sleep could have overcome him at any time that he momentarily relaxed his vigilance"** | S1 p2 |

## 3 · Why the ship sailed tired — the structural finding

| id | fact | source |
|---|---|---|
| VZ-201 | The chief mate is given responsibility for loading and discharging cargo and ballast and is on duty during all critical stages. **This is widely practised.** The result is many hours of work for him and, in most cases, **his in-port watches are taken by the other two mates** | S1 p2 |
| VZ-202 | **So on three-mate vessels the other two mates are essentially standing 6 hours on and 6 hours off — a schedule that seldom enables any officer to acquire adequate rest until the vessel returns to sea** | S1 p2 |
| VZ-203 | **The consequence, in the Board's words:** the first part of the voyage — the transit through the port and other confined or congested waters — **"is likely to be conducted by navigation watch officers who are in varying stages of fatigue"** | S1 p2 |
| VZ-204 | Some masters recognise this and **take the navigation watch themselves** until a watch officer is rested. **This is not the practice on all three-mate vessels** | S1 p2 |
| VZ-205 | The Board's remedies, listed: keep the vessel in port long enough after loading for an officer to rest; **assign a fourth deck officer, "as was the practice in the past on many tankships, including those of the Exxon Shipping Company"**; or temporarily assign a qualified officer to take the chief mate's in-port watch | S1 p2 |
| VZ-206 | **The EXXON VALDEZ was operated with a reduced crew complement**, and evidence indicated **watchkeeping safeguards had been compromised because of the manning level** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-207 | There were not enough unlicensed deck crew for uninterrupted lookout. When one able seaman had to serve as helmsman, the remaining crew covered all work and lookout duties unless somebody was turned to on overtime | S1 p3 |
| VZ-208 | **"As a result, on the EXXON VALDEZ, the lookout position routinely went unattended when the AB was called for other tasks or took a break."** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-209 | Union officials testified that **the sea passages between Alaska and California were not long enough for necessary maintenance or thorough crew rest** between the around-the-clock demands of cargo handling in port | S1 p3 |
| VZ-210 | **The decisive detail.** When the minimum crew requirements were established for the EXXON VALDEZ, **the vessel had been scheduled for the Valdez-to-Panama trade. That trade was discontinued after December 1988**, and she then began operating regularly between **Valdez and California** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-211 | **"The mates on the EXXON VALDEZ were usually fatigued after cargo handling operations in Valdez, and the vessel usually put to sea with a fatigued crew."** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-212 | Cargo handling automation **did not eliminate the need for deck officers to spend many hours on cargo watches** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-213 | **Exxon Shipping Company procedures required two officers on the bridge during manoeuvring.** The Board says compliance **"may have provided sufficient sharing of the workload to prevent the grounding"** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-214 | The Board is careful not to make that the answer: it is **reluctant to endorse the routine use of two officers who may not have had adequate rest** as a way of manning a navigation watch | S1 p3 |
| VZ-215 | **The Board's verdict on the manning practices:** it considers them **"generally incautious and without apparent justification from the standpoint of safety"**, and says the financial advantage from eliminating officers and crew **"does not seem to justify incurring the foreseeable risks of serious accidents"** | S1 p3 |
| VZ-216 | Four things the Board **could find no reasonable explanation for**: the absence of company programmes to ensure crew observed hours-of-service rules; no procedure to ensure a rested deck officer besides the master was available at departure; **rating a crewmember's performance in part on willingness to work overtime, giving an incentive to work excessive hours**; and the indiscriminate increase in work loads and standby time across the fleet before and after the grounding | S1 p4 |

**VZ-210 and VZ-216 are the spine.** The crew number was set for a voyage the ship had
stopped making, and the appraisal system paid people to be tired.

## 4 · The master, handled the way the record handles him

| id | fact | source |
|---|---|---|
| VZ-301 | The master underwent treatment in **1985**. Exxon's alcohol policy directive then in effect instructed supervisors to refer employees whose performance was unsatisfactory owing to perceived alcohol use to the medical department | S1 p4 |
| VZ-302 | **His supervisor was apparently unaware that he had an alcohol dependency problem prior to his hospitalisation**, and on learning of it was supposed to refer the case to the medical department | S1 p4 |
| VZ-303 | The attending physician recommended a **follow-up treatment programme**. He was given a **90-day leave of absence**. **No documents were provided to establish that the outpatient programme was followed or that his progress was monitored by management** | S1 p4 |
| VZ-304 | **The Exxon medical department does not appear to have contacted the hospital** where he received in-patient treatment | S1 p4 |
| VZ-305 | **No one in the Exxon management structure appears to have consulted an expert on alcoholism** about what support, supervision or monitoring he would need, the chances of relapse, the signs of it, or what assignments he could safely perform | S1 p4 |
| VZ-306 | The president of Exxon Shipping Company testified that the master **"thought he was the most scrutinized employee in the company."** The Board notes that if that scrutiny happened, **the written records either do not exist or were not provided — except one constructed from memory after the grounding** | S1 p4 |
| VZ-307 | Some personnel evaluations were **unsigned**, so their authenticity could not be established | S1 p4–5 |
| VZ-308 | The Board's conclusion: **Exxon should have removed the master from seagoing employment until there was ample proof that he had his alcohol problem under control** | S1 p5 |
| VZ-309 | The Board's framing, which the film should keep: it is reasonable to assume that if Exxon had had **a technical problem such as an autopilot failure**, the problem would have gone to an expert inside the company or an outside consultant would have been hired | S1 p5 |

## 5 · The six recommendations

| id | fact | source |
|---|---|---|
| VZ-401 | **M-90-26** — eliminate personnel policies, **including performance appraisal criteria**, that encourage employees to work long hours without concern for debilitating fatigue | S1 p5 |
| VZ-402 | **M-90-27** — implement manning policies that prevent excessively long working hours during cargo handling | S1 p5 |
| VZ-403 | **M-90-28** — institute a written policy **forbidding deck officers to share navigation and cargo watch duties on a 6-on, 6-off basis**, except in emergencies | S1 p5 |
| VZ-404 | **M-90-29** — require that **two licensed watch officers be present** to conn and navigate vessels in Prince William Sound | S1 p5 |
| VZ-405 | **M-90-30** — implement an alcohol and drug programme that prevents personnel returning to sea until dependency is under control | S1 p5 |
| VZ-406 | **M-90-31** — train the people who monitor rehabilitation to recognise relapse, to make professional referrals, and in the interpersonal skills competent supervision needs | S1 p5–6 |

**Five of six are about staffing and scheduling.**

## 6 · What it cost, and what a court decided it should cost

| id | fact | source |
|---|---|---|
| VZ-501 | **Exxon spent some $2.1 billion in cleanup efforts** | S2 p1 |
| VZ-502 | Exxon **pleaded guilty to criminal violations occasioning fines** | S2 p1 |
| VZ-503 | Exxon **settled a civil action by the United States and Alaska for at least $900 million** | S2 p1 |
| VZ-504 | Exxon **paid another $303 million in voluntary payments to private parties** | S2 p1 |
| VZ-505 | In Phase II of the trial the jury awarded **$287 million in compensatory damages** to some plaintiffs; others had settled their compensatory claims for **$22.6 million** | S2 p1 |
| VZ-506 | In Phase III the jury awarded **$5,000 in punitive damages against the master and $5 billion against Exxon** | S2 p1 |
| VZ-507 | The District Court reconsidered the award twice on remand and **each time concluded that a $5 billion award was justified by the facts**, based in large part on a finding that **"Exxon's conduct was highly reprehensible"** — reducing it only slightly, and only when the Court of Appeals specifically demanded | S2 p56 |
| VZ-508 | **The Ninth Circuit ultimately remitted the award to $2.5 billion** | S2 p1, p52 |
| VZ-509 | The Supreme Court held that **a 1:1 ratio of punitive to compensatory damages is a fair upper limit in maritime cases like this one** | S2 p4, p43 |
| VZ-510 | Applying that, the Court **took for granted the District Court's calculation of total relevant compensatory damages at $507.5 million**, so the maximum punitive award became **$507.5 million** | S2 p4, p45 |
| VZ-511 | The judgment was **vacated and remanded**, on **25 June 2008 — nineteen years after the grounding** | S2 p1, p45 |
| VZ-512 | The plaintiffs are described as people **"who depend on Prince William Sound for their livelihoods"** | S2 p1 |
