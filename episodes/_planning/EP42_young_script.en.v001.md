## ACT 1 — "THE WRONG DOOR" — role: body *(kept short and fast; the raid runs in present tense)*
**[VISUAL: West-Side apartment as symbolic space — kitchen cabinets ajar, scattered papers, an unworn coat over a chair, an ankle-monitor icon glowing elsewhere on a city map. Never her face/body.]**

> Her name is Anjanette Young. She is a social worker on Chicago's West Side. That night she had just come home from a shift spent steering other people through the worst days of their lives. This time the person who needed help was her.
>
> The officers had a search warrant. A real one, signed by a judge. But the information behind it was wrong. Someone had told the police that a man with a gun lived at her address. He did not. The man they wanted lived somewhere else entirely. And one detail turns the whole night from tragedy into farce: he was already wearing a court-ordered ankle monitor. His exact location sat on a screen the entire time. They came to her door instead.
>
> A shout at the door. Then the frame gives way. Boots. Flashlights. Men filling a small apartment. She is handcuffed where she stands, before she can cover herself. And she says the only true thing anyone will say all night. You have the wrong house. She says it to the first officer. She says it to the next.
>
> **【SILENCE 1.5s — the body camera keeps recording】**
>
> They keep searching anyway. They open her cabinets. They pull her life apart looking for a man who was never here. On one officer's chest, a body camera runs the whole time, recording her voice, the number of times she says it, and the silence where an answer should be.
>
> Eventually they understand. They uncuff her, let her dress, and leave. And that could have been the end of it — a bad night, an honest mistake, an apology. But no one apologized. The officers filed their paperwork and moved on. The city never called. The recording of what happened in that apartment went into a drawer, and the people who put it there were content to leave it.

## ACT 2 — "THE TAPE" — role: body · (re-hook at act close)
**[VISUAL: a manila folder sealed shut; a courthouse corridor; a television glowing in a dark living room; a redacted document with unreadable text (per accuracy_lock §3).]**

> For almost two years, nothing happened. Anjanette Young did everything the system tells you to do. She filed complaints. She asked for the video from the officers' own cameras, the proof she had told the truth from the first second. The city fought her. It went to court to keep that video from ever being seen, and it moved to sanction her lawyer for sharing it.
>
> She was the one they raided. She stood in her own home while strangers with rifles decided it was theirs. And she was the one the city moved to punish.
>
> Then, in December 2020, a CBS station in Chicago obtained the body-camera footage and put it on television. Overnight, the whole country was standing inside that apartment. People heard her voice. They heard how many times she said it. They watched a woman ringed by armed men ask only to be believed, and go unbelieved.
>
> The reaction was fast. The mayor apologized. Officials who had said they knew nothing suddenly remembered. And the city's own police-oversight agency opened a full investigation. It ran for roughly sixteen months, and in the end it flagged something close to one hundred alleged violations, tied to more than a dozen officers from that night. It looked, at last, like accountability arriving.
>
> **【SILENCE 0.9s】**
>
> On paper. Because a finding by a watchdog is not a ruling by a court, and an apology, however sincere, is not the law admitting it broke it. Everything that came next would turn on that gap — the distance between being sorry and being liable. To see why the gap runs so wide, you have to leave Chicago and go back more than twenty years, to a doorway in Detroit.

## ACT 3 — "THE COMMAND" — role: body *(doctrinal core — deliberately the SLOWEST act; the ruling lands in present tense. Accuracy constraints 4 & 5 govern every line.)*
**[VISUAL: single line of Fourth Amendment text as light on a wall; empty marble chamber; nine empty seats; a gavel resting, unused. Booker Hudson NOT depicted as a person — only a generic doorway/threshold in Detroit.]**

> The Fourth Amendment is one sentence long. It says the people have a right to be secure in their houses against unreasonable searches, and that warrants require probable cause. For most of American history, that security carried an old rule, inherited from English common law. Before police force their way into a home, they knock, they announce who they are, and they give you a moment to reach the door. Knock, and announce. It is the line between a search and an ambush.
>
> For decades, when police broke that rule, courts held one answer officers actually felt. Evidence from a bad entry could be thrown out of court, and a thrown-out search could sink an entire case. That was the teeth.
>
> Then came Hudson versus Michigan.
>
> Detroit, August 1998. Police hold a valid warrant to search the home of a man named Booker Hudson. They announce themselves — police, search warrant — and then they wait only three to five seconds before going through an unlocked door. It is not long enough. Everyone in the case agrees it is not long enough; Michigan does not even argue the point. The violation is admitted. The only question left for the Supreme Court is what to do about it.
>
> June 2006. The Court answers, five to four. Justice Antonin Scalia writes for the majority, and the ruling is narrow and precise, and devastating precisely because it is narrow. The knock-and-announce rule, he writes, is still a command of the Fourth Amendment. It still exists. Police still have to obey it. The Court does not say officers can stop knocking.
>
> What the Court takes away is the punishment. When police break the knock-and-announce rule, Scalia writes, the evidence they find does not get thrown out. Your privacy in that last moment, your dignity, the chance to open your own door — none of it changes whether the thing they find inside is real. So the evidence stays. The exclusionary rule, the Court says, should apply only where its benefits outweigh its heavy social costs. Here, it decides, they do not.
>
> And Scalia gives reasons the old teeth can be spared. The country of the 1960s, when the exclusionary rule grew into what it is, is not the country of 2006, he writes. Police forces are more professional now, more disciplined from within than they were then. And the courthouse door is open: a person whose rights were trampled can bring a civil-rights suit, and those suits, he says, had grown common enough to keep police in line. The deterrent has simply moved — out of the criminal case and into the civil one, where money is on the table.
>
> But those reasons carried only four votes. The fifth, Justice Anthony Kennedy, supplied the deciding vote, yet wrote separately that the exclusionary rule was not in doubt. The argument that civil suits are enough was never the majority's — only the result was.
>
> Justice Stephen Breyer writes the dissent, joined by three others, and his warning is blunt. Take away the remedy, he says, and you take away the rule. He walks through the history: the exclusion of evidence exists precisely because nothing softer ever made police obey; civil suits had sat on the books for generations and changed almost nothing. A right with no consequence for breaking it, he writes, is not really a right at all. The majority's reply is that one remedy still stands — a civil lawsuit. Under a federal statute, section nineteen eighty-three, you can take the government to court and ask for money.
>
> That is the whole promise, stripped to the frame. Break into a home the wrong way, and the Constitution's reply is not that the case collapses, and not that anyone is automatically held to account. It is that you, whose door it was, may go to court and try. You carry the burden. You hire the lawyer. And you hope a jury agrees. That is the remedy the Constitution leaves you.

## ACT 4 — "THE REACH" — role: body
**[VISUAL: a check on a table; a City Council chamber with an empty podium; a vote tally board; the printed ordinance stamped and set aside. Text unreadable per accuracy_lock.]**

> Now Anjanette Young. Her case and Hudson's are not the same door. Hudson was a convicted man asking a court to throw out evidence. Young was never charged with anything; there was nothing of hers to suppress. What links them is not a remedy. It is a pattern. When the wrong is in how the police get in, the Constitution's cupboard runs nearly bare, and the one thing left on the shelf is exactly what the Court pointed to — the civil suit.
>
> So she brought one. And she reached the only door still open. The city agreed to pay her two point nine million dollars, and on December 15th, 2021, the City Council approved it — reported as forty-eight to nothing.
>
> It sounds like victory. Look at what a settlement actually is. No court found the city broke the law. No judge ruled her rights were violated. Not one officer was declared liable by any court. The city paid to make the case disappear; it bought quiet, not a verdict. In the language of the law, nobody did anything wrong to Anjanette Young that night. There is a check, and a page that stays blank where a finding of fault should be.
>
> There were gestures. One sergeant who led the raid, Alex Wolinski, was fired by the city's Police Board in the summer of 2023, by a vote of five to three. Most of the other officers who stood in her apartment kept their jobs, their pensions, everything. And the law itself? A reform was written and named for her, the Anjanette Young Ordinance. It asked for small, human things: a written plan before a raid, thirty seconds of waiting at the door, protection for children inside. In November 2022, a City Council committee voted it down, ten to four. It never reached the floor. Years on, the practice it was written to fix is still legal in the city where it happened.

> So, the question we started with. If armed police break down the wrong door — your door — does the law owe you anything?
>
> The honest answer comes in two halves.
>
> The first half is that yes, you might get paid. If the mistake is bad enough, and the footage is damning enough, and a television station airs your worst night, a city may decide a check is cheaper than a fight. Two point nine million dollars is real money. For Anjanette Young, it was real.
>
> **【SILENCE 1.0s】**
>
> The second half is the one that lasts. In all of it — the settlement, the apology, the long investigation — no court ever said a single person violated Anjanette Young's rights. The money admitted nothing. The reform that carried her name was voted down. The rule that was supposed to guard her front door is still, in the Supreme Court's own words, a command of the Constitution, with almost nothing standing behind it.
>
> You can be completely innocent. You can be completely right. You can say the wrong house again and again into a camera that captures every word, and still, at the end, the law can hand you money while refusing to say that anyone wronged you at all. Being right was never her problem. It took a television station to make her believed. And being made whole? That door never opened.
>
> Anjanette Young still talks about that night. Not for the money; she has the money. She talks because there is one sentence the whole system spent years not saying, the sentence no check will ever contain. We were wrong, and it was your door.
>

And subscribe, because we are documenting, one ruling at a time, exactly how much the law owes you. This is Prime Documentary.
