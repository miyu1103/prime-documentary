## 2 · The probable cause

| id | fact | page |
|---|---|---|
| AK-101 | **Verbatim.** *"The National Transportation Safety Board determines that the probable cause of this accident was a loss of airplane pitch control resulting from the in-flight failure of the horizontal stabilizer trim system jackscrew assembly's acme nut threads. The thread failure was caused by excessive wear resulting from Alaska Airlines' insufficient lubrication of the jackscrew assembly."* | 14 |
| AK-102 | **Contributing, verbatim:** *"Alaska Airlines' extended lubrication interval and the Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) approval of that extension, which increased the likelihood that a missed or inadequate lubrication would result in excessive wear of the acme nut threads"* | 14 |
| AK-103 | **Contributing, verbatim:** *"Alaska Airlines' extended end play check interval and the FAA's approval of that extension, which allowed the excessive wear of the acme nut threads to progress to failure without the opportunity for detection"* | 14 |
| AK-104 | **Contributing, verbatim:** *"the absence on the McDonnell Douglas MD-80 of a fail-safe mechanism to prevent the catastrophic effects of total acme nut thread loss"* | 14 |

**Note the structure.** One cause, and three contributing factors — **two of which are approvals**.

## 3 · The lubrication interval, in order

| id | fact | page |
|---|---|---|
| AK-201 | Douglas's original recommendation, from MSG-2 guidance, was a lubrication interval of **600 to 900 flight hours** | 161 |
| AK-202 | **March 1985** — B checks every 350 flight hours, jackscrew lubricated every other B check: **every 700 flight hours** | 32 |
| AK-203 | **March 1987** — B-check interval raised to 500 hours, lubrication moved to every B check: **every 500 flight hours** | 32 |
| AK-204 | **July 1988** — B checks eliminated. Lubrication moved to every eighth A check: **every 1,000 flight hours** | 32 |
| AK-205 | **February 1991** — A-check interval raised to 150 hours. Still every eighth A check: **every 1,200 flight hours** | 32 |
| AK-206 | **December 1994** — A-check interval raised to 200 hours. Still every eighth A check: **every 1,600 flight hours** | 32 |
| AK-207 | **July 1996** — the task was removed from the A check altogether and put on a time-controlled card with a maximum interval of **8 months and no flight-hour limit**. At the utilisation rates of the time, that was about **2,550 flight hours** | 32 |
| AK-208 | **"Thus, at the time of the accident, Alaska Airlines' lubrication interval for the jackscrew assembly was more than 400 percent greater than it was in 1987."** | 161 |
| AK-209 | The Board records that **the investigation did not determine what information, if any, was presented as justification** for the extensions of 1988, 1991 and 1994 | 161 |
| AK-210 | At the time it moved to the 8-month interval, Alaska Airlines was **the only U.S. airline with a calendar-time lubrication interval carrying no flight-hour limit and no "whichever comes first"** | 162 |
| AK-211 | The Board's reasoning, plainly: *"Longer lubrication intervals increase the likelihood that a missed or inadequate lubrication will result in excessive wear."* And a calendar-only interval degrades the margin because **wear tracks flight hours, not calendar time** | 162 |
| AK-212 | Douglas's earlier 600-to-900-hour recommendation **was not considered** during the MSG-3 process that extended the recommended interval | 32 |

## 4 · The end play check, in order

| id | fact | page |
|---|---|---|
| AK-301 | An end play check measures the play between the acme screw and nut by applying a specified torque through a tool called a **restraining fixture** and reading a **dial indicator**. The number is used as the indication of thread wear | 39 |
| AK-302 | Jackscrew assemblies could stay in service as long as end play stayed **between 0.003 and 0.040 inch** | 40 |
| AK-303 | Douglas originally recommended end play checks at C-check intervals: **every 3,600 flight hours** | 47 |
| AK-304 | The recommendation was later relaxed to every other C check: MSG-2 **every 7,000 hours or 30 months**, MSG-3 **every 7,200 hours or 30 months** | 47 |
| AK-305 | **March 1985** — Alaska: end play checks every other C check, C checks every 2,500 hours: **every 5,000 flight hours** | 47 |
| AK-306 | **July 1988** — C-check intervals extended to 13 months with no flight-hour limit: end play checks **every 26 calendar months, about 6,400 flight hours** | 47 |
| AK-307 | **April 1996** — C-check intervals extended to 15 months, about every 4,775 flight hours | 47 |
| AK-308 | **At the time of the accident** Alaska's end play check interval was **every 30 months, about every 9,550 flight hours** | 61 |
| AK-309 | Before **AD 2000-15-15**, issued in **July 2000** — five months after the accident — **end play measurements that fell within limits were not required to be recorded at all** | 53 |

**AK-309 is the hinge of this film.** A measurement inside limits produced no number, so there was
nothing to compare a later measurement against, and nothing for anyone to go back and read.

## 6 · What the wreckage showed

| id | fact | page |
|---|---|---|
| AK-501 | Sheared remnants of the acme nut threads were recovered **wrapped around the acme screw** | 143 |
| AK-502 | **About 90 percent of the thread thickness had worn away** before the remainder sheared off | 143 |
| AK-503 | For comparison: a jackscrew at the **maximum wear permitted in service** — an end play reading of 0.040 inch — would have only about **22 percent** of the thread thickness worn away | 143 |
| AK-504 | The interior of the recovered acme nut contained **no intact threads at all** | 143 |
| AK-505 | Each acme nut thread makes 16 full spiral revolutions, 32 in total. Shearing all 32 at once, at the accident nut's degree of wear, would have required loads of **more than 190,000 pounds** — far beyond the flight loads | 143 |

## 7 · What the design assumed

| id | fact | page |
|---|---|---|
| AK-601 | The certification documents show the design was required to survive **the loss of 90 percent of acme screw and nut threads**, achieved by giving the assembly more threads than were structurally necessary | 36 |
| AK-602 | It was also required to survive **the failure of one entire thread spiral**, achieved by using two independent spirals. A Boeing structures engineering manager testified that the reason for two threads is that **if one fails the other carries the load** | 36 |
| AK-603 | A Safety Board review of the **15 April 1965 DC-9 Flight Controls System Fault Analysis Report** (revised 14 July 1997) found **no contingency for the complete loss of the acme nut threads** | 36 |
| AK-604 | The FAA's senior certification engineer testified that the jackscrew assembly was a combination of structural and systems element, and that **the acme nut was not considered a system** and therefore **was not required to comply with airplane systems certification requirements** | 37 |
| AK-605 | The Board concluded that **catastrophic single-point failure modes should be prohibited** in the design of all future airplanes with horizontal stabilizer trim systems, regardless of whether an element is classed as structure rather than system | 167 |

## 8 · That it was not one airline

| id | fact | page |
|---|---|---|
| AK-701 | ⚠ **These belong to other airlines, found during this investigation. Never attribute them to flight 261.** | — |
| AK-702 | On 17 February 2000 a jackscrew was removed from a **Hawaiian Airlines** DC-9 after an on-wing reading of **0.043 inch**; a bench check after cleaning read **0.048 inch** | 83 |
| AK-703 | Grease from two Hawaiian assemblies contained pink sandlike particles that **matched the Australian garnet the airline used to blast corrosion off aircraft structure** | 83 |
| AK-704 | Of **214** jackscrew assemblies sent for overhaul between 1996 and 1999, **13** had bench-check end play of 0.045 inch or more and **5** had 0.055 inch or more. The highest reported to investigators was **0.075 inch**, on an assembly originally delivered new to Delta | 83 |
| AK-705 | As far back as **29 May 1984**, McDonnell Douglas issued AOL 9-1526 reporting three premature jackscrew removals, noting that grease samples from two of them were **"dry and without evidence of recent renewal"**, and urging operators to lubricate at 600-flight-hour intervals | 41 |
| AK-706 | In 1990 another airline reported an acme nut worn past the 0.040-inch limit after 5,780 flight hours — a wear rate of 0.0057 inch per 1,000 hours | 41–42 |
