## ACT 1 — 普通の子供たち（不条理の提示） [S01 S02 S03 S04]

In Luzerne County, Pennsylvania, the courthouse in Wilkes-Barre was supposed to be the place where a child got a second chance. [L]

The children who came through it had done small things. A schoolyard fight. A bit of shoplifting. A sarcastic page online. The kind of thing that, in most towns, ends with a warning and a ride home.

Here, it ended somewhere else.

They would stand in front of Judge Mark Ciavarella. [L: name/role] Many of them had no lawyer — and no one told them they were allowed one. The hearings were fast. Sometimes a child was found delinquent and taken away in the time it takes to microwave a meal. Handcuffs. A van. A locked door that closed behind them.

Parents sat in the back of that room and watched it happen, and were told this was justice. They believed it, because who doesn't believe a judge?

That was the first thing he was selling. Not the children yet. Their trust.

## ACT 2 — 仕組み（金の流れ・因果の解剖） [S05 S07 S08 S09 S15]

Here is how it actually worked.

There were two men in robes. Mark Ciavarella, who sat in the juvenile court. And Michael Conahan, the senior judge who controlled the county's money. [L: names/roles]

The county had run its own juvenile detention center — a public one. Conahan quietly starved it of funding and helped shut it down. [L] Now there was nowhere to send a child but a new, private, for-profit facility. Two of them, in fact: PA Child Care, and its sister site to the west. [L: PA Child Care / Western PA Child Care]

A private jail only makes money when its beds are full. And beds were filled one child at a time — by the judge upstairs.

Between 2003 and 2008, the builder and the owner of those facilities paid the two judges roughly two and a half to two point eight million dollars. [L: $2.6M–$2.8M / Robert Mericle, Robert Powell / 2003–2008] They called it finder's fees. Everyone else would come to call it something simpler.

Every hearing that felt too fast, every child sent away for something small — was not a mistake, and not a bad mood on the bench. It was inventory. A living child, converted into a line on a ledger, and a payment in an envelope.

## ACT 3 — 壊れた一家（人間の代償・最も暗い底） [S06 S10 S11 S18]

Numbers hide people. So look at one.

Ed Kenzakoski was seventeen. A wrestler, with a father who had dreams for him. He was brought before Ciavarella over something minor, and he was sent away. [L: Edward Kenzakoski, age, offense]

He came back changed. The boy who went in did not fully come out. For years afterward he carried it, and one day, at twenty-three, he took his own life. [L: age/outcome — 自死は事実のみ・方法描写なし]

His mother is named Sandy Fonzo. [L] And when Mark Ciavarella walked out of a courthouse years later — this time as the accused — she was waiting on the steps. She screamed at him across the pavement, in front of every camera, the grief of a mother who had buried her son. He had no answer for her. There is no answer for her.

Multiply that by a town. By thousands of children who were told, at thirteen, at fifteen, that they were criminals — and believed it. Some never recovered. That is the part no sentence gives back.

## ACT 4 — 暴かれる（転回：司法が司法を裁く） [S12 S13 S14 S16 S17]

It almost worked forever.

Because who would listen to a kid with a record, or a parent who "should have raised them better"? For years, the complaints went nowhere.

Then a mother made one more phone call — to a small group of lawyers in Philadelphia called the Juvenile Law Center. [L] They pulled the files. And what they found wasn't a few harsh sentences. It was a pattern: hundreds of children, again and again, adjudicated in minutes, without counsel, and shipped to the same private beds. [L]

They took it to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. And the court did something almost unheard of. It erased the record of Ciavarella's courtroom — vacating thousands of cases, wiping the convictions of as many as several thousand children clean. [L: ~2,251 vacated / up to ~4,000–6,500 covered] As if the state itself was admitting: none of this should ever have counted.

Then the law turned on the men who had sold it. Michael Conahan pleaded guilty and was sentenced to seventeen and a half years. [L] Mark Ciavarella went to trial, was convicted of racketeering, and was sentenced to twenty-eight. [L: 12/39 counts, RICO, 28y] Later, the two of them were ordered to pay more than two hundred million dollars to the families they had harmed. [L: $200M+ civil]

It was, at last, a kind of justice. But ask Sandy Fonzo if it was enough.

## ED — 余韻＋射程＋登録CTA [S19 S20 Bookends]

We tell ourselves the powerful are held to a higher standard. That a courtroom is the one room where money is supposed to stay outside the door.

For thousands of children in one American county, that promise was for sale — and the price, per child, was a few thousand dollars.

The judges are named and gone now. One went to prison for decades. The other, years later, had his sentence quietly commuted. [L: Conahan commuted 2024] The children keep their years.

If a story like this should be seen and not forgotten, subscribe — the next case we tell is about [NEXT-EP HOOK, TBD]. Because the line between an ordinary family and this one is thinner than any of us would like to believe.
