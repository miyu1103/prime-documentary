## HOOK  (0:00–0:08 · 最後に書いた · flash-forward)
For nearly thirty years, the state of Alabama kept a date to kill Anthony Ray Hinton — for two murders he did not commit. The entire case rested on a single bullet. And that bullet, it turned out, matched nothing at all.
【OST: 30 YEARS · INNOCENT】

## OPENING  (金 BrandOpening の後 · thesis)
This is the story of how one piece of "science" and a broke defense sent an innocent man to death row for nearly three decades — how the system almost killed him for it — and how it never managed to take the one thing that kept him alive.
【OST: ANTHONY RAY HINTON · ALABAMA】

## ACT I — 逮捕  (アリバイと"確実"の芽)
In 1985, around Birmingham, Alabama, two fast-food managers were murdered in separate late-night robberies — John Davidson in February, Thomas Wayne Vason in July. Both were killed for the cash in a restaurant safe. There were no eyewitnesses to either killing. There were no fingerprints. For months, the cases went nowhere — and an unsolved murder of a working man is exactly the kind of case a police department badly needs to close.

Then, weeks later, a third restaurant was robbed. This time the manager, a man named Sidney Smotherman, survived being shot at — and later picked a face out of a photo lineup. The face belonged to a twenty-nine-year-old Black man named Anthony Ray Hinton.

That identification is the only reason Hinton entered this story at all. There was nothing else tying him to the first two murders — no witness, no print, no confession. Nothing except what the police believed a gun could tell them.

But here is the fact the jury would be asked to set aside. At the moment of that third robbery, Anthony Ray Hinton was fifteen miles away, locked inside a supermarket warehouse, working a night shift. He was clocked in. A supervisor could place him there. His own time card could place him there. He could not have been in two places at once.

In almost any other case, that is where a prosecution ends. A man locked inside a warehouse, on the clock, miles away, is not your suspect. But the state had already decided who it was looking for — and it believed it had found something that could beat any alibi at all.
【OST: He was clocked in, 15 miles away.】

## ACT II — 崩れた裁判  (制度の欠陥)
That something was the bullets.

Prosecutors claimed the bullets from the crime scenes had been fired from an **old .38-caliber revolver** that belonged to Hinton's mother, recovered from her home. There were no fingerprints. No eyewitness who saw the murders. No confession. There were only those bullets, and a gun that had sat, for years, in an aging woman's house. If the bullets matched that revolver, the case was closed. If they didn't, the state had nothing. The entire question of whether a man would live or die came down to a duel between firearms experts.

And this is where being poor decides everything. To fight the state's ballistics claim, Hinton needed a real forensic expert of his own. But his court-appointed lawyer was working under a belief that turned out to be false: he thought Alabama law capped the money for a defense expert at just **one thousand dollars**. There was no such cap. The law would have paid for a qualified expert, if he had only asked for it. He didn't ask. He took the thousand dollars, and with it he hired the only person willing to work for that price.

The man he found was a civil engineer named Andrew Payne. On the stand, under cross-examination, Payne admitted that he had sight in **only one eye** — and that he could not properly operate the comparison microscope, the very instrument used to match a bullet to a gun. The prosecutor took him apart in front of the jury, piece by piece. The one witness standing between Anthony Ray Hinton and the electric chair could not see, and could not work his own equipment.

Understand what that thousand-dollar mistake really was. In a case built entirely on the science of matching a bullet to a gun, a man's life depended on having a scientist who could actually do it. The money was there for the asking. His lawyer, wrongly believing it wasn't, never asked. That single unasked question is what sent an innocent man toward death.

The jury took about an hour. In September of 1986, they convicted him of both murders, and he was sentenced to die.

Hinton always said what he believed put him there. He was Black. He was poor. He could not afford a defense that could fight back. And in Alabama, in 1986, that was enough.
【OST: Black. Poor. Sentenced to die.】

## ACT III — 30年  (時間と尊厳)
He was sent to a cell the size of a bathroom on Alabama's death row. And he would stay there for the better part of thirty years.

For the first three years, he barely spoke — swallowed by rage and grief at a system that would not listen. And then Anthony Ray Hinton made the decision that is the heart of this story. He decided that a cell could hold his body, but it would not hold his mind.

He started a book club — condemned men meeting among the law books to argue about writers like James Baldwin. In his imagination, he simply left. He traveled the world. He married movie stars. He played center field for the New York Yankees. He found humor in a place with no reason for any. You can lock up a man's body, he understood. You cannot lock up what he allows himself to imagine.

But imagination is not innocence, and the years kept coming. He had founded that book club with a handful of other condemned men — and one by one, over the years, he watched its members walked past his cell to be executed, until he was the last of them still breathing. Over his decades on the row, Hinton says he counted **fifty-four men** taken to the electric chair, close enough to know each one. Any year, it could be his door.

Then a lawyer named **Bryan Stevenson**, and his Equal Justice Initiative, took Hinton's case — and would fight it for the next sixteen years. Sixteen years spent trying to make a state admit it had condemned the wrong man. In 2002, Stevenson did the one thing Hinton's first defense never could. He put **three of the most qualified firearms experts in the country** on the stand — one of them from the FBI — and they testified, plainly, that the crime bullets could not be matched to that revolver. They could not even be certain the bullets had come from a single gun.

This was not a loophole or a clever defense. It was the plain truth of the physical evidence, told by the best in the field. The one thread the entire death sentence hung from had never actually held. And still — for more than a decade after that — the state of Alabama refused to let him go.
【OST: The bullets matched nothing. He stayed on death row.】

## ACT IV — 光  (逆転)
In the end, it took the Supreme Court of the United States.

In 2014, in *Hinton v. Alabama*, the Court ruled — and it was **unanimous, nine to nothing**. Every justice agreed that Hinton's original trial had been unconstitutional, because his lawyer's failure to seek the money for a real expert — a failure built on that false belief about a thousand-dollar cap — had denied him a real defense. The right to a lawyer, the Court has always said, means the right to a lawyer who does the basic work. In a case that lived and died on expert testimony, asking for the money to hire a competent expert was as basic as it gets. The verdict was thrown out. The case was sent back.

And when the state finally had to prove its ballistics match again, with modern experts, it couldn't. The evidence that had sent him to death row simply could not be reproduced — because it had never been true. With no case left, prosecutors moved to drop the charges.

On April 3rd, 2015, Anthony Ray Hinton walked out of prison a free man, after nearly thirty years — the hundred-and-fifty-second person freed from death row in America after being wrongly condemned to die. He stepped into the sunlight, and he said the words that would become the title of his book: the sun does shine.
【OST: 2014 — 9–0. Freed 2015.】

The two murders he spent thirty years condemned for were never solved.

## ENDING
Think about what nearly killed him. Not a lie anyone could see through at a glance — the opposite. A neat, confident, official-sounding claim that a bullet matched a gun. It looked like science. It looked certain. And almost no one with the power to stop it ever checked whether it was actually true.

That is the quiet danger in this story. The evidence that is most dangerous is not the evidence that looks weak. It is the evidence that looks certain — because certainty is exactly what stops us from looking closer.

Anthony Ray Hinton lost thirty years to a bullet that matched nothing, and a defense that could not see. He walked out with his humor, his imagination, and his life — which, when a state spends three decades trying to end it, is its own kind of victory.

If a system could do this to an innocent man, and very nearly get away with it, hit like — so that it's a little harder to look away.
【OST: Certainty is not proof.】
