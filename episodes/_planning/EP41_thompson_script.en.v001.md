## 【BODY 第1幕】The Choice — 逮捕と、口を封じられた選択
役割: cold-open question を人に接地。escalating stakes。時系列を一度だけ折る（Deegan告白の先出しティーズ）。決め所同期語 [robbery first / type O / buried]。
画: シルエットの陪審席／空の証人席／血の付いた布（非グラフィック・象徴）／閉じたファイル。

Twelve strangers sit in a jury box, and every one of them believes a version of your life that is not true. You know a single fact that could set you free. And the one office built to find the truth is sitting on the proof of it, because that truth would cost them a conviction. This is not a bad dream. It happened to a man named John Thompson.

New Orleans, 1984. A man named Raymond Liuzza Jr. is shot and killed. The city wants an answer, and it wants one fast. Early in 1985, the answer has a name. John Thompson.

The police tie him to something else, too — an attempted armed robbery from weeks apart. In that robbery, the attacker had bled, and his blood had soaked into a victim's clothing. One of the men in that prosecutor's office would carry a secret about that blood for nine years, almost to his grave. But that is a later story.

Then the prosecutors make the decision that defines everything that follows. They try the robbery first.

The reason is cold. A conviction is a weapon. If Thompson takes the stand at his murder trial and swears he did not do this, a prosecutor can rise, turn to the jury, and say that this man is a convicted armed robber, so do not believe a word. So the robbery goes first, and they win it. With that conviction in hand, John Thompson is left with no safe way to testify at the trial where his life is on the line.

He stays silent. The jury never hears him say a single word. They find him guilty of murder, and they send him to death row.

Now, the blood on the victim's clothes. The State's own crime lab had already typed it: type B. John Thompson's blood is type O. It was never his. The report existed. It sat in a file. And not one prosecutor handed it to the defense, though the Constitution demanded that they do.

One page could have broken the whole chain before it began. It stayed buried for fourteen years.

## 【BODY 第2幕】The Wait — 死刑囚房の14年と、死の床の告白
役割: 尊厳喪失（象徴表現）＋open loop を後半へ。設計された沈黙で不作為を演出。決め所同期語 [steel door / confession / the twentieth of May]。
画: 独房天井のグラデ変化／鉄扉／めくれるカレンダー／夜の廊下のシルエット。裸の身体検査等は一切描かない。

Death row is a small place. A single cell. A steel door. The same ceiling, morning after morning, for years.

Fourteen years is not a number when you are the one inside it. Fourteen winters you only hear about. The lock seating twice a day, every day, until you stop noticing it — and then, some nights, you cannot stop hearing it. You learn the exact shade the ceiling turns at dawn. And all the while, beyond the walls, the paperwork of your own death keeps moving forward on a schedule, signed by people who will never once look you in the face.

John Thompson lived that for fourteen years on death row, eighteen behind bars in all. Outside, the world went on without him.

Then, in 1994, a man begins to die. His name is Gerry Deegan, and he had been one of the prosecutors on Thompson's robbery case. Cancer is taking him now, and something else is taking him too. He tells a former colleague a secret he has carried for nine years: in the Thompson case, he hid the blood evidence that pointed away from the accused. He says it out loud, to another lawyer.

That is a dying man admitting he helped send someone to death row, then handing the secret to a colleague like a stone too heavy to keep holding. And still nothing happened. No call. No motion filed.

〔SILENCE 2.2s, BGM mute, SFX steel-door tail only — 夜の廊下シルエットのみ。不作為を音で描く〕

The secret settled back into the dark, and Thompson kept waiting to die. You would think that surely, now, someone stops this.

No one did.

Years pass. The State picks the day. On the sixteenth of April, 1999, Louisiana sets John Thompson's execution date, and marks it for the twentieth of May. A little over a month left to live.

With those weeks bleeding away, Thompson's private investigator is tearing through old files, chasing anything at all. And in late April, 1999, he finds it — the crime lab report, the blood type, the proof on paper that had been sitting in the system the entire time.

The execution is stopped. Not by anyone's conscience, but by an investigator, with almost no time left on the clock.

## 【BODY 第3幕】The Verdict — 無罪、1,400万ドル、そして5対4
役割: 僅差判決の緊張。争点は多数意見/反対意見に中立帰属。命令デバイスはここで1回だけ使う。決め所同期語 [not guilty / fourteen million / five to four / standard operating procedure]。
画: 空の陪審席→最高裁列柱→分割された9つの席（顔なし）。判決文はillegibleに雰囲気だけ。

2003. Thompson is tried again for the murder, the charge that had put him on death row. This time the jury hears everything the first one never did. When they come back, it takes only minutes. Not guilty.

The same State that had spent eighteen years insisting he was a killer could not, when finally forced to show its whole hand, convince a single jury of it.

Eighteen years, gone. So John Thompson does the one thing this whole story has been building toward. He sues, not the police, but the prosecutor's office. Orleans Parish. Its District Attorney, Harry Connick. His claim is simple and enormous: this office never trained its lawyers to follow the most basic rule of a fair trial, which is to turn over the evidence that helps the accused.

A jury of ordinary citizens hears it, and they agree. They award John Thompson fourteen million dollars, one figure standing in for eighteen stolen years. The court adds more than a million on top, in fees and costs.

For one moment, the answer to our question is yes. Yes, you can make them pay.

And then the case begins to climb, all the way to the Supreme Court of the United States. The arguments come in October of 2010. The decision lands on the twenty-ninth of March, 2011.

Five to four. The Court reverses. Every dollar, gone.

Understand what those five votes undid. Not a technicality. A jury of citizens had weighed eighteen years of a man's life and settled on a number, and five people in Washington erased it with their signatures. Thompson, who had already survived the State's attempt to kill him, now had to watch it walk away owing him nothing.

Justice Clarence Thomas writes for the majority, and his logic runs on a single rule that never blinks. Prosecutors, he says, are trained lawyers. They go to law school. They know their duties. So you cannot hold their office responsible for one violation by calling it poor training, not without a pattern of similar failures, proven ahead of time. One buried report, however deadly, is not a pattern. Ordinarily, he writes, you need more than that.

There had been other reversals in Louisiana, the majority allowed — four in the ten years before this trial, thrown out for hidden evidence. But those, the Court said, were different enough that they gave no real warning.

Justice Scalia goes further. What Deegan did, he suggests, was not a training gap at all. It has the look of a deliberate, dishonest act by one man, and you cannot hand the whole office the bill for that.

Four justices refuse to sign.

Ruth Bader Ginsburg reads her dissent aloud from the bench. In Orleans Parish, she says, indifference to this rule was not one man's mistake. It was how the work got done. Four prosecutors knew the blood was not his. Four. And every one of them let the ceiling of that cell turn gray at dawn, fourteen years running. She calls the failure pervasive, standard operating procedure.

But she is writing in dissent. And a dissent frees no one and pays no one. It is the sound of losing, written down.

## 【BODY 第4幕】The Reach — これは、あなたの話だ
役割: 普遍化。二人称で射程を撃つ。抽象語は具体像へ。決め所同期語 [Brady / not one dollar / the person holding the file]。
画: 名もなき人物のシルエットが机の向こうのフォルダを見る／黒背景に一枚の紙。

Now the story comes all the way back to you.

The rule those prosecutors broke has a name. Brady. One word for a promise: the State cannot keep the thing that would prove you innocent. Picture that thing as a single sheet of paper — a lab report, blood type B beside a defendant who is type O. On its own it is nothing: ink, a date, a technician's initials. In a jury's hands, it is the difference between a man who did it and a man who did not. Thompson's sheet sat in a folder while twelve people decided he should die. Handing it over would have cost the office a conviction, so the office kept it.

Connick versus Thompson did not erase the rule. Hiding that page is still wrong. But the case asked a narrower question — not whether it was wrong, but whether you can make them pay for it — and the answer hollowed the rule from the inside. To win, Thompson had to prove not a single buried report but a pattern: that the office knew its lawyers would hide evidence and trained them anyway. One hidden page, even one that nearly kills you, is not enough. A jury said fourteen million dollars. The Court said the office owed none of it. Not fourteen million. Not one dollar.

So the door the jury opened, the Court quietly closed — and it closed for everyone standing behind him. The office with the power to end your life may be holding the page that would save it, and after this, what stands between you and that page is not the law. It is whether the person holding the folder decides, on their own, to open it.

That is the country John Thompson walked back out into. A free man, told in the calm language of the law that eighteen years and fourteen death warrants had a price, and the price was zero.
