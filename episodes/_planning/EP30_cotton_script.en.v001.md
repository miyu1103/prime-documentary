## OPENING  (金 BrandOpening の後 · thesis)
This is the story of how the most careful, most confident eyewitness imaginable sent an innocent man to prison for more than a decade — how the real culprit was not a liar, but human memory itself — and how the two of them ended up, years later, standing on the same side.
【OST: RONALD COTTON · JENNIFER THOMPSON】

## ACT I — 顔  (確信の誕生)
In the summer of 1984, in Burlington, North Carolina, a twenty-two-year-old college student named Jennifer Thompson was attacked in her own apartment at knifepoint. In the middle of the worst night of her life, she made a decision that most people never could. She forced herself to study her attacker — his face, his voice, every detail she could hold onto — so that if she lived, she could make certain he was caught.

She did live. And she was determined to get it right. That determination matters, because everything that went wrong here went wrong in spite of it.

Police built a composite sketch from her description. A tip brought in a name — Ronald Cotton — and a photograph. Detectives laid out a lineup of six photos, and Thompson studied them hard. She narrowed it to two. "It's between these," she said, before finally settling on one: Ronald Cotton. Days later, they brought her in for a physical lineup — real men, standing in a row — and again, she chose Cotton.

Now hold on to one detail, because it is the hinge of this entire story. Ronald Cotton was the only person who appeared in **both** the photo lineup and the live lineup. By the time she saw him standing in front of her, she had already spent time studying his photograph. She may not have been recognizing her attacker at all. She may have been recognizing the picture the police had shown her.

And when she chose him, the detectives told her she'd done a great job. Later, when she asked whether she had picked the right man, they told her yes. Each reassurance did something quiet and permanent. It took a woman who had hesitated between two photographs and slowly, invisibly, turned her into a witness who was absolutely, unshakably sure.

None of this was a trick played on a foolish woman. It is how memory works in all of us. A face glimpsed in terror is not filed away like a photograph; it is a rough sketch — and every time we are asked to look again, the mind quietly fills in the blanks, often with whatever it was just shown. Add a confident "you did great" from someone in authority, and a tentative guess can harden into a certainty that feels, from the inside, exactly like the truth.
【OST: The only man in both lineups.】

## ACT II — 二人の男  (綻び)
By the time of the trial, that certainty was total. Jennifer Thompson took the stand, looked directly at Ronald Cotton, and told the jury: that is the man. There is a special, overwhelming power in a victim who is that certain. In 1985, it was enough to convict Ronald Cotton and to send him to prison for the rest of his life.

Cotton said, from the very first day, that they had the wrong man. But no one had a reason to believe him. He had the word of a man who claimed to be innocent; she had the certainty of the woman who had survived the crime. In a courtroom, certainty wins that contest almost every single time. His conviction was later overturned on appeal — and then, at a second trial, he was convicted all over again, this time for both attacks that night. By now he had spent years fighting to be heard, from inside a cell, against the one thing a jury trusts most: a victim's certain word. He was losing, and running out of ways to be believed.

But inside the prison, something happened that stories like this almost never give you. Ronald Cotton crossed paths with another inmate — a man named **Bobby Poole**, locked up for other sexual assaults — and the resemblance stopped him cold. Poole looked like him. So much like him that, it was said, even guards confused the two men. And then Cotton learned something more: Poole had bragged to a fellow prisoner that Ronald Cotton was doing time for a crime that Poole himself had committed.

Cotton fought to get that in front of a court, and at his second trial, the man he believed was the real attacker was actually brought into the courtroom, where Jennifer Thompson could see him with her own eyes. She looked at Bobby Poole — the man who had truly assaulted her — and felt nothing. A stranger. The real perpetrator stood only a few feet away, and her memory did not so much as flicker.

The court let the conviction stand. Ronald Cotton went back to prison. And the question the whole case now turned on was terrible in its simplicity: if the witness could look directly at the truth and not recognize it, what on earth would it take to prove it?
【OST: The real attacker was in the room. She felt nothing.】

## ACT III — 真実  (科学が勝つ)
It would take a science that, in 1984, had barely been born.

Ronald Cotton served more than ten years. And across those years, a new tool was quietly maturing in laboratories — the ability to read a person's identity out of their DNA. Cotton pushed for it, again and again. The physical evidence from the case had been preserved, and in 1995 it was finally sent for testing to a lab in Burlington called **LabCorp**.

The result did two things at once. It **excluded Ronald Cotton** completely. And it **matched Bobby Poole** — the man Thompson's memory had sworn was a total stranger.

Sit with what that means, because it is the whole point. The certainty that had convicted an innocent man was not a lie. It was something more unsettling. It was honest. Jennifer Thompson had been completely sincere and completely wrong — because memory is not a recording you play back untouched. It is a story your mind rebuilds every time you reach for it, and it can be quietly edited by a photograph you were shown, or a detective's kind reassurance, without you ever feeling the change.

That is what makes an error like this so dangerous. A liar can be caught — in a nervous glance, a story that doesn't hold together. But an honest witness who is certain and wrong carries no tell at all. Jennifer Thompson's sincerity was completely real, and it was precisely that sincerity that made her so convincing to a jury, and so devastating to an innocent man.

On June 30th, 1995, after more than a decade behind bars for a crime he did not commit, Ronald Cotton walked out of prison. He was pardoned, and the state paid him a small sum for what it had taken — a little over a hundred thousand dollars, set against eleven stolen years, as if a decade of a man's life could be balanced out with a check. Bobby Poole later pleaded guilty to the attacks.

But the truth arrived carrying a cruelty of its own. Because now Jennifer Thompson had to live with the knowledge that her certainty — careful, well-meant, absolute — had cost an innocent man eleven years of his life. And the question that hung over both of them was whether Ronald Cotton could ever forgive the woman who had put him there.
【OST: DNA cleared him — after 11 years.】

## ACT IV — 赦し  (ペイオフ)
Here is his answer.

Ronald Cotton wanted to meet her. But he waited — nearly two years — until she was ready. And in 1997, in a small church office, Jennifer Thompson finally sat down across from the man she had sent to prison. She was shaking. She told him that even if she spent every remaining day of her life apologizing, it would never be enough.

Ronald Cotton told her that he had already forgiven her — years earlier. That he was not angry. That he did not want her to carry it anymore. He wanted them both to be free of it.

And then something extraordinary grew out of the wreckage. They became friends — real, close friends. Together they wrote a book about what had happened to them both, called *Picking Cotton*. Together, they began to travel the country, telling their story to police departments and law schools and courtrooms, so that the next confident witness could not convict the next innocent man. Because of cases like theirs, the way police show a witness a lineup was reformed — done blind, done carefully, so that a memory can no longer be nudged toward the wrong face. The detective who had once built the case against Ronald Cotton became one of those reformers himself, changing how his own officers ran a lineup so the same mistake could not happen again. And years later the survivor and the man she had wrongly accused were honored together, side by side — two people with every reason to be enemies, standing instead as partners.

And there is one last detail, almost too perfect to be true. After he was released, Ronald Cotton took a job — at LabCorp. The very laboratory whose test had finally set him free.
【OST: They forgave. Then they fought — together.】

## ENDING
So go back to the beginning — to a woman looking her attacker in the eyes and being absolutely sure. She was not careless. She was not lying. She did everything a good witness is supposed to do — and she still sent an innocent man to prison for eleven years.

That is the part that should stay with you. The problem was never that Jennifer Thompson wasn't certain enough. The problem is that certainty and truth are not the same thing — and a courtroom, a jury, all of us, we tend to treat them as if they are. Jennifer Thompson has spent years now saying it out loud, so that no one can miss it: memory can be honest, and confident, and still be completely wrong.

An innocent man lost eleven years to a memory that was honestly, confidently wrong. And instead of spending the rest of their lives as the accuser and the accused, two people decided to spend it making sure it happens to fewer others.

If you have ever been sure — really, truly sure — and turned out to be wrong, hit like, so that a jury somewhere remembers that it's possible too.
【OST: Certainty is not proof.】
