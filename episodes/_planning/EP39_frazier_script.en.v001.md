## ACT I — 1987年8月13日  (発端)

Oxford Township, Adams County, Pennsylvania. Farm country, two hours west of Philadelphia, where the corn comes up to the shoulder in August.

On the morning of August 13, 1987, a neighbor saw Edna Laughman out in her yard. She was eighty-five years old. Later that day she was found dead inside her own house.

[SILENCE 2.5秒。無人の台所、網戸、床の錠剤の瓶。象徴のみ。]

She had been sexually assaulted. Law and Crime reported that she was beaten and suffocated. The Innocence Project and the legal scholar Brandon Garrett's exoneration record both describe the suffocation the same way: pills, forced down her throat.

A killing like that, in a township like that, does not stay quiet, and within weeks the state police had a name from the neighborhood. A twenty-four-year-old man who lived nearby, with an IQ of 70, who functioned — by the assessments in his own case file — at roughly the level of a ten-year-old child.

Then an officer told him that his fingerprints had been found at the scene.

Barry had no way to check that. He could not ask to see the report, and he could not know there was no report to ask for. He did not know that a man in a room like this one, in another state, in another decade, had already tried to stop an interview and been talked past, and that the answer a court gave that man was sitting in the room with him now.

Barry confessed.

[SILENCE 2秒。取調室の椅子二脚、机に置かれた指紋カード、閉まる扉。]

What came out of that room was a statement in his own name. It would take sixteen years and a private laboratory to take apart what was wrong with it.

【OST: IQ 70. TOLD: "YOUR PRINTS ARE THERE."】

## ACT II — 供述  (不条理)

The statement did not fit the house.

He described getting in through a window that had not been disturbed. He said he killed her on August 12, though a neighbor had seen her alive in her yard on the morning of the 13th. A confession should not need a neighbor to tell it what day it was.

Then there was his blood. The victim was an A secretor, Barry Laughman was a B secretor, and the vaginal swab carried A-type material with no B antigen on it at all. In 1987, before DNA typing, that was the strongest exclusion science could offer. The body had already answered. Not this man.

At trial the state's chemist offered the jury a way around it. Bacteria, she speculated, can convert one blood group substance into another — given enough time, A material could become B, and B could become A. That testimony was wrong, and the Innocence Project's record of the case says it plainly. The analyst testified incorrectly. But it was said under oath, by someone the jury had every reason to trust, and the jury had in front of it a statement in Barry's name that agreed with the officers and disagreed with the swab.

[SILENCE 2秒。法廷の空席と、ガラス瓶の並んだ棚。]

And there was the card the prosecution held back for last. Barry's statement contained details of the crime that the public did not have. That she had been sexually assaulted. That the pills forced into her mouth were what killed her. A wound to her head that, according to the case record, was not visible until the autopsy.

Officers testified that they had never given those details to Barry, or to his family.

[SILENCE 2秒。検死台のステンレスの縁だけ。人体は映さない。]

His federal complaint says otherwise. It says the details came from the officers. That claim has never been tried.

On December 16, 1988, Barry Laughman was convicted of murder, rape, robbery and burglary, and sentenced to life. Twenty-five years old.

【OST: DEC 16, 1988 · LIFE】

## ACT III — Frazier v. Cupp  (最高裁の線)

Portland, Oregon. September 22, 1964. A man named Russell Anton Marleau was killed, and police brought in a young Marine named Martin Frazier.

Frazier did not want to talk, so an officer told him something that was not true: that his cousin, Jerry Lee Rawls, had been brought in, and that Rawls had confessed. The opinion states it without softening. The officer questioning petitioner told him, falsely, that Rawls had been brought in and that he had confessed.

Then Frazier said this. "I think I had better get a lawyer before I talk any more. I am going to get into trouble more than I am in now."

The officer answered. "You can't be in any more trouble than you are in now."

The questioning continued, and Frazier confessed, and he was convicted of second-degree murder — and four and a half years later, after argument that February, Justice Thurgood Marshall handed down the opinion that answered him.

One sentence has outlived everything else in the case. "The fact that the police misrepresented the statements that Rawls had made is, while relevant, insufficient in our view to make this otherwise voluntary confession inadmissible."

The Court did not bless the lie. It weighed it, and found it too light, by itself, to sink the confession. The test, the Court said, citing Clewis v. Texas, is the totality of the circumstances. Relevant means it counts. It goes onto the scale with everything else that was in the room, and nothing on that scale decides the question alone.

Two things get left out. Frazier was tried after Escobedo and before Miranda, and the Court said so itself — Miranda does not apply to this case. And the line about a lawyer, the one that sounds so plain in the transcript, the Court held was neither as clear nor as unambiguous as the request Escobedo had made; an officer might have taken it for a passing comment rather than a demand to stop. Frazier had been given partial warnings, and the Court put those in the balance too.

So the exchange in that room turns over as you listen to it a second time. The sailor asking for a lawyer was not, in law, asking.

Once Miranda swallowed the rest, what survived from 1969 is narrow. A lie about the evidence, standing alone, does not make a confession inadmissible.

That is the rule that was waiting in the room when an officer told Barry Laughman about a fingerprint.

【OST: FRAZIER v. CUPP · 394 U.S. 731 (1969) · ARGUED FEB 26, 1969 · DECIDED APR 22, 1969】

## ACT IV — 十六年後  (射程)

In 1993, DNA testing was tried. The technology of the day — PCR, DQ Alpha — came back inconclusive. It took another decade. In November 2003 the laboratory Orchid Cellmark ran Y-STR testing on the same evidence, and the result excluded Barry Laughman.

He was released that month to supervised home confinement. In August 2004 a new trial was granted, and on August 26, 2004, the Adams County District Attorney, Shawn C. Wagner, dropped every charge. (The federal court's later opinion dates the new trial and the withdrawal of charges to August 24; the records differ by a couple of days.)

Sixteen years. He had gone in at twenty-five and come out at forty.

[SILENCE 3秒。開いた鉄扉の外側、朝の駐車場。無人。]

He sued the men who had taken them. A federal court held that a jury could hear it. No jury ever did.

【OST: 42 U.S.C. §1983 · M.D. PA. 1:05-cv-01033 · 4TH & 14TH AMENDMENTS · HOLTZ / BLEVINS / ROADCAP · COMMONWEALTH, COUNTY & DA DISMISSED MAR 17, 2006 · SUMMARY JUDGMENT DENIED AUG 16, 2007 — NOT A VERDICT】

Then, in August 2018, the same vaginal swab that had cleared him was sent to a private laboratory, Parabon NanoLabs, for genetic genealogy. The analysis pointed to a family — a common ancestor surnamed Laughman or Speelman — and then to one man. On July 14, 2021, Adams County District Attorney Brian Sinnett announced that Christopher Speelman's DNA was, in his words, an absolute match to the crime scene evidence. Barry Laughman was fifty-eight the day another man's name was read out in place of his own.

On June 22, 2023, Chris Joseph Larry Speelman, then fifty-nine, pleaded guilty to third-degree murder and burglary and no contest to rape. Judge Thomas Campbell sentenced him to twenty-five to fifty years, with no parole eligibility until 2046.

Barry Laughman lived to see it. He died on March 21, 2024, at Jefferson Einstein Hospital in Philadelphia, at sixty.

He had twenty years of freedom, and almost none of them are in the record this film is built from. The paperwork keeps his conviction, his exclusion, his lawsuit and his death, and almost nothing of the man in between.

[SILENCE 2.5秒。空の椅子、朝の窓。人物は映さない。]

【OST: EXONERATED 2004 · DIED 2024】

Of the 257 cases the Innocence Project has won, the average client served sixteen years. The same sixteen. Lay those 257 sentences end to end and they come to four thousand, one hundred and two years.

[SILENCE 4秒。画面は数字のみ: 4,102 YEARS。]

【OST: 257 CASES · 29% FALSE CONFESSION · 62% MISTAKEN EYEWITNESS ID · 205 CLEARED BY DNA · IN AT 27, OUT AT 45】

Among exonerees convicted as children, 34 percent had falsely confessed, against 10 percent of those convicted as adults. A lie with a badge behind it roughly triples the odds that a child will confess to something they did not do.

Barry Laughman was twenty-four. The law counted him as an adult. Nothing else about him did.

Legislatures started with the children. Oregon's Senate Bill 418, carried by Senator Chris Gorsek, a former police officer, was signed by Governor Kate Brown on July 14, 2021: a statement taken from someone under eighteen through information the officer knew to be false is presumed involuntary, and the state must prove otherwise by clear and convincing evidence. Illinois signed its own the next day. Both took effect on January 1, 2022, and by 2026 roughly ten states have some version of it.

【OST: ILLINOIS SB 2122 — PRITZKER, JULY 15, 2021 · WIDELY REPORTED AS THE FIRST IN THE NATION; BY SIGNATURE, OREGON WAS A DAY AHEAD】

For adults, no state has a law that generally forbids an officer from lying to your face about the evidence.

There is a line, though. In State v. Cayward, decided by Florida's Second District Court of Appeal on November 15, 1989, officers manufactured two fake scientific reports — one on the letterhead of the Florida Department of Criminal Law Enforcement, one on the letterhead of a company called Life Codes, Inc. — and showed the suspect a document saying his semen had been found on the victim's underwear. The court upheld the suppression of his confession. Spoken deception, it said, is one thing; fabricated documents that impersonate a real institution have no place in the criminal justice system. Florida's Supreme Court declined review in 1990 and never ruled on the merits.

They may say it. In some courts, they may not print it.

Kassin and six colleagues, in Police-Induced Confessions 2.0, published in Law and Human Behavior in 2025 and endorsed as a Scientific Review Paper by the American Psychology-Law Society, conclude that confrontational interrogation built on false evidence raises the risk that an innocent person confesses. They recommend recording interrogations end to end, requiring evidence-based suspicion before questioning begins, and restricting the false evidence ploy itself. In January 2019 a court in the Bronx took that research as newly discovered evidence and vacated a conviction built on a confession given at sixteen.

## ENDING  (感情ペイオフ → earned CTA → `BrandEndcard` → 次回引き)

A man with the comprehension of a ten-year-old is told a fingerprint has placed him at a murder. He cannot check it. He cannot demand the report. He does not know that the officer is allowed to say it, or that an opinion written before he was born had already weighed that lie and called it relevant, but not enough.

Everything after that — the trial, the chemist, the life sentence, the sixteen years, a laboratory in 2003 — was the state spending sixteen years undoing one sentence spoken in a room with the door closed.

The print was invented. The permission was not.
