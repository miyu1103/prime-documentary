## 2. OPENING

This is Montgomery, Alabama, and a small debt that turned into a sentence.

The Supreme Court of the United States answered this question a long time ago, in 1983, and the answer was no. A state cannot put you in jail for the one reason that you are too poor to pay what you owe it. This is the story of the woman it happened to anyway, the machine that did it to her, and the distance between what the Constitution says on paper and what a courtroom actually does on an ordinary Tuesday.

## 3. BODY

### ACT 1 — The road that ends at a cell
【Shortest, fastest act. Present tense. Restrained. Symbols only. R2: Harriet Cleveland is never shown; use the citation stack, the suspended license, the empty wallet, the courthouse hallway, the bus stop, the booking clock. Constraint 4: dignity, no sensationalizing of family.】

Harriet Cleveland lives in Montgomery. She gets traffic tickets, the ordinary kind, the kind almost everyone watching this has gotten at least once. What she cannot do is pay them all at the same time.

So the fines sit. And in Alabama, a fine that sits does not stay the same size. It grows.

Her license is suspended for the tickets she has not paid. But a life still has to be lived, so she keeps driving, to look for work, to reach a store, to get anywhere at all. And every time she is stopped driving on that suspended license, there is another citation, another charge, another fee laid on top of the pile. She is out of work. She cannot afford car insurance. Each thing that breaks makes the next thing more expensive.

【beat】

This is what a debt spiral looks like from the inside. It is not one reckless choice. It is a first bill she could not clear, and a system that charged her again for every day she failed to clear it.

Then the arithmetic ends the only way this arithmetic can. A judge in Montgomery signs an order. Pay one thousand five hundred and fifty-four dollars, or serve thirty-one days in jail.

〔CARD: figures per the Fines and Fees Justice Center's account of Cleveland v. City of Montgomery. confidence: medium.〕

And the number is not the cruelest thing on the page. The order was entered without anyone asking the question that was supposed to come before it. Could she pay? There was no hearing on what she had. No finding about the state of her wallet. There was a sum, and a cell waiting if the sum went unmet.

She could not meet it. So Harriet Cleveland was booked into the county jail, and the door closed on a woman whose only proven offense that day was that she was poor.

### ACT 2 — The machine that turns a debt into a sentence
【Medium pace. The turn: how a rule the Supreme Court laid down in 1983 gets violated in practice, at scale, for profit. Symbols: a payment ledger; a rubber stamp; an invoice with a company logo blurred; a long courthouse corridor; a spreadsheet. Constraint 5: describe the system, do not attack named individuals.】

How does that happen, in a country whose highest court forbade it decades earlier? The answer is in the money, because in Montgomery the money had somewhere to go.

Cleveland was not simply fined and sent home to pay. She was placed on probation, and her probation was not run by the county. It was run by a private company, in business to make a profit. Its name was Judicial Correction Services.

〔CARD: Judicial Correction Services (JCS), for-profit probation, founded 2001, based in Georgia. confidence: high.〕

The model has a name of its own. It is called offender-funded probation, and the premise is exactly as blunt as it sounds. The person being punished is made to pay for the machinery of their own punishment. The court hands the case to the company. The company collects the debt, and skims a fee for the service of collecting it.

Cleveland was ordered to pay two hundred dollars a month. Of that two hundred, forty dollars never touched her actual fines. Every month, it went to the company, as its charge for supervising her.

〔CARD: $200/month, of which $40/month to JCS, per the Fines and Fees Justice Center. confidence: medium.〕

Consider the incentive that builds. A company whose income depends on collection has one thing it wants from every case, which is more collection. And here is where the constitutional rule quietly bent. The single question the Supreme Court said must be answered before anyone is jailed, the question of whether a person genuinely cannot pay, was in practice handed to the people whose paychecks depended on the answer being no. Human Rights Watch documented precisely that pattern, courts delegating the ability-to-pay decision to probation-company employees, a conflict of interest wired straight into the design.

And there was one more thing missing from these rooms. In the proceedings where the fines were tallied and the jail time was handed down, people in Cleveland's position were not told they had any right to a lawyer. So the one voice in the room that might have been trained to insist on the words the Constitution required, could she pay, was often the one voice that was never in it.

This was not a small operation, and it was not one bad courtroom. By 2013, that one company was working in at least a hundred Alabama courts, supervising something like thirty-eight thousand people across four states.

And a judge saw it plainly. In 2012, an Alabama circuit judge named Hub Harrington moved to stop the company's practices in a town called Harpersville, and he did not reach for gentle language when he described what he had found. He called it a judicially sanctioned extortion racket. That is a judge, speaking about a court.

### ACT 3 — Bearden, and the difference between a right and a remedy
【Longest, slowest act. Doctrinal core. This is where the promise from the hook pays off, and where all six constraints have to hold. Present tense at the ruling. Keep the human thread alive: return to Cleveland by name inside the doctrine. Constraint 2: Bearden is the Supreme Court line; Cleveland's relief is lower-court, not SCOTUS. Constraint 3: precise holding, no overreach. Constraint 1: never say this is legal.】

This returns us to the promise the country made, the one from 1983. It has a name. Bearden versus Georgia.

〔CARD: Bearden v. Georgia, 461 U.S. 660. Decided May 24, 1983. confidence: high.〕

The man in the case was named Bearden. He pleaded guilty to burglary and theft, and the court sentenced him to a five hundred dollar fine and two hundred and fifty dollars in restitution, on a schedule: a hundred dollars the day of sentencing, another hundred the next day, and the remaining five hundred and fifty within four months. He borrowed the money to make the first two hundred. Then he was laid off. He had a ninth-grade education, he could not read, and with no work and no income, he could not pay the balance. Georgia revoked his probation and sent him to prison for the shortfall.

His case climbed all the way to the Supreme Court, and in the spring of 1983 the Court ruled in his favor. Justice Sandra Day O'Connor wrote the opinion.

【Present tense through the holding.】

The rule she lays down is narrow, and the narrowness is the entire point. A court cannot revoke probation and jail a person for failing to pay a fine unless it first finds one of two things. Either that the person willfully refused to pay when he actually had the resources. Or that he did not make sufficient bona fide efforts to find work or to borrow the money. And even where nonpayment is not willful, the court still has to ask whether some punishment short of a cell would serve. O'Connor writes that when a probationer has made all reasonable efforts and still cannot pay through no fault of his own, it is fundamentally unfair to revoke his probation automatically, without even considering whether an adequate alternative to prison exists.

The Constitution she reaches for is the Fourteenth Amendment, where, the Court says, the principles of due process and equal protection converge.

And Bearden did not arrive out of nowhere. Thirteen years earlier, the Court had already held that a state cannot keep a person locked up past the normal maximum sentence just because he is too poor to pay a fine. Two years after that, it held that a state cannot take a fine and automatically turn it into jail time for the single reason that the defendant is broke and cannot pay it all at once. Bearden was the capstone on that line.

〔CARD: Williams v. Illinois, 399 U.S. 235 (1970); Tate v. Short, 401 U.S. 395 (1971). confidence: high.〕

【beat. This is the honesty turn. Constraints 1, 2, 3. Do not let the ruling inflate into more than it is.】

Now the part a nine-word summary would get wrong.

Bearden did not abolish fines. It did not say the state can never make you pay a dime. Courts can still impose fines, fees, and restitution. They can still punish a person who has the money and simply refuses to hand it over. What the Constitution forbids is smaller and more exact than that, and the exactness matters: jailing a person for the sole reason that they cannot pay, without ever stopping to ask whether they could.

That is the line. And that is the line the order against Harriet Cleveland walked straight across, because no one asked.

So here is the second thing the summary gets wrong. It would be natural to picture the Supreme Court riding back in to rescue her, the way it rescued Bearden. It did not. Cleveland's case never reached the Supreme Court at all. Her freedom did not come from nine justices in Washington. It came from an ordinary lawsuit in a lower court, and from a settlement, announced in late 2014, in the case that carried her name.

The terms of that settlement read like the rule from 1983 being written out a second time, by hand, because the first time had not been enough. No person shall be incarcerated for their inability to pay fines and fees. Ability-to-pay must be determined, on the record, before anyone is jailed. The extra fees layered onto payment plans were to be eliminated. And the City of Montgomery agreed to end its contract with the private probation company.

Sit with the shape of that for a moment. A settlement, in the year 2014, had to promise a city would stop doing the precise thing the Supreme Court had declared unconstitutional in 1983. The law had not changed. The law was already on Harriet Cleveland's side. What had failed was not the rule. It was the enforcement of it, three decades deep.

And the machine did not vanish quietly either. In March of 2015, the Southern Poverty Law Center sued the probation company under a federal racketeering statute. By the end of that year, the company announced it was closing all of its Alabama operations, as one municipality after another cut ties.

## 4. ENDING
【Emotional payoff → resolve the cold-open question → one earned CTA in the last 30 seconds. ENDCARD_SEC=9.00.】

Go back to the number. One thousand five hundred and fifty-four dollars, or thirty-one days.

For anyone with the money, it was not really a choice at all. You write the check, you go home, you never think about it again. The jail sentence on that page existed only for the people who could not reach into a wallet and make it disappear. Which means the thirty-one days were never a punishment for what Harriet Cleveland had done. They were a punishment for what she did not have.

And the spiral that brought her there had a cruelty folded into its very shape. The license they suspended for unpaid tickets was the same license she needed in order to earn the money the tickets demanded. Take away the driving, and the work goes with it. Take away the work, and the debt has nowhere left to go but up. The punishment for owing money was to be made steadily less able to earn it.

The Constitution had already answered her, and answered her clearly, in 1983. The answer simply never made it into the room where she was standing.

This is the quiet distance the whole case lives inside, the distance between a right and a remedy. It is one thing for the Supreme Court to write that you cannot be jailed for being poor. It is another thing entirely for that sentence to be honored by a particular judge, in a particular county, on the morning your name is called. A right you cannot enforce is a promise, and a promise is only as good as the room it is spoken in.

So, the question from the very start. Can they lock you in a cell for the single reason that you cannot pay? The Supreme Court's answer, since 1983, is no, and that answer still stands. But an answer settled in a book in Washington is not the same as an answer obeyed in a courthouse a thousand miles away. The rule was never the weak point. What kept failing, case after case, county after county, was the simple act of following it.
