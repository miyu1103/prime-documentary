## ACT I — THE HUNDRED-YEAR RULE (≈0:22–2:45)
[VO:] Start with what surprises most people. To search your house, the police almost always need a warrant — a judge's signature, probable cause on paper. That's the Fourth Amendment at its strongest. **(VIS: a warrant document; a house icon wrapped in a protective outline.)**
[VO:] But your car is different. And it has been different since 1925. **(VIS: timeline snaps back — "1925.") (SFX: page turn / stamp.)**
[VO:] It was Prohibition. A man named George Carroll was running liquor between Detroit and Grand Rapids. Federal agents stopped his car on the highway, tore open the seat cushions — and pulled out sixty-eight bottles of whisky and gin, hidden behind the upholstery. **(VIS: reconstruction — gloved hands tearing a seat; bottles revealed. Anonymous figures, no real likeness.) (SFX: fabric tear; a StatCounter ticks up to 68.)** [CLM-0002]
[VO:] Carroll argued the search was illegal — no warrant. The case went all the way to the Supreme Court. And the Court created a rule that still governs your glovebox today. **(VIS: the marble courthouse; "Carroll v. United States, 1925.")**
[VO:] The Court said: a house stays put. You can leave an officer to guard it while someone fetches a warrant. But a car? A car can be driven away — out of the city, out of the state — long before any warrant arrives. **(VIS: split screen — a house holding still vs a car accelerating off-frame; the warrant "arriving" too late.) (SFX: engine pull; whoosh.)** [CLM-0001]
[VO:] So the Court carved out what we now call the **automobile exception**: if police have probable cause to believe your car contains contraband, they can search it — no warrant required. **(VIS: kinetic type — "THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION"; the house keeps its shield, the car loses part of its own.)** [CLM-0001]
[VO:] And that word matters more than any other in this whole story. **Probable cause.** Hold on to it. Because it's the difference between a legal search and an illegal one — and we'll come back to exactly where it bites. **(VIS: the words "PROBABLE CAUSE" lock in and glow — an open loop marker.) (SFX: sub-drop.)**

## ACT II — WHAT "PROBABLE CAUSE" ACTUALLY MEANS (≈2:45–5:30)
[VO:] Here's the part the movies get wrong. The automobile exception is **not** a blank check. Being pulled over is not, by itself, permission to search your car. **(VIS: a big red "NOT" stamps over a clip of a routine stop.) (SFX: stamp / soft impact.)** [CLM-0003]
[VO:] A hunch isn't enough either. The officer needs probable cause — a fair, fact-based probability that there's contraband or evidence in the car. A broken taillight doesn't get him into your trunk. **(VIS: a "hunch" meter that fails to reach the line labeled PROBABLE CAUSE; a taillight vs a trunk, with an X between them.) (SFX: ui-tick as the meter climbs, then a dull thud when it falls short.)** [CLM-0003]
[VO:] But once that bar is cleared — once there **is** probable cause — the search can go surprisingly far. **(VIS: the line finally lights up; the car's zones illuminate one by one.) (SFX: rising data-blips.)**
[VO:] The Supreme Court has said the reach of the search is defined by what the police are looking for. If the object could fit in the glovebox, they can open the glovebox. If it could be in the trunk, they can open the trunk. If it could be inside a closed bag on the back seat, they can open the bag. **(VIS: an animated cutaway of a car — glovebox, trunk, a duffel bag — each highlighted as it's named. This is a moving diagram, not a still.) (SFX: a soft click per zone.)** [CLM-0004]
[VO:] They don't even have to prove a separate emergency. The Court has been blunt about it: if the car is drivable and there's probable cause, that's enough. No extra excuse required. **(VIS: a checklist — "readily mobile ✓ / probable cause ✓" → "search: permitted," the third box just… absent.) (SFX: two checks, then a hollow tone where the third would be.)** [CLM-0005]
[VO:] So think about what that means for you. Your car — the place you keep your bag, your phone, your private life in transit — carries far less protection than your living room. One century-old idea about mobility quietly reshaped the privacy of the single most common place Americans spend their time outside the home: behind the wheel. **(VIS: pull back — thousands of cars on a night highway, each a small lit box of private life.) (SFX: ambient highway bed swells.)**
[VO:] Which raises the obvious question. If the car is so exposed… is there anywhere the police still cannot follow it? **(VIS: the bright-line motif flickers at the edge of the frame — the loop tightens.) (SFX: a single low pulse.)**

## ACT III — THE MOTORCYCLE UNDER THE TARP (≈5:30–8:20)
[VO:] To answer that, go to Virginia, and a motorcycle that kept getting away. **(VIS: an orange-and-black motorcycle blurring past a police car twice; "eluded police.") (SFX: a fast pass-by whoosh.)** [CLM-0006]
[VO:] An officer had been chasing a distinctive orange-and-black bike — one he suspected was stolen. One day he tracked it to a house. And there it was: parked in the driveway, right up next to the home, covered by a tarp. **(VIS: the quiet house; the shape under the tarp in the driveway.) (SFX: ambient suburban quiet; a wind rustle on the tarp.)**
[VO:] So the officer walked up the driveway. He lifted the tarp. He ran the plate and the numbers — and confirmed it was stolen. Then he arrested the man staying there, Ryan Collins. **(VIS: footsteps up the driveway; a hand lifts the tarp; the reveal. Anonymous figures.) (SFX: footsteps; the tarp pull; a data-blip on "confirmed.")** [CLM-0006]
[VO:] Now — the officer had good reason to think that bike was stolen. Under the automobile exception, if that motorcycle had been sitting on a public street, this is a routine, legal search. So the state said: same thing. A vehicle, probable cause, done. **(VIS: the "automobile exception" rule tries to stretch from the street… up the driveway.) (SFX: a stretching, straining tone.)**
[VO:] The Supreme Court said no. **(VIS: the bright line SNAPS down across the driveway; the rule recoils. This is the payoff of the hook's motif.) (SFX: a hard, clean impact — the biggest hit in the film.)** [CLM-0007]
[VO:] In 2018, by a vote of eight to one, the Court drew the line — and it drew it at the edge of the home. **(VIS: "Collins v. Virginia · 2018 · 8–1" as a clean animated title; a Timeline dot lands.) (SFX: gavel knock.)** [CLM-0007]
[VO:] The area right around your house — the porch, the front steps, the driveway pressed against the walls — has a name in the law: the **curtilage**. And curtilage gets the full protection of the home itself. **(VIS: the house's protective outline expands outward to wrap the driveway and porch; the word "CURTILAGE" mask-reveals.) (SFX: a warm, resolving swell.)** [CLM-0007]
[VO:] Justice Sotomayor, writing for the Court, put it in one sentence you can keep: **"The scope of the automobile exception extends no further than the automobile itself."** **(VIS: the quote types on, attributed; the car-search power visibly stops at the car and cannot cross onto the ground by the house.) (SFX: type ticks; a soft period-beat.)** [CLM-0007]
[VO:] The car can be exposed. But the moment that car is parked in the protected ground hugging your home, the hundred-year exception runs out of road. To come onto that ground and search, police need a warrant — or a different exception entirely. **(VIS: the bright line holds; a warrant icon appears on the house side of the line.) (SFX: a single confident note.)** [CLM-0007]

## ACT IV — WHERE THE LINE LEAVES YOU (≈8:20–10:40)
[VO:] So put it together, because this is the map of your actual rights on the road. **(VIS: a clean summary diagram assembles — car / probable cause / curtilage — each element flying into place.) (SFX: three quick assembling ticks.)**
[VO:] On a public street or a routine stop, your car can be searched without a warrant — but only if police have probable cause, and only as far as the thing they're looking for could be. **(VIS: street scene; the "probable cause" gate; the zoned car.)** [CLM-0001][CLM-0003][CLM-0004]
[VO:] Getting arrested doesn't automatically open the car either. The Court has said that once you're secured and away from the vehicle, they generally can't search it just because they arrested you — not unless it's reasonable to think evidence of that offense is inside. **(VIS: a person handcuffed and seated away from the car; the car stays closed.) (SFX: a restrained click.)** [CLM-0008]
[VO:] And the protection can follow you into places you might not expect — even a rental car you're driving that isn't in your name can still carry privacy the Fourth Amendment respects. **(VIS: a rental agreement; a name not listed; a small shield still appears over the car.)** [CLM-0009]
[VO:] There's one live wire worth naming honestly. For years, the smell of marijuana was treated in many places as probable cause all by itself. But there's no single national rule from the Supreme Court on that — and in states that have legalized or decriminalized cannabis, some courts now say the odor alone isn't enough anymore. On that question, the honest answer is: it depends where you are. **(VIS: a US map where different states light different colors under a "smell = probable cause?" label — deliberately not uniform.) (SFX: a soft uncertain shimmer, no resolution.)** [CLM-0010]
[VO:] But the throughline is a hundred years old and it's simple. Your car trades privacy for mobility. The exception is broad — broader than most people believe. It just isn't infinite. **(VIS: the car and the house, side by side, with the bright line between them.)**
[VO:] It stops, exactly, at the edge of your home. **(VIS: slow push to the bright line at the curtilage — the film's final image resolves the hook.) (SFX: the low boom from the hook returns, resolved and quiet.)** [CLM-0007]
