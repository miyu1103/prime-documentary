## ACT I — THE HUNDRED-YEAR RULE (≈0:24–3:05)
[VO:] Start with the thing that surprises almost everyone. To search your house, the police usually need a warrant — a judge's signature, probable cause written down, a name for the place and the thing they're after. That is the Fourth Amendment at full strength.
(VIS: a warrant document; a house wrapped in a glowing protective outline.)
[VO:] Your car does not get that. And it hasn't since 1925 — before most of the roads it drives on even existed.
(VIS: timeline snaps back — "1925"; a Model-T era street.) (SFX: page turn; a soft stamp.)
[VO:] It was Prohibition. Alcohol was illegal, and a whole underground economy ran it across state lines by car. A man named George Carroll was one of those runners, moving liquor along the highway between Detroit and Grand Rapids.
(VIS: headlights on a dark 1920s highway; crates in a back seat. Anonymous figures.) (SFX: a low engine hum, ambient night road.)
[VO:] Federal agents had been watching him. They stopped his car, and they didn't find anything in plain sight — so they started tearing into it. They ripped open the seat cushions. And hidden behind the upholstery of the seats, they pulled out sixty-eight bottles of whisky and gin. [CLM-0002]
(VIS: gloved hands tearing a seat; bottles revealed one after another; a StatCounter ticks up to 68.) (SFX: fabric tear; glass clinks; the counter blips.)
[VO:] Carroll's lawyers had what sounds like a winning argument: no warrant, no search. The agents had torn his car apart on the side of the road with nothing on paper. The case climbed all the way to the Supreme Court.
(VIS: the marble courthouse; "Carroll v. United States, 1925.")
[VO:] And the Court gave an answer that has governed your glovebox ever since. It drew a line — not around what the police wanted, but around the thing being searched.
(VIS: a split screen forms: HOUSE on one side, CAR on the other.)
[VO:] A house, the Court said, stays put. If officers have to leave and get a warrant, they can post someone at the door; the house isn't going anywhere. [CLM-0001]
(VIS: a house holding still; an officer waiting at the door as a clock spins.)
[VO:] But a car is built to move. By the time a warrant is signed, a car — and whatever is hidden in its seats — can be in another county, another state, gone. Chief Justice Taft wrote that you can't treat the two the same, "because the vehicle can be quickly moved out of the locality or jurisdiction in which the warrant must be sought." [CLM-0001]
(VIS: the car accelerates off-frame; the warrant "arrives" at an empty curb, too late.) (SFX: engine pull; a whoosh past the mic.)
[VO:] So the Court carved out what we now call the **automobile exception**. If police have probable cause to believe your car contains contraband, they can search it — right there, no warrant. [CLM-0001]
(VIS: kinetic type locks in — "THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION." The house keeps its glowing shield; the car's shield cracks and thins.)
[VO:] And this isn't a dusty relic. That 1925 rule is still the rule behind why an ordinary traffic stop can, under the right facts, become an open trunk on the shoulder of the highway.
(VIS: a single stop multiplies into a grid of stops across a map at night.) (SFX: a soft multiplying tick.)
[VO:] Not everyone agreed at the time. Justice McReynolds, joined by Justice Sutherland, dissented.
(VIS: a lone dissent page; a magnifier that never gets used.)
[VO:] But the majority held — and here's the phrase to hold onto, because the entire story turns on it. **Probable cause.** Not a warrant. Not certainty. But not a hunch either. We're going to see exactly where they bite.
(VIS: "PROBABLE CAUSE" ignites and locks — an open-loop marker.) (SFX: a sub-drop.)

## ACT II — WHAT "PROBABLE CAUSE" ACTUALLY MEANS (≈3:05–6:00)
[VO:] Here's the part movies and TV get wrong. The automobile exception is not a blank check. Being pulled over is not, by itself, permission to search your car. [CLM-0003]
(VIS: a big red "NOT" stamps over a routine traffic stop.) (SFX: a hard stamp / soft impact.)
[VO:] And a hunch is not enough. Probable cause means a fair, fact-based probability that there's contraband or evidence inside — something an officer can point to and articulate, not just a feeling. A busted taillight does not, on its own, get anyone into your trunk. [CLM-0003]
(VIS: a "hunch" meter climbs but stalls below a bright line labeled PROBABLE CAUSE; a taillight and a trunk with an X between them.) (SFX: ui-ticks as the meter rises, a dull thud when it falls short.)
[VO:] So what does clear the bar? The smell of something illegal in some places. Something an officer sees in plain view on the seat. An admission. A trained dog's alert. Facts that add up.
(VIS: small evidence chips — a plain-view item, an alert, a statement — stack onto the meter until it crosses the line.) (SFX: rising data-blips.)
[VO:] And once that line is crossed — once there really is probable cause — the search can go much further than people expect. Because the Supreme Court has said the reach of the search is defined by the thing they're looking for.
(VIS: the line finally lights up; a cutaway of the car begins to glow zone by zone.)
[VO:] If the object could fit in the glovebox, they can open the glovebox. If it could be in the trunk, they can open the trunk. If it could be inside a zipped bag on the back seat, they can open the bag. Locked container, loose container — if that's a place the thing could be, it's fair game. [CLM-0004]
(VIS: an animated car cutaway — glovebox, trunk, a duffel, a shoebox — each lights and opens as it's named. A moving diagram, never a still.) (SFX: a soft mechanical click per zone.)
[VO:] That reach didn't come out of nowhere. It was settled in two Supreme Court decisions — United States v. Ross in 1982, and California v. Acevedo in 1991 — which held that a closed bag or a locked box inside the car gets no special shield of its own. If the thing they have probable cause to find could be in there, they can open it. [CLM-0004]
(VIS: two case cards flip up — "Ross · 1982" and "Acevedo · 1991" — then a padlock on a container clicks open.) (SFX: two card flips; a lock pop.)
[VO:] But that same rule cuts the other way, and it's the closest thing you have to a leash. The search has to fit the reason for it. If the probable cause is about a stolen fifty-inch television, that does not justify opening a tiny pill bottle — a TV can't hide in there. The bigger the thing they're chasing, the fewer the places they're allowed to look. The smaller it is, the more of your car opens up. [CLM-0004]
(VIS: a big-item search — only the trunk and back seat glow; then a small-item search — the whole car, down to the ashtray, lights up.) (SFX: contrasting low and high blips.)
[VO:] They don't even have to prove a separate emergency, though. The Court has been blunt about it: if the car is drivable and there's probable cause, that's enough — no extra excuse required. [CLM-0005]
(VIS: a checklist — "readily mobile ✓ / probable cause ✓" → "search: permitted." Where a third box for "emergency" should sit, there's just… nothing.) (SFX: two crisp checks, then a hollow tone where the third would be.)
[VO:] The Justices spelled that out again in the nineteen-nineties: a car that's readily mobile, plus probable cause, needs nothing more. No ticking clock. No proof the driver was about to speed off. The car being a car is the emergency. [CLM-0005]
(VIS: the clock icon fades out of the checklist entirely; "readily mobile ✓ / probable cause ✓" glows alone.) (SFX: a single low, settled tone.)
[VO:] Now step back and feel what that means for you. Your car is where you keep your bag, your phone, your paperwork, half of your private life while it's in motion. And it carries far thinner protection than your living room — for a reason written down before your grandparents were born.
(VIS: slow pull-back — a night highway, thousands of cars, each a small lit box of a private life.) (SFX: an ambient highway bed swells under the line.)
[VO:] Which raises the only question that's left. If the car is this exposed — is there anywhere the police still cannot follow it?
(VIS: the loop tightens; a single low pulse.) (SFX: one deep, patient beat.)

## ACT III — THE MOTORCYCLE UNDER THE TARP (≈6:00–8:55)
[VO:] To find the line, go to Virginia — and a motorcycle that kept getting away. [CLM-0006]
(VIS: an orange-and-black motorcycle blurs past a patrol car; a speedometer spikes.) (SFX: a fast pass-by whoosh.)
[VO:] An officer had twice been outrun by the same distinctive orange-and-black bike, and he suspected it was stolen. Then, one day, he tracked it down. Not on a street. To a house.
(VIS: a phone photo of the bike; a map pin dropping on a quiet residential address.) (SFX: an ambient suburban hush.)
[VO:] And there it was: parked in the driveway, pulled up close to the side of the home, with a tarp thrown over it. [CLM-0006]
(VIS: the quiet house at dusk; a covered shape in the driveway.) (SFX: a light wind rustling the tarp.)
[VO:] So the officer walked up the driveway. He lifted the tarp. He ran the plate and the vehicle number — and it came back stolen. Then he waited for the man staying there, Ryan Collins, and arrested him. [CLM-0006]
(VIS: footsteps up the driveway; a hand peels back the tarp; a "STOLEN — CONFIRMED" chip. Anonymous figures, no likeness.) (SFX: footsteps; the tarp pull; a data-blip on "confirmed.")
[VO:] Here's why this case is the whole ballgame. The officer had real reason to think that bike was stolen — that's probable cause. And if that motorcycle had been sitting on a public street, lifting the tarp is a clean, legal, automobile-exception search.
(VIS: replay the same act, but on a public curb — a green "LEGAL" check.) (SFX: a confirming tick.)
[VO:] So Virginia argued: same thing here. A vehicle, probable cause, done — it shouldn't matter that it was in a driveway.
(VIS: the "automobile exception" rule visibly stretches off the street and reaches up the driveway toward the house.) (SFX: a straining, elastic tone.)
[VO:] But think about how much that position was really asking for. To search a car parked at a stranger's public curb, an officer needs nothing but probable cause. Virginia wanted that same power to include walking onto private property — up the driveway, past the point where a home begins. If that were allowed, the most exposed thing you own, your car, could become the key that unlocks the most protected place you have, your home.
(VIS: a car-shaped "key" turning in a lock set into the front door of a house.) (SFX: a tense metallic turn.)
[VO:] The Supreme Court said no. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: the bright line SLAMS down across the driveway; the stretched rule snaps back. This is the hook's motif, paid off.) (SFX: the single hardest impact in the film, then quiet.)
[VO:] In 2018, by a vote of eight to one, the Court held that the car-search power does not reach onto the ground that belongs to your home. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: a clean animated title — "Collins v. Virginia · 2018 · 8–1"; a Timeline dot lands beside 1925.) (SFX: a gavel knock.)
[VO:] There's a word for that ground. The porch, the front steps, the strip of driveway pressed against the walls — the law calls it the **curtilage**. And curtilage is treated as part of the home. It gets the home's full protection. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: the house's glowing outline expands outward to wrap the driveway and porch; the word "CURTILAGE" mask-reveals into place.) (SFX: a warm, resolving swell.)
[VO:] And that isn't a technicality. The home is the ground the Fourth Amendment guards most fiercely. The Court's point was simple: you cannot let a rule built for the open road quietly swallow the most protected ground a person owns. Where you park does not shrink your home. Your home enlarges what it protects. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: the home's shield pulses outward once, firmly, over the driveway.) (SFX: a single grounded low note.)
[VO:] Justice Sotomayor, writing for the Court, said it in one line worth keeping: "The scope of the automobile exception extends no further than the automobile itself." [CLM-0007]
(VIS: the quote types on, attributed; the car-search glow tries to cross onto the home's ground and stops dead at the line.) (SFX: type ticks; a soft period-beat.)
[VO:] So both things are true at once. Out on the street, that same search is fine. But the instant the car is tucked into the protected ground hugging your house, the hundred-year exception runs out of road. To step onto that ground and search, police need a warrant — or a completely different exception. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: the bright line holds firm; a warrant icon appears on the house side.) (SFX: a single confident note.)
[VO:] Only Justice Alito dissented — worried the Court was letting a technicality shield a bike he had strong reason to believe was stolen. But he stood alone. The line held. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: a lone dissent card; the bright line, unmoved.)
[VO:] So now you know where the line falls. The question that actually matters is what it means the next time it's your window the officer is walking up to.
(VIS: the bright-line motif holds, then pulls back to a driver's-side window at night — waiting.) (SFX: a low, forward-leaning pulse.)

## ACT IV — WHERE THE LINE LEAVES YOU (≈8:55–10:45)
[VO:] So here's the actual map of your rights on the road — the one almost nobody is handed.
(VIS: a clean summary diagram assembles piece by piece — car / probable cause / curtilage.) (SFX: three quick assembling ticks.)
[VO:] On a public street or a routine stop, your car can be searched without a warrant — but only with probable cause, and only as far as the thing they're looking for could actually be. [CLM-0001][CLM-0003][CLM-0004]
(VIS: street scene → the probable-cause gate → the zoned car.)
[VO:] Being arrested doesn't automatically open the car either. In a case called Gant, the Court said that once you're handcuffed and set away from the vehicle, officers generally can't search it just because they arrested you — not unless it's reasonable to think evidence of that specific offense is inside. [CLM-0008]
(VIS: a person cuffed and seated on a curb, well away from the car; the car stays shut.) (SFX: a restrained click.)
[VO:] The protection can even follow you into a rental. The Court has said that if you're lawfully driving a rental car, you can keep a real expectation of privacy in it — even if your name isn't on the paperwork. [CLM-0009]
(VIS: a rental agreement; a name conspicuously not listed; a small shield still settles over the car.)
[VO:] And there's one honest gray area worth naming. For years, in many places, the smell of marijuana alone was treated as probable cause. But the Supreme Court has never set a single nationwide rule on that — and in states that have legalized or decriminalized it, several courts now say the odor by itself isn't enough anymore. On that one, the honest answer is: it depends where you are. [CLM-0010]
(VIS: a US map where states light different colors under a "smell = probable cause?" label — deliberately not uniform.) (SFX: an uncertain shimmer that never resolves.)
[VO:] So what does all of this mean the next time you're actually pulled over? You don't have to be a lawyer. Just carry the shape of it: they need a real, articulable reason — not a mood — before they can search; the search has to match the thing they're after; and the ground pressed against your house is not the road.
(VIS: a calm driver at a window; the bright-line motif faint but present in the frame.) (SFX: a steady, reassuring pulse.)
[VO:] But the throughline is a century old, and it's simple. Your car trades privacy for mobility. The exception is broad — broader than most people believe — and it isn't a myth. It's just not infinite.
(VIS: the car and the house, side by side, the bright line between them.)
[VO:] It stops, exactly, at the edge of your home. [CLM-0007]
(VIS: a slow push into the bright line at the curtilage — the film's final image, resolving the hook.) (SFX: the low boom from the hook returns, quiet and settled.)
